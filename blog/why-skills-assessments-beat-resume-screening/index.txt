3:T1241,<p>Résumés tell you where someone worked and what they claim they did. They don&#39;t tell you whether the person can actually do the job. That gap is where most hiring goes wrong — teams spend hours interviewing candidates who read well on paper and can&#39;t reason through a real problem, while overlooking strong people whose backgrounds don&#39;t pattern-match to a recruiter&#39;s mental model. A well-designed skills assessment closes that gap. Here&#39;s the case for using one, and how to do it without becoming the company candidates warn each other about.</p>
<h2>The résumé is a weak predictor</h2>
<p>Keyword-matching a résumé optimizes for the wrong things:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>It rewards pedigree over ability.</strong> Brand-name employers and elite schools correlate loosely, at best, with day-to-day performance.</li>
<li><strong>It rewards self-promotion.</strong> The people who write the best résumés aren&#39;t reliably the ones who do the best work.</li>
<li><strong>It&#39;s easy to inflate.</strong> &quot;Led,&quot; &quot;architected,&quot; &quot;drove&quot; — claims are cheap and unverifiable on paper.</li>
<li><strong>It filters out non-traditional talent.</strong> Career switchers, self-taught engineers, and people from underrepresented backgrounds get screened out by proxies that have nothing to do with skill.</li>
</ul>
<p>A structured assessment measures the thing you actually care about: can this person reason through the work?</p>
<h2>Assessments reduce bias</h2>
<p>When you evaluate what a candidate <em>did</em> on a standardized exercise instead of where they went to school, you strip out a lot of the noise that drives biased decisions. Everyone answers comparable questions, scored the same way, against the same rubric. That&#39;s fairer to the candidate and more defensible for you — and it&#39;s how you find the great engineer whose résumé a keyword filter would have discarded.</p>
<h2>They save your team&#39;s most expensive resource</h2>
<p>Interviewer time is the scarcest thing in hiring. Sending a focused assessment early means your engineers spend their interview hours on candidates who&#39;ve already demonstrated they can do the work — not re-screening a stack of maybes. A good assessment turns a pile of applications into a ranked shortlist, so the humans focus on judgment calls, culture, and selling the role.</p>
<h2>The catch: bad assessments do real harm</h2>
<p>Testing is only a win if the test is good and the experience is humane. Done badly, assessments backfire:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Trivia and gotchas</strong> measure memorization, not ability, and annoy exactly the senior people you want.</li>
<li><strong>Hostile, proctored exams</strong> with webcams and lockdown browsers insult strong candidates and drive them to competitors who respect them.</li>
<li><strong>Marathon take-homes</strong> that extract free work screen <em>out</em> people with options.</li>
<li><strong>Opaque scoring</strong> that candidates can&#39;t trust erodes your brand.</li>
</ul>
<p>The goal isn&#39;t to make candidates suffer to prove they&#39;re worthy. It&#39;s to get a fair, accurate read on ability while making people <em>more</em> excited to join, not less.</p>
<h2>What good looks like</h2>
<p>A skills assessment earns its place when it is:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Role-relevant</strong> — it tests the judgment the job actually requires, not API trivia.</li>
<li><strong>Short and respectful</strong> of the candidate&#39;s time.</li>
<li><strong>Fair</strong> — comparable questions, consistent scoring, no negative marking.</li>
<li><strong>Calm</strong> — a focused, pleasant experience, not a surveillance exam.</li>
<li><strong>Transparent</strong> — clear about what&#39;s measured and what happens with the results.</li>
</ul>
<p>That combination gives you strong hiring signal <em>and</em> a candidate experience that wins offers. Those two goals aren&#39;t in tension — the best assessments serve both at once.</p>
<h2>The bottom line</h2>
<p>Résumé screening asks &quot;does this person&#39;s history look like the ones we&#39;ve hired before?&quot; A skills assessment asks &quot;can this person do the work?&quot; The second question is the one that actually predicts a good hire — and it&#39;s the one that gives overlooked candidates a fair shot. The rest of the decision is what interviews are for: the assessment just makes sure you&#39;re spending those interviews on the right people. In the articles that follow, we explain exactly how we&#39;ve built assessments to get that signal <em>without</em> the hostility that gives testing a bad name.</p>
