30 Interview Questions to Ask Developers (and What Good Answers Sound Like)

A complete guide to interviewing software developers with 30 structured interview questions, scoring criteria, resume review tips, technical evaluation guidance, and examples of what strong answers sound like.

The JobsList.dev Team·2026年7月13日·9 blog.article.minuteRead
interviewinghiringrecruitingsoftware-engineeringtechnical-interviewshiring-managers

Hiring great software engineers is difficult for one simple reason: most interviews don't measure how someone will perform on the job.

Instead, companies often compare candidates based on unrelated trivia, inconsistent questioning, or vague "culture fit." One interviewer asks about React hooks. Another asks system design. Someone else spends forty minutes discussing hobbies. At the hiring meeting everyone compares feelings instead of evidence.

The strongest hiring processes look different.

Every candidate is evaluated against the same criteria.

Every interviewer knows exactly what they're responsible for assessing.

Every answer is scored before anyone discusses opinions.

The result is a process that's fairer for candidates, produces better hiring decisions, and dramatically reduces bias.

This guide contains thirty practical interview questions that reveal how developers think, solve problems, collaborate, and learn. It also explains what strong answers sound like, what warning signs to watch for, and how to score responses consistently.

Before You Start the Interview

A great interview starts before the candidate joins the call.

Review their résumé.

Review their GitHub if they shared one.

Open any portfolio projects.

Read the job description again.

Know exactly what level you're hiring:

  • Junior
  • Mid-level
  • Senior
  • Staff
  • Principal

Don't ask senior-level questions to junior candidates.

Don't reject juniors because they don't have production experience.

Likewise, don't hire senior engineers based purely on enthusiasm.

Evaluate people at the level you're hiring.

A Simple Interview Scorecard

Create a scorecard before the interview starts.

Rate every category from 1–5.

Category Score
Technical fundamentals
Problem solving
Communication
Ownership
System design
Collaboration
Learning ability
Overall recommendation

Complete it immediately after the interview.

Do not compare notes with other interviewers until everyone has independently scored the candidate.

This dramatically improves hiring consistency.

Section 1 — Resume Validation

The first few questions aren't technical.

They're verifying whether the résumé accurately reflects reality.

1. Tell me about something you've built that you're proud of.

Strong answers include:

  • the business problem
  • their role
  • technical decisions
  • trade-offs
  • lessons learned
  • measurable outcomes

Red flags

  • only listing technologies
  • speaking only in generalities
  • unable to explain architecture

2. Walk me through your most recent project.

You're listening for ownership.

Good candidates naturally explain:

  • why it existed
  • what they owned
  • who else worked on it
  • biggest challenges
  • deployment process

3. What was your specific contribution?

Pay attention to pronouns.

Strong candidates comfortably switch between:

"The team built..."

and

"My responsibility was..."

Candidates who cannot explain their own contribution may have been passengers rather than contributors.

4. Why are you looking for a new opportunity?

There isn't one right answer.

Good answers are future focused.

Examples:

  • looking for more ownership
  • larger technical challenges
  • better engineering culture
  • remote work
  • career progression

Red flags:

  • spending several minutes criticizing previous employers
  • blaming everyone else
  • having no clear goals

5. What interested you about this position?

Good candidates have researched:

  • the product
  • engineering blog
  • technology
  • customers
  • mission

They don't need to know everything.

But they should know why they applied.

Section 2 — Technical Depth

Framework knowledge comes and goes.

Understanding lasts.

6. Teach me something you know well.

Instead of trivia, let candidates choose.

Ask:

"Pick something from your résumé and teach it to me."

This immediately reveals:

  • communication
  • confidence
  • technical depth

Great engineers explain complex topics simply.

7. Tell me about the hardest bug you've ever fixed.

Listen for process.

Strong answers usually include:

  • reproducing the issue
  • gathering evidence
  • logging
  • debugging
  • narrowing hypotheses
  • verifying fixes

Weak answers sound like guessing.

8. What's one technical decision you would change today?

This measures reflection.

Experienced engineers change their minds.

Listen for:

  • humility
  • reasoning
  • learning

9. How do you decide what deserves testing?

Good answers discuss:

  • business risk
  • edge cases
  • critical paths
  • regression prevention

Weak answers focus only on code coverage percentages.

10. Tell me about a mistake you made.

Strong candidates:

  • admit it quickly
  • explain the impact
  • explain the fix
  • explain prevention

Blame shifting is one of the strongest warning signs.

11. What's something you've changed your mind about?

Examples:

  • microservices
  • testing philosophy
  • architecture
  • cloud
  • state management
  • agile processes

People who never change their minds rarely improve.

Section 3 — System Design

Ask about real problems.

Avoid abstract whiteboard exercises whenever possible.

12. Design a feature from our product.

Let them ask questions first.

The questions often matter more than the final solution.

Strong candidates ask about:

  • users
  • scale
  • constraints
  • latency
  • security
  • failure handling

Weak candidates begin drawing immediately.

13. How would you solve this real engineering problem?

Use an issue your own team recently faced.

Real problems produce realistic conversations.

14. An endpoint suddenly became ten times slower.

How would you investigate?

Listen for:

  • metrics
  • logs
  • tracing
  • profiling
  • recent deployments
  • database analysis
  • rollback strategy

15. Describe how you'd migrate a database without downtime.

Look for discussion of:

  • backwards compatibility
  • feature flags
  • rolling deployments
  • dual writes
  • backfills
  • monitoring
  • rollback

16. If traffic increased tenfold tomorrow, what breaks first?

You're testing prioritization.

Nobody expects perfect answers.

You're evaluating reasoning.

Section 4 — Collaboration

Engineering is a team sport.

Poor communication can destroy an otherwise excellent engineer.

17. Tell me about a disagreement with another engineer.

Strong candidates discuss:

  • listening
  • evidence
  • experiments
  • compromise

Weak candidates discuss winning.

18. What makes a great code review?

Look for:

  • constructive feedback
  • respect
  • education
  • maintainability

Not:

"LGTM"

19. Explain a technical concept to a non-technical executive.

Excellent engineers simplify.

They don't show off.

20. Tell me about pushing back on a deadline.

Good answers include:

  • evidence
  • alternatives
  • communication
  • negotiation

Not simply refusing work.

21. How do you learn a new codebase?

Strong engineers often describe:

  • running locally
  • tracing requests
  • reading tests
  • reading documentation
  • fixing a small bug
  • talking to teammates

Section 5 — Ownership

The best engineers own problems.

Not tickets.

22. Tell me about a production incident.

Listen for:

  • ownership
  • calm communication
  • diagnosis
  • prevention

Not hero stories.

23. When have you knowingly shipped something imperfect?

Every engineer has.

Great answers discuss:

  • deadlines
  • business value
  • acceptable risk
  • follow-up plans

24. What do you do when you're blocked?

Healthy pattern:

  • investigate
  • research
  • ask questions
  • escalate appropriately

Unhealthy pattern:

  • silently struggle for days

25. What's something you improved without being asked?

Initiative is difficult to teach.

Strong engineers naturally notice friction.

26. How do you know software is finished?

Good answers include:

  • tests
  • documentation
  • monitoring
  • deployment
  • customer validation

Not simply:

"The feature works."

Section 6 — Long-Term Fit

These questions work best near the end.

27. What type of work energizes you?

You're checking alignment.

Someone who loves infrastructure may struggle in a purely frontend role.

28. What kind of environment brings out your worst work?

Great answers demonstrate self-awareness.

Examples:

  • unclear priorities
  • constant interruptions
  • no feedback
  • chaotic planning

29. What would your last manager say you should improve?

Strong candidates usually answer immediately.

Growth-minded engineers know their development areas.

30. Is there anything about this role that concerns you?

This question saves offers.

It surfaces:

  • relocation concerns
  • remote expectations
  • compensation
  • technology concerns
  • growth opportunities

Many objections can be resolved immediately.

Questions Candidates Should Ask You

Remember:

Great candidates are interviewing you too.

Expect questions like:

  • What does success look like after six months?
  • How does engineering influence product decisions?
  • How often do deployments happen?
  • How are incidents handled?
  • What is the onboarding process?
  • How are promotions decided?
  • How is technical debt prioritized?
  • What does your development workflow look like?
  • What are the biggest engineering challenges today?
  • Why is this role open?

Candidates who ask thoughtful questions are usually thinking long-term.

Resume Review Tips Before the Interview

Before scheduling interviews, look for evidence instead of keywords.

Strong résumés typically include:

  • measurable accomplishments
  • production systems
  • links to GitHub or portfolios
  • open-source contributions
  • technical writing
  • leadership
  • mentoring
  • architecture decisions
  • promotions
  • ownership

Be cautious of résumés that contain long technology lists with very little explanation of outcomes.

A concise résumé showing meaningful impact often tells you more than several pages filled with buzzwords.

Interview Mistakes to Avoid

Avoid these common interviewing mistakes:

  • Asking different questions to every candidate.
  • Judging confidence instead of competence.
  • Interrupting candidates before they finish explaining.
  • Turning interviews into trivia contests.
  • Spending the entire interview talking.
  • Ignoring communication skills.
  • Letting one impressive answer outweigh several weak ones.
  • Failing to document interview notes immediately.
  • Discussing candidates before everyone has independently scored them.
  • Rejecting candidates for not knowing one specific framework.

Consistency almost always beats clever interviewing.

Building a Great Interview Process

A strong technical hiring process usually follows this structure:

  1. Recruiter or Hiring Manager Call (20–30 minutes) — Motivation, communication, salary expectations, availability.
  2. Technical Experience Interview (60 minutes) — Past projects, debugging, architecture, ownership.
  3. Coding or Practical Exercise (60–90 minutes) — Pair programming, realistic feature, or a well-scoped take-home assignment.
  4. System Design (Senior+ Roles) — Real-world engineering problems and trade-offs.
  5. Team Interview — Collaboration, communication, and mutual fit.
  6. Hiring Decision — Independent scoring followed by structured discussion.

Each stage should have a clear purpose. Avoid asking the same questions repeatedly across multiple rounds.

The Takeaway

Great developer interviews aren't about asking the hardest questions—they're about asking the right questions consistently.

Evaluate technical ability, communication, ownership, collaboration, and judgment with the same structured process for every candidate. Encourage candidates to explain their reasoning, discuss real experiences, and demonstrate how they solve problems rather than how many facts they can memorize.

When interviews focus on real engineering work instead of trivia, companies make better hiring decisions, candidates have a more respectful experience, and teams grow stronger over time.

Whether you're hiring your first software engineer or scaling an engineering organization, a structured interview process will outperform gut instinct every time.
