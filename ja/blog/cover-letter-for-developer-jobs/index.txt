e:T8d7e,<p>Developers tend to have strong opinions about cover letters.</p>
<p>Some hiring managers read every one. Others barely glance at them. Many large companies treat them as optional attachments, while a founder at a ten-person startup may read the letter before opening the résumé.</p>
<p>That makes the real answer more nuanced than either “always write one” or “cover letters are dead.”</p>
<p>A generic cover letter usually adds no value. It may even hurt your application if it sounds copied, vague, overly formal, or clearly written for another company.</p>
<p>A short, specific cover letter can still be useful when it answers something your résumé cannot explain on its own.</p>
<p>The best cover letters do not repeat your work history. They help a hiring manager understand:</p>
<ol>
<li>Why you chose this company.</li>
<li>Why your experience is relevant.</li>
<li>Why your career story makes sense.</li>
<li>What evidence they should examine next.</li>
</ol>
<p>For most developer applications, the ideal cover letter is not a full page.</p>
<p>It is often closer to <strong>150–250 focused words</strong>.</p>
<h1>Do developers really need a cover letter?</h1>
<p>Not for every application.</p>
<p>A cover letter is most useful when it adds context, relevance, or evidence that the résumé alone does not provide.</p>
<p>It is less useful when it merely restates your job titles, technologies, and responsibilities in paragraph form.</p>
<p>A practical rule is:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Write a cover letter when you have something useful and specific to say. Skip it when you would only be sending a template.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The absence of a cover letter is rarely fatal when the application says it is optional.</p>
<p>A weak cover letter, however, can create doubt.</p>
<h1>What a cover letter can do that a résumé cannot</h1>
<p>A résumé is optimized for scanning.</p>
<p>It communicates:</p>
<ul>
<li>job titles;</li>
<li>dates;</li>
<li>technologies;</li>
<li>responsibilities;</li>
<li>accomplishments;</li>
<li>education;</li>
<li>certifications;</li>
<li>projects.</li>
</ul>
<p>A cover letter can explain the relationship between those facts.</p>
<p>For example, it can clarify:</p>
<ul>
<li>why a backend developer is moving into platform engineering;</li>
<li>why a teacher is applying for an education-technology role;</li>
<li>why a developer with mostly Java experience is applying to a Kotlin team;</li>
<li>what happened during a career break;</li>
<li>why a candidate is interested in one specific product;</li>
<li>how domain experience from a previous career strengthens the application;</li>
<li>why an apparently lateral move is intentional;</li>
<li>why a candidate is relocating or targeting remote work.</li>
</ul>
<p>Think of the résumé as evidence and the cover letter as interpretation.</p>
<h1>When a cover letter is worth writing</h1>
<h2>The employer asks for one</h2>
<p>This is the simplest case.</p>
<p>When the application explicitly requests a cover letter, provide one.</p>
<p>Ignoring the instruction may suggest that you:</p>
<ul>
<li>did not read the listing;</li>
<li>did not care enough to complete the application;</li>
<li>struggle to follow written requirements.</li>
</ul>
<p>It may also be a deliberate filter.</p>
<p>You do not need to write an essay. You do need to respond thoughtfully.</p>
<h2>You are changing careers</h2>
<p>A career changer often has a résumé that makes perfect sense after explanation but looks unusual without it.</p>
<p>For example:</p>
<ul>
<li>logistics manager to backend developer;</li>
<li>graphic designer to frontend engineer;</li>
<li>accountant to data analyst;</li>
<li>technical support specialist to DevOps engineer;</li>
<li>teacher to software developer.</li>
</ul>
<p>A cover letter gives you space to connect the old career to the new one.</p>
<p>Instead of hiding your previous experience, explain the advantage it creates.</p>
<p>Example:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>My background in logistics operations gives me firsthand knowledge of inventory exceptions, shipment delays, and warehouse workflows. I moved into software development after building internal automation tools for those processes, and I am now looking for a backend role where that domain knowledge is directly useful.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>That is more compelling than apologizing for not having a traditional engineering résumé.</p>
<h2>You are changing specialties</h2>
<p>Your résumé may show one path while the role represents another.</p>
<p>Examples include:</p>
<ul>
<li>frontend to full-stack;</li>
<li>backend to platform engineering;</li>
<li>QA automation to software development;</li>
<li>developer to engineering manager;</li>
<li>individual contributor returning from management;</li>
<li>mobile engineer moving from Android to cross-platform work;</li>
<li>data analyst moving into data engineering.</li>
</ul>
<p>Use the letter to show that the transition is intentional and supported by evidence.</p>
<p>Example:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Although my recent titles have been in backend product engineering, much of my most impactful work has involved deployment automation, observability, and developer tooling. I recently led the rollout of reusable CI pipelines across twelve services, which is why I am now pursuing a dedicated platform-engineering role.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2>You have a résumé gap</h2>
<p>Most gaps do not require a long explanation.</p>
<p>A cover letter can address one briefly and calmly.</p>
<p>Example:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>I took a ten-month career break for family caregiving and am now ready to return to full-time engineering. During that period, I kept my skills current through open-source contributions and a TypeScript project focused on accessibility testing.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>That is enough.</p>
<p>You do not need to provide private medical or family details.</p>
<h2>You are returning after a layoff</h2>
<p>A layoff is not a performance verdict.</p>
<p>State it factually and redirect attention to your work.</p>
<p>Example:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>My role was eliminated during a company-wide restructuring earlier this year. Since then, I have been contributing to an open-source data-validation project while looking for a backend role focused on reliability and data correctness.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Do not spend several paragraphs defending the situation.</p>
<h2>You have used the company&#39;s product</h2>
<p>This is one of the strongest reasons to write.</p>
<p>Product familiarity lets you open with something concrete.</p>
<p>Example:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>I have used your deployment platform for the past eighteen months, and the preview-environment workflow removed a recurring bottleneck for our frontend team. When I saw that this role would work on developer experience and release tooling, the connection was immediate.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>That sounds real because it is specific.</p>
<h2>You understand the company&#39;s technical problem</h2>
<p>You may have read:</p>
<ul>
<li>an engineering article;</li>
<li>a public architecture post;</li>
<li>a technical talk;</li>
<li>a product update;</li>
<li>an open-source repository;</li>
<li>the job description itself.</li>
</ul>
<p>You can connect your experience to that problem.</p>
<p>Example:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Your engineering post about moving account processing from nightly batches toward event-driven workflows caught my attention because I led a similar migration last year. The hardest part was preserving compatibility while both systems ran in parallel, and I would be excited to apply those lessons here.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2>You are applying to a small company or startup</h2>
<p>At smaller companies, a founder, engineering manager, or lead developer may personally review applications.</p>
<p>A thoughtful letter can matter because it demonstrates:</p>
<ul>
<li>genuine interest;</li>
<li>product understanding;</li>
<li>initiative;</li>
<li>communication;</li>
<li>willingness to work in an ambiguous environment.</li>
</ul>
<p>Small teams often care less about perfect credentials and more about whether you understand what they are building.</p>
<h2>You are applying through a direct introduction</h2>
<p>Even when you have a referral, a short note can help the recipient understand why the introduction makes sense.</p>
<p>It may be only a few sentences rather than a formal letter.</p>
<p>Example:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Jordan suggested I reach out because your team is hiring for the billing platform. I have spent the past three years working on subscription, retry, and reconciliation systems, including a processor migration completed without customer-facing downtime. I would be glad to share more about the project if the experience looks relevant.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2>Your résumé needs prioritization</h2>
<p>A résumé may contain several relevant projects, but the reader may not know which one matters most.</p>
<p>The letter can direct attention.</p>
<p>Example:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>The project most relevant to this role is the authorization redesign described under my current position. I led the move from account-level permissions to organization-scoped access, including the schema migration, compatibility layer, and integration tests.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>This helps the reviewer find the strongest evidence quickly.</p>
<h1>When a cover letter probably will not help</h1>
<h2>The application is extremely high-volume and automated</h2>
<p>Some large companies process thousands of applications through structured systems.</p>
<p>When the cover letter is optional and there is no meaningful context to add, your time may be better spent on:</p>
<ul>
<li>tailoring the résumé;</li>
<li>improving the opening summary;</li>
<li>aligning your skills section;</li>
<li>updating your portfolio;</li>
<li>getting a referral;</li>
<li>applying earlier.</li>
</ul>
<p>You can still include a short note, but do not spend an hour writing a page that is unlikely to be read.</p>
<h2>A recruiter is already representing you</h2>
<p>When a recruiter has already spoken with you and is submitting your résumé directly, they may provide the context a cover letter would normally supply.</p>
<p>You can ask whether a note would be useful.</p>
<p>Do not create extra documents merely because they feel traditional.</p>
<h2>You have a strong internal referral</h2>
<p>A referral often answers the first question a cover letter tries to answer:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Why should someone pay attention to this candidate?</p>
</blockquote>
<p>You may still send a concise introduction, but a formal letter may be unnecessary.</p>
<h2>The role is an exact match</h2>
<p>When your current title, stack, scope, and accomplishments align clearly with the listing, there may be little additional context to explain.</p>
<p>For example, a senior Android engineer applying for a senior Android role in the same domain may gain more from a stronger résumé than a long letter.</p>
<h2>You have nothing specific to say</h2>
<p>Do not send a letter built from phrases such as:</p>
<ul>
<li>I am writing to apply.</li>
<li>I am passionate about technology.</li>
<li>I am a hard-working team player.</li>
<li>I believe I would be a strong fit.</li>
<li>Your innovative company excites me.</li>
<li>I am eager to take on new challenges.</li>
</ul>
<p>These statements could be sent to any company by any applicant.</p>
<p>A blank optional field may be better.</p>
<h1>What hiring managers want from a developer cover letter</h1>
<p>A useful letter answers a small number of questions quickly.</p>
<h1>Why this company?</h1>
<p>Not why you need a job.</p>
<p>Not why software development is exciting.</p>
<p>Why this organization?</p>
<p>Possible reasons include:</p>
<ul>
<li>you use the product;</li>
<li>you understand the customer;</li>
<li>the domain matches your experience;</li>
<li>the technical challenge matches your background;</li>
<li>the company stage suits how you work;</li>
<li>the team&#39;s engineering philosophy appeals to you;</li>
<li>the role&#39;s ownership is aligned with your goals.</li>
</ul>
<p>Weak:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>I am excited to work for an innovative technology company.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Stronger:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Your focus on reducing manual reconciliation for small finance teams stood out because I spent two years building similar workflows for subscription billing.</p>
</blockquote>
<h1>Why this role?</h1>
<p>Explain why the responsibilities fit your experience or direction.</p>
<p>Weak:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>I am looking for a challenging software engineering position.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Stronger:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>I am looking for a backend role where data integrity and production ownership are central responsibilities, which is why the reconciliation and migration work described in this position caught my attention.</p>
</blockquote>
<h1>What evidence shows you can contribute?</h1>
<p>Choose one strong example.</p>
<p>Include:</p>
<ul>
<li>the problem;</li>
<li>your contribution;</li>
<li>the result;</li>
<li>the relevance.</li>
</ul>
<p>Example:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>At Acme, I redesigned a retry workflow that had been creating duplicate payment attempts. I introduced idempotency controls, database constraints, and reconciliation alerts, reducing duplicate charges to zero during the following two quarters.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>One concrete result is more persuasive than five adjectives.</p>
<h1>What does your résumé not explain?</h1>
<p>Use one or two sentences to address:</p>
<ul>
<li>a transition;</li>
<li>a gap;</li>
<li>an unusual title;</li>
<li>an industry change;</li>
<li>a stack change;</li>
<li>a location issue;</li>
<li>a level change.</li>
</ul>
<p>Do not turn the letter into a defense brief.</p>
<h1>What should the reader do next?</h1>
<p>End with a simple invitation.</p>
<p>Examples:</p>
<ul>
<li>I would be glad to discuss the migration in more detail.</li>
<li>My portfolio includes a live version of the project.</li>
<li>The relevant repository and architecture notes are linked below.</li>
<li>I would welcome the opportunity to learn more about the team.</li>
</ul>
<p>Do not overstate confidence or demand a response.</p>
<h1>The ideal length</h1>
<p>For most developer applications, aim for <strong>150–250 words</strong>.</p>
<p>That is usually:</p>
<ul>
<li>three or four short paragraphs;</li>
<li>one screen on a laptop;</li>
<li>less than a minute to read;</li>
<li>enough room for one strong example.</li>
</ul>
<p>A cover letter is not stronger because it is longer.</p>
<p>Long letters create several risks:</p>
<ul>
<li>the strongest evidence gets buried;</li>
<li>the reader assumes you cannot prioritize;</li>
<li>generic language fills the extra space;</li>
<li>important details are skimmed or missed.</li>
</ul>
<p>A senior or specialized candidate may occasionally need more space, but a full page should remain the upper limit.</p>
<h1>A structure that works</h1>
<p>Use this four-part structure.</p>
<h2>Paragraph one: Why this company and role</h2>
<p>Open with something specific.</p>
<p>You may mention:</p>
<ul>
<li>the product;</li>
<li>the customer;</li>
<li>a technical challenge;</li>
<li>a public engineering decision;</li>
<li>a personal connection to the domain.</li>
</ul>
<p>Example:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>I have used Atlas for the past year to manage releases across three customer applications, and the preview-environment workflow has saved our team hours of manual coordination. I was especially interested to see that this role focuses on deployment reliability and developer tooling.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2>Paragraph two: Your strongest evidence</h2>
<p>Choose one relevant project or accomplishment.</p>
<p>Example:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>In my current role, I led a redesign of our CI pipeline across twelve services. We replaced duplicated workflows with reusable actions, introduced progressive deployment checks, and reduced average deployment preparation time from forty minutes to under ten.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2>Paragraph three: Context the résumé does not provide</h2>
<p>Use this only when needed.</p>
<p>Example:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>My recent title is Backend Engineer, but platform work has become the main focus of my role. The CI project, service templates, and observability tooling I now own are the reason I am intentionally moving toward platform engineering.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Delete this paragraph when there is nothing to explain.</p>
<h2>Paragraph four: Close</h2>
<p>Example:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>I would be glad to walk through the migration and the trade-offs we made. My résumé and portfolio include additional details, and I appreciate your consideration.</p>
</blockquote>
<h1>A complete short template</h1>
<p>Dear [Hiring Manager&#39;s Name],</p>
<p>I was interested in the [Role Title] position because [specific product, technical challenge, customer problem, or company detail]. [One sentence showing why this matters to you.]</p>
<p>In my current or most relevant role, I [describe one relevant responsibility or project]. I [explain your personal contribution], which resulted in [specific technical, user, team, or business outcome].</p>
<p>[Optional context: My background is primarily in X, but I am moving toward Y because Z. Evidence of that transition includes A.]</p>
<p>I would be glad to discuss [project or relevant experience] in more detail. You can also view [portfolio, repository, application, or writing] at [link].</p>
<p>Best regards,
[Your Name]</p>
<h1>A stronger developer cover letter example</h1>

<h1>How to tailor a cover letter efficiently</h1>
<p>A strong letter should be tailored, but that does not mean starting from nothing every time.</p>
<p>Keep a reusable foundation containing:</p>
<ul>
<li>your professional identity;</li>
<li>one or two strongest accomplishments;</li>
<li>your transition explanation, if applicable;</li>
<li>links to your work;</li>
<li>a concise closing.</li>
</ul>
<p>Then customize:</p>
<ol>
<li>The opening.</li>
<li>The selected accomplishment.</li>
<li>The connection to the role.</li>
<li>Any résumé context.</li>
<li>The company and hiring-manager names.</li>
</ol>
<p>The opening and the evidence should change when the role changes.</p>
<h1>A ten-minute tailoring process</h1>
<h2>Minute 1–2: Read the job description again</h2>
<p>Identify:</p>
<ul>
<li>the main problem;</li>
<li>repeated technologies;</li>
<li>expected level;</li>
<li>primary users;</li>
<li>first-year responsibilities;</li>
<li>company stage.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Minute 3–4: Research the company</h2>
<p>Look at:</p>
<ul>
<li>product pages;</li>
<li>engineering blog;</li>
<li>open-source work;</li>
<li>public product documentation;</li>
<li>company mission;</li>
<li>recent releases.</li>
</ul>
<p>You do not need a complete company history.</p>
<p>Find one real point of connection.</p>
<h2>Minute 5–6: Select one accomplishment</h2>
<p>Choose the example that best matches the role.</p>
<p>Do not choose your most impressive project if another is more relevant.</p>
<h2>Minute 7–8: Write the opening and connection</h2>
<p>Explain why the role makes sense.</p>
<h2>Minute 9: Cut generic language</h2>
<p>Delete anything that could appear in another applicant&#39;s letter.</p>
<h2>Minute 10: Proofread</h2>
<p>Check:</p>
<ul>
<li>company name;</li>
<li>job title;</li>
<li>hiring-manager name;</li>
<li>links;</li>
<li>spelling;</li>
<li>dates;</li>
<li>copied text from another application.</li>
</ul>
<h1>How to use AI without sounding generated</h1>
<p>AI tools can help improve a cover letter.</p>
<p>They are less useful when asked to create the entire letter from a résumé and job description without meaningful input from you.</p>
<p>The result often contains:</p>
<ul>
<li>exaggerated enthusiasm;</li>
<li>generic praise;</li>
<li>repeated résumé bullets;</li>
<li>unnatural formality;</li>
<li>unsupported claims;</li>
<li>phrases used by thousands of applicants.</li>
</ul>
<p>A better workflow is:</p>
<ol>
<li>Write the specific facts yourself.</li>
<li>Include the company detail that interested you.</li>
<li>Choose the accomplishment.</li>
<li>Explain the connection.</li>
<li>Use AI to shorten, clarify, or proofread.</li>
</ol>
<p>Useful prompts include:</p>
<ul>
<li>“Cut this to 180 words without removing the metrics.”</li>
<li>“Make this sound more natural and less formal.”</li>
<li>“Remove generic claims and preserve only evidence.”</li>
<li>“Check for repetition with my résumé.”</li>
<li>“Suggest three clearer opening sentences based on these facts.”</li>
</ul>
<p>Do not let the tool invent:</p>
<ul>
<li>product experience;</li>
<li>metrics;</li>
<li>responsibilities;</li>
<li>personal connections;</li>
<li>technical expertise.</li>
</ul>
<p>You remain responsible for every claim.</p>
<h1>What to include</h1>
<p>A strong developer cover letter may include:</p>
<ul>
<li>one specific company detail;</li>
<li>one relevant accomplishment;</li>
<li>your individual contribution;</li>
<li>a technical or business result;</li>
<li>a concise transition explanation;</li>
<li>one useful link;</li>
<li>a direct, professional close.</li>
</ul>
<h1>What to leave out</h1>
<p>Usually remove:</p>
<ul>
<li>your full work history;</li>
<li>every technology you know;</li>
<li>childhood interest in computers;</li>
<li>generic personality adjectives;</li>
<li>salary expectations unless requested;</li>
<li>negative comments about employers;</li>
<li>personal medical information;</li>
<li>detailed family circumstances;</li>
<li>a long explanation of why you need work;</li>
<li>inflated praise for the company;</li>
<li>unsupported claims about passion.</li>
</ul>
<h1>Common mistakes</h1>
<h1>Opening with an empty formality</h1>
<p>Avoid:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>I am writing to apply for the Software Engineer position at Acme.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The recipient already knows why the document exists.</p>
<p>Use the opening for relevance.</p>
<p>Better:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>I have used Acme&#39;s reconciliation API for the past year, and the failure-recovery work described in this role closely matches the systems I currently own.</p>
</blockquote>
<h1>Repeating the résumé</h1>
<p>A cover letter should not convert every résumé bullet into prose.</p>
<p>Choose one example and explain why it matters.</p>
<h1>Using adjectives instead of evidence</h1>
<p>Avoid:</p>
<ul>
<li>passionate;</li>
<li>hardworking;</li>
<li>detail-oriented;</li>
<li>innovative;</li>
<li>results-driven;</li>
<li>team player;</li>
<li>fast learner.</li>
</ul>
<p>Show those traits.</p>
<p>Instead of:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>I am a fast learner.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Write:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>I moved from Python to Go for a service migration and delivered the first production endpoint within six weeks.</p>
</blockquote>
<h1>Writing too much</h1>
<p>A hiring manager may review dozens of applications in one session.</p>
<p>Respect that reality.</p>
<p>Cut:</p>
<ul>
<li>background details;</li>
<li>unrelated projects;</li>
<li>repeated context;</li>
<li>long company praise;</li>
<li>technical implementation details better saved for the interview.</li>
</ul>
<h1>Making it about what the company can do for you</h1>
<p>It is reasonable to mention growth or mentorship, especially for junior roles.</p>
<p>But the letter should also explain the value you bring.</p>
<p>Weak:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>This role would be a great opportunity for me to learn React.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Stronger:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>I have built and deployed two TypeScript applications and am looking for a team where I can contribute that experience while developing stronger React architecture skills through code review and mentorship.</p>
</blockquote>
<h1>Overstating familiarity</h1>
<p>Do not write as though you have deeply researched a company when you have only read the homepage.</p>
<p>Be specific and honest.</p>
<h1>Addressing the wrong company</h1>
<p>This is one of the most damaging and avoidable errors.</p>
<p>Check every proper noun.</p>
<h1>Sending broken links</h1>
<p>Open your:</p>
<ul>
<li>portfolio;</li>
<li>GitHub;</li>
<li>demo;</li>
<li>writing sample;</li>
<li>project documentation.</li>
</ul>
<p>Verify that links work without requiring your private account.</p>
<h1>Using unexplained technical jargon</h1>
<p>The first reader may be:</p>
<ul>
<li>recruiter;</li>
<li>founder;</li>
<li>hiring manager;</li>
<li>engineering manager.</li>
</ul>
<p>Write clearly enough for each of them.</p>
<h1>Sounding desperate</h1>
<p>Avoid:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>I would do anything for this opportunity.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Professional interest is stronger than desperation.</p>
<h1>Explaining a gap defensively</h1>
<p>One factual sentence is usually sufficient.</p>
<h1>Writing the same letter for every role</h1>
<p>A reusable structure is fine.</p>
<p>Reusable claims of interest are not.</p>
<h1>Should you mention salary?</h1>
<p>Only when:</p>
<ul>
<li>the employer requests expectations;</li>
<li>the application requires it;</li>
<li>you need to establish alignment early;</li>
<li>you are responding to direct outreach.</li>
</ul>
<p>A cover letter is usually not the best place for detailed negotiation.</p>
<h1>Should you mention relocation?</h1>
<p>Yes, when location could create uncertainty.</p>
<p>Example:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>I am currently based in Denver and plan to relocate to Chicago in September. I do not require relocation assistance.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Or:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>I am applying only to permanently remote roles and can maintain at least five hours of overlap with Eastern Time.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Clarity prevents unnecessary rejection.</p>
<h1>Should you mention work authorization?</h1>
<p>When the application does not ask and it may be relevant, a short factual line can help.</p>
<p>Example:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>I am authorized to work in the United States without current or future sponsorship.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Do not add this when it is irrelevant.</p>
<h1>Should you explain being laid off?</h1>
<p>Only briefly.</p>
<p>Example:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>My position was eliminated during a company-wide reduction in force in May.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Then move on to your evidence and goals.</p>
<h1>Should junior developers write cover letters?</h1>
<p>Junior candidates often benefit more than experienced candidates because their résumé contains less professional evidence.</p>
<p>A cover letter can highlight:</p>
<ul>
<li>a strong project;</li>
<li>learning trajectory;</li>
<li>previous-career experience;</li>
<li>open-source work;</li>
<li>communication;</li>
<li>reason for choosing the company;</li>
<li>evidence of initiative.</li>
</ul>
<p>Do not apologize for being junior.</p>
<p>Show what you have built and how you learn.</p>
<h1>Should senior developers write cover letters?</h1>
<p>A senior candidate may use a letter to clarify:</p>
<ul>
<li>scope;</li>
<li>desired technical direction;</li>
<li>leadership style;</li>
<li>move between management and individual contribution;</li>
<li>interest in the company&#39;s architecture;</li>
<li>experience with a specific domain problem.</li>
</ul>
<p>Senior letters should focus less on enthusiasm and more on alignment.</p>
<p>Example:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>I am looking for a staff-level individual-contributor role centered on cross-team platform work rather than people management. The ownership described across deployment, service templates, and observability is closely aligned with the work I have led during the past three years.</p>
</blockquote>
<h1>Should you include a cover letter on JobsList.dev?</h1>
<p>When a listing allows an application note, use it when you can add specific value.</p>
<p>A concise message may be enough:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>I was interested in this role because your team is modernizing a PostgreSQL billing platform, which closely matches the migration work I currently own. I recently led a staged schema and API migration involving 40 million records with no customer-visible downtime. I would be glad to share more details.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The goal is not formality.</p>
<p>It is helping the company see the match.</p>
<h1>A final editing checklist</h1>
<p>Before submitting, ask:</p>
<ul>
<li>Is the company name correct?</li>
<li>Is the role title correct?</li>
<li>Does the first sentence contain something specific?</li>
<li>Does the letter explain why this company?</li>
<li>Does it include one concrete example?</li>
<li>Is my individual contribution clear?</li>
<li>Is there a result?</li>
<li>Does it explain anything the résumé might confuse?</li>
<li>Could this letter be sent to another company unchanged?</li>
<li>Is it under 250 words?</li>
<li>Are all links working?</li>
<li>Does it sound like a person?</li>
<li>Have I removed unsupported adjectives?</li>
<li>Have I proofread it aloud?</li>
</ul>
<p>When the answer to the “could this go to another company?” question is yes, revise the letter or skip it.</p>
<h1>The takeaway</h1>
<p>Developers do not need a cover letter for every application.</p>
<p>They need one when it provides information the résumé cannot communicate efficiently.</p>
<p>A useful cover letter should:</p>
<ol>
<li>Explain why this company and role are relevant.</li>
<li>Present one strong piece of evidence.</li>
<li>Clarify a transition, gap, or unusual background when necessary.</li>
<li>Direct the reader toward your best work.</li>
<li>Stay concise, specific, and human.</li>
</ol>
<p>Do not send a full page of generic enthusiasm.</p>
<p>Do not rewrite your résumé in paragraph form.</p>
<p>Do not let a template speak for you.</p>
<p>Lead with something only you could honestly say. Connect it to something the company genuinely needs. Support the claim with evidence, and stop while the reader still wants to know more.</p>
<p>A cover letter will not rescue a weak résumé or compensate for missing qualifications.</p>
<p>But when two capable developers look similar on paper, the one who clearly explains the connection may earn the interview.</p>
