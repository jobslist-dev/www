How to Do Your Best on an Assessment (and Why It's Not a Trick)

For candidates: practical advice on preparing for technical assessments, managing your time, avoiding common mistakes, and demonstrating the skills employers actually want to hire.

How to Do Your Best on an Assessment (and Why It's Not a Trick)

Technical assessments make a lot of candidates nervous. Some worry they're full of trick questions. Others assume they're just another obstacle between them and an interview.

The reality is much simpler.

A well-designed assessment isn't trying to catch you making mistakes. It's trying to answer a straightforward question:

"Can this person think through problems in the same way they'll need to on the job?"

That's good news for candidates.

Unlike résumé filters—which often rely on keywords, years of experience, or previous employers—an assessment gives you the opportunity to demonstrate your actual ability.

Whether you're self-taught, a computer science graduate, changing careers, or returning after a career break, an assessment lets your skills speak for themselves.

This guide explains how to prepare, what employers are really looking for, and how to perform at your best without unnecessary stress.

Why Companies Use Assessments

Hiring managers face a difficult problem.

A single developer position may receive hundreds of applications.

Many applicants have similar résumés.

Many claim experience with the same technologies.

An assessment gives employers another signal beyond a résumé.

Instead of asking:

"Where have you worked?"

they can also ask:

"How do you solve problems?"

Good assessments reduce bias because they evaluate demonstrated thinking instead of assumptions based on:

  • Previous employers
  • University attended
  • Years of experience
  • Professional network
  • CV formatting

That makes them one of the fairest parts of a modern hiring process.

What Employers Are Actually Looking For

Many candidates assume every question has one perfect answer.

In reality, employers are usually evaluating much broader qualities.

They're looking for evidence that you can:

  • Read requirements carefully
  • Understand unfamiliar code
  • Debug methodically
  • Think logically
  • Balance trade-offs
  • Write maintainable solutions
  • Communicate clearly
  • Stay calm under pressure

They're evaluating how you think, not just what you know.

There Are No Hidden Traps

Good assessments are not designed to trick candidates.

They shouldn't depend on:

  • intentionally misleading wording
  • obscure trivia
  • memorized syntax
  • gotcha questions
  • impossible time limits

Instead, they reward the kinds of thinking successful engineers use every day.

If you catch yourself searching for "the trick," stop.

There usually isn't one.

Read the question.

Understand what it's asking.

Answer honestly.

Move on.

Before You Start

A little preparation goes a long way.

Pick your best time

If you're most alert in the morning, don't schedule your assessment late at night.

Choose a time when you're focused and unlikely to be interrupted.

Use a comfortable setup

Whenever possible:

  • Use a desktop or laptop.
  • Use a full keyboard.
  • Close unnecessary applications.
  • Silence notifications.
  • Have water nearby.

You're creating the same conditions you'd want for focused work.

Check your internet connection

Most modern assessment platforms automatically save progress.

Still, it's worth checking your internet connection beforehand to reduce unnecessary stress.

Get proper rest

Being well rested is worth more than an extra hour of last-minute studying.

Assessments usually reward reasoning more than memorization.

A fresh mind performs noticeably better.

Don't Cram the Night Before

Many candidates spend hours reviewing syntax they'll never use.

Instead, spend your preparation time reviewing fundamentals.

For example:

  • Variables and data structures
  • Loops
  • Functions
  • Objects
  • Arrays
  • Error handling
  • Basic SQL
  • HTTP fundamentals
  • REST APIs
  • Git basics
  • HTML/CSS fundamentals (for frontend roles)

You don't need to memorize everything.

You need to understand the concepts.

Read Every Question Carefully

One of the most common reasons candidates lose points has nothing to do with technical skill.

They simply answer a different question than the one being asked.

Before selecting an answer:

  • Read the entire question.

  • Read every option.

  • Identify exactly what it's asking.

  • Watch for words like:

    • NOT
    • BEST
    • MOST
    • FIRST
    • LEAST
    • ALWAYS
    • EXCEPT

Small words often change the entire meaning.

Taking ten extra seconds is usually worth it.

Pace Yourself

Technical assessments are rarely won by finishing first.

They're won by finishing thoughtfully.

A useful strategy:

  1. Answer easy questions quickly.
  2. Skip difficult questions temporarily.
  3. Return later with fresh eyes.
  4. Leave a few minutes at the end for review.

Many candidates spend ten minutes wrestling with one question while leaving five easier ones unanswered.

Don't let one difficult problem steal time from everything else.

Use the Assessment Features

Most modern assessment platforms include helpful features.

Depending on the platform, you may be able to:

  • Skip and return later
  • Change answers before submitting
  • Flag difficult questions
  • Track remaining time
  • Automatically save progress

Take advantage of those tools.

They're there to help you manage your time—not pressure you.

Think Before You Code

If your assessment includes coding exercises, resist the temptation to immediately start typing.

Spend a minute planning.

Ask yourself:

  • What inputs exist?
  • What outputs are expected?
  • What edge cases matter?
  • What's the simplest solution?
  • Can this be broken into smaller pieces?

Professional developers spend a surprising amount of time thinking before writing code.

Interviewers expect that.

Write Simple Code

Many candidates believe they need to impress reviewers with advanced techniques.

Usually the opposite is true.

Reviewers prefer code that is:

  • Correct
  • Readable
  • Well named
  • Easy to maintain
  • Appropriately commented
  • Easy to debug

Simple, clean code almost always beats clever code.

Handle Edge Cases

Strong engineers think beyond the happy path.

Before submitting, ask yourself:

  • What happens if the input is empty?
  • What if it's null?
  • What if values are duplicated?
  • What if they're extremely large?
  • What if the API fails?
  • What if the network is slow?
  • What if permissions are missing?

You don't always need to handle every edge case.

But demonstrating that you considered them is a strong hiring signal.

Test Your Thinking

Even if the assessment doesn't require automated tests, mentally verify your solution.

Walk through it.

Pretend you're the computer.

Use small examples.

Ask:

  • Does this work?
  • What breaks it?
  • Did I overlook anything?

Many bugs become obvious when you slow down and trace the execution.

Communicate Your Reasoning

Some assessments include written explanations.

Don't skip them.

Companies often care just as much about your reasoning as your final answer.

Explain:

  • Why you chose an approach
  • What trade-offs you considered
  • Assumptions you made
  • Alternative solutions
  • Improvements you'd make with more time

Great engineers explain their thinking clearly.

That's a valuable skill on every team.

What If You Don't Know the Answer?

Everyone encounters questions they can't solve immediately.

That's normal.

Don't panic.

Instead:

  • Eliminate obviously incorrect answers.
  • Apply first principles.
  • Make your best judgment.
  • Move forward.

Perfect scores are rarely required.

Consistent reasoning matters far more than perfection.

Using AI During Assessments

Artificial intelligence has changed software development.

Many engineers use AI every day at work.

Different employers have different policies regarding assessments.

Always follow the instructions provided.

If AI tools are permitted:

  • Use them responsibly.
  • Understand every answer.
  • Be able to explain every line.

If AI tools are prohibited:

  • Respect the rules.

Remember that interviews often follow assessments.

If you can't explain your own work, it quickly becomes obvious.

Ultimately, assessments are there to help place you into a role where you'll succeed—not simply to produce the highest score possible.

If Something Goes Wrong

Technology isn't perfect.

Your browser may crash.

Your laptop battery might die.

Your internet connection could briefly disconnect.

Don't panic.

Most modern assessment systems automatically save your progress and allow you to resume.

If you experience a genuine technical issue:

  • Reopen the assessment.
  • Resume where you left off.
  • Contact support if instructed.

Don't assume your effort has been lost.

After You Finish

Before hitting Submit:

Take two or three minutes to review everything.

Ask yourself:

  • Did I answer every question?
  • Did I accidentally skip one?
  • Are there obvious mistakes?
  • Could I improve anything quickly?

Those final minutes often recover more points than an hour of last-minute studying.

What Happens Next?

Every company uses assessment results differently.

Some use them as:

  • an interview screening tool
  • one input among many
  • a discussion starter during technical interviews
  • evidence of problem-solving ability

A lower score doesn't necessarily eliminate you.

Likewise, a perfect score doesn't automatically guarantee an offer.

Hiring decisions also consider:

  • Communication
  • Teamwork
  • Experience
  • Motivation
  • Culture fit
  • Portfolio
  • References
  • Interviews

Treat the assessment as one important part of the overall hiring process.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Avoid these frequent pitfalls:

  • Starting immediately without reading carefully.
  • Spending too long on one difficult question.
  • Trying to memorize obscure trivia beforehand.
  • Writing overly complicated solutions.
  • Ignoring edge cases.
  • Not reviewing your answers before submitting.
  • Panicking after encountering one difficult question.
  • Assuming every question has a hidden trick.

Staying calm often improves your score more than learning one extra concept.

Assessment Day Checklist

Before you begin:

  • ✅ Get a good night's sleep.
  • ✅ Eat something beforehand.
  • ✅ Choose a quiet environment.
  • ✅ Use a reliable computer and internet connection.
  • ✅ Close distractions.
  • ✅ Read every question carefully.
  • ✅ Manage your time.
  • ✅ Think before coding.
  • ✅ Keep solutions simple.
  • ✅ Review your answers before submitting.

Frequently Asked Questions

What if I don't finish?

Many candidates don't complete every question.

Answering more questions correctly is usually better than spending all your time perfecting one answer.

Should I guess?

If there's no penalty for incorrect answers, making an educated guess is usually better than leaving a question blank.

Read the assessment instructions carefully.

Will employers only hire people with perfect scores?

No.

Most hiring decisions combine assessment performance with interviews, experience, communication, and portfolio quality.

Can I improve over time?

Absolutely.

Technical assessments measure skills that improve with practice.

Building projects, reading code, solving real problems, and interviewing regularly all strengthen your performance.

The Takeaway

A technical assessment isn't designed to catch you out—it's designed to help employers see how you approach real-world problems.

Come prepared, stay calm, manage your time, and focus on demonstrating your thinking rather than chasing perfection. Read carefully, solve problems methodically, and communicate your reasoning whenever you can. Those are the same habits that make someone successful on the job, and they're exactly what great employers hope to find.

Whether you're applying for your first developer role or your tenth, remember that assessments are opportunities—not obstacles. Every one you complete gives you more experience, more confidence, and another chance to show what you can really do.
