Building a Design Portfolio That Lands Interviews

The JobsList.dev Team·19 марта 2026 г.·28 min read

For designers, the portfolio is often the most important part of the application.

A résumé tells a hiring team where you worked, what roles you held, and which skills you claim. A portfolio shows how you understand problems, make decisions, collaborate with others, respond to constraints, and turn ideas into useful outcomes.

That distinction matters.

Most hiring teams are not looking for a gallery of attractive screens. They are trying to answer deeper questions:

  • Can this person understand a real product problem?
  • Can they explain why they made a decision?
  • Can they work within technical and business constraints?
  • Can they collaborate with researchers, engineers, product managers, and stakeholders?
  • Can they evaluate whether a design worked?
  • Can they distinguish their own contribution from the work of a team?
  • Can they communicate clearly enough to defend and evolve the work?

A strong portfolio does not simply prove that you can make polished interfaces.

It proves that you can solve meaningful problems through design.

Understand what your portfolio is supposed to do

Your portfolio does not need to document every project you have ever completed.

Its job is to create enough confidence for a hiring team to invite you into the interview process.

A good portfolio should help a reviewer understand:

  • what kind of designer you are;
  • which problems you are strongest at solving;
  • what level of responsibility you have held;
  • how you approach ambiguity;
  • how you make and defend decisions;
  • what you personally contributed;
  • what changed because of your work;
  • whether your experience matches the role.

The best portfolios are selective, structured, and easy to understand.

They do not try to prove everything at once.

Start by choosing the role you want

Before selecting projects, define the type of position you are targeting.

A portfolio for a product designer should not be organized the same way as one for a brand designer, design systems designer, UX researcher, or visual designer.

Possible targets include:

  • Product Designer
  • Senior Product Designer
  • UX Designer
  • UI Designer
  • Interaction Designer
  • Visual Designer
  • Brand Designer
  • Design Systems Designer
  • UX Researcher
  • Service Designer
  • Content Designer
  • Motion Designer
  • Design Engineer
  • Creative Director

Your target affects which projects you include and which parts of each project you emphasize.

For example, a product-design portfolio may prioritize:

  • product strategy;
  • user research;
  • flows;
  • interaction design;
  • prototyping;
  • usability;
  • experimentation;
  • product outcomes.

A visual-design portfolio may emphasize:

  • composition;
  • typography;
  • art direction;
  • systems;
  • campaign consistency;
  • brand expression;
  • production quality.

A design-systems portfolio may focus on:

  • component architecture;
  • accessibility;
  • governance;
  • adoption;
  • documentation;
  • cross-platform consistency;
  • collaboration with engineering.

A portfolio becomes more persuasive when its contents clearly support the job you are applying for.

Lead with a small number of strong case studies

Three or four strong case studies are usually more useful than a gallery containing twenty loosely explained projects.

A reviewer should be able to understand your best work without searching for it.

Your featured projects should collectively demonstrate:

  • relevant experience;
  • range;
  • increasing complexity;
  • clear ownership;
  • strong communication;
  • meaningful outcomes.

A useful portfolio might include:

  1. One flagship project with substantial depth.
  2. One project showing a different type of problem.
  3. One project demonstrating collaboration, systems thinking, or measurable impact.
  4. One shorter project showing craft or exploration.

You do not need the maximum number of projects. You need enough evidence to support the role.

Curate for relevance, not chronology

Your most recent project is not automatically your strongest project.

Order your work according to what the hiring team should see first.

For a product-design role, you might lead with:

  • an end-to-end product redesign;
  • a complex workflow;
  • an onboarding or conversion project;
  • a feature that produced measurable results.

For a systems-focused role, you might lead with:

  • a design system;
  • a component library;
  • accessibility improvements;
  • governance and adoption work.

For a brand role, you might lead with:

  • a complete identity system;
  • a campaign with multiple touchpoints;
  • a repositioning project;
  • a large-scale editorial or digital system.

The first project should create the strongest case that you can do the job.

Build case studies, not image galleries

A case study should explain what happened, not merely display the final artifacts.

A useful structure is:

  1. Context
  2. Problem
  3. Your role
  4. Goals
  5. Constraints
  6. Research or discovery
  7. Process
  8. Important decisions
  9. Collaboration
  10. Final solution
  11. Outcome
  12. Reflection

You do not need to use these exact headings, but the reader should be able to find the information easily.

Open with a clear project summary

At the beginning of each case study, give the reviewer the information needed to decide whether to keep reading.

Include:

  • the product or organization;
  • the problem;
  • your role;
  • the team;
  • the timeline;
  • the outcome.

Example:

I led product design for a six-month redesign of a B2B logistics dashboard used by more than 4,000 dispatchers. I worked with one product manager, six engineers, and a researcher to simplify route planning and exception management. The new workflow reduced average planning time by 31% and lowered support requests related to scheduling errors.

This gives the reader useful context immediately.

Explain the problem before showing the solution

Do not begin with polished screens.

Start with the situation the team needed to improve.

A strong problem statement explains:

  • who experienced the problem;
  • what they were trying to accomplish;
  • what made the current experience difficult;
  • why the problem mattered;
  • what evidence supported the problem.

Weak:

“The dashboard needed a redesign.”

Stronger:

“Dispatchers were using five separate views to assign routes, compare driver availability, and resolve conflicts. Internal analytics and support interviews showed that the fragmented workflow increased planning time and caused avoidable scheduling errors.”

The stronger version gives the project a reason to exist.

Describe your role honestly

Team projects are normal. Hiring teams do not expect you to have done everything yourself.

They do expect you to explain your contribution accurately.

Include:

  • what you owned;
  • what others owned;
  • where you collaborated;
  • what decisions you influenced;
  • whether you led or supported the work.

Example:

“I owned the workflow design, information architecture, prototyping, and usability testing. A UX researcher led the initial interviews, and a visual designer helped refine the final component styling.”

Avoid language that implies sole ownership of a team effort.

You should be able to answer:

  • Which work was yours?
  • Which decisions did you make?
  • Where did the team disagree?
  • What changed because of your input?

Show the constraints

Good design does not happen in ideal conditions.

Constraints often reveal more about your judgment than polished output does.

Relevant constraints may include:

  • limited engineering capacity;
  • legacy systems;
  • accessibility requirements;
  • regulatory obligations;
  • technical debt;
  • fixed deadlines;
  • incomplete data;
  • multiple user groups;
  • internationalization;
  • security;
  • performance;
  • brand limitations;
  • organizational disagreement.

Example:

“The team could not replace the legacy scheduling engine during the initial release, so the design had to improve clarity and error prevention without changing the underlying allocation logic.”

This explains why the solution took a particular form.

Show enough research to support the decisions

You do not need to include every research note or affinity map.

Show the evidence that materially influenced the design.

Relevant evidence may include:

  • user interviews;
  • usability tests;
  • surveys;
  • support tickets;
  • analytics;
  • session recordings;
  • market research;
  • stakeholder interviews;
  • competitive review;
  • accessibility audits;
  • customer feedback.

Explain:

  • what you wanted to learn;
  • which method you used;
  • what you discovered;
  • how the discovery affected the design.

Weak:

“We conducted user research.”

Stronger:

“We interviewed eight dispatchers and reviewed 120 recent support tickets. The most common issue was not route creation itself, but recovering from conflicts after a driver became unavailable. That finding changed the project focus from creating a faster form to building a more resilient exception-management flow.”

The connection between evidence and decision is what matters.

Do not turn the case study into a process checklist

Hiring teams do not need to see every workshop, sticky note, and design artifact.

Process is valuable only when it explains how the project moved forward.

Avoid:

  • showing research because every case study is expected to contain research;
  • including a persona that did not affect the design;
  • adding a journey map without explaining what it revealed;
  • displaying dozens of low-resolution wireframes;
  • using design-process diagrams as decoration.

Ask:

“Does this artifact help the reader understand a decision?”

If not, remove it.

Show the alternatives you considered

Design judgment becomes visible when the reviewer can see that you evaluated more than one option.

Include:

  • early explorations;
  • competing flows;
  • layout alternatives;
  • interaction models;
  • rejected approaches;
  • prototype variations.

Then explain why one direction was selected.

Example:

“We tested a persistent side panel and a full-screen conflict-resolution flow. The side panel allowed faster comparison but became difficult to use on smaller screens. The full-screen flow reduced context switching and performed better in moderated testing, so we used it for the initial release.”

This shows decision-making rather than decoration.

Explain trade-offs

There is rarely one objectively correct design.

Strong designers understand that every decision creates trade-offs.

Discuss trade-offs involving:

  • simplicity versus flexibility;
  • speed versus control;
  • discoverability versus density;
  • consistency versus specialized behavior;
  • short-term delivery versus long-term scalability;
  • user preference versus business requirements;
  • automation versus manual oversight;
  • visual polish versus performance.

Example:

“We intentionally reduced customization in the first release. That limited some advanced workflows, but it allowed the team to simplify onboarding and ship within the quarter. We documented the extension points needed for future configuration.”

Trade-off discussions are especially valuable for senior roles.

Show before and after

Before-and-after comparisons help reviewers understand what improved.

Useful comparisons may include:

  • old and new workflows;
  • information hierarchy;
  • task completion steps;
  • error handling;
  • navigation;
  • mobile behavior;
  • accessibility;
  • component consistency;
  • visual clarity.

Do not rely only on side-by-side screenshots.

Explain what changed and why.

Example:

“The previous workflow required seven screens and repeated data entry. The redesigned flow consolidated the task into three stages and reused existing shipment data, reducing average completion time from 11 minutes to 6 minutes.”

Connect design choices to user needs

Do not explain design only in visual terms.

Instead of:

“We used a blue button because it looked cleaner.”

say:

“We reserved the primary color for the next required action so users could distinguish task progression from secondary controls.”

Instead of:

“We used cards to modernize the dashboard.”

say:

“We grouped related exceptions by route so dispatchers could evaluate risk without opening each item individually.”

Strong explanations connect the interface to behavior and outcomes.

Show collaboration

Design is rarely an isolated activity.

Explain how you worked with:

  • product managers;
  • engineers;
  • researchers;
  • data analysts;
  • customer support;
  • sales;
  • legal;
  • security;
  • executives;
  • customers.

Useful examples include:

  • negotiating scope;
  • reviewing technical feasibility;
  • resolving disagreement;
  • changing direction after feedback;
  • supporting implementation;
  • defining acceptance criteria;
  • participating in quality assurance.

Example:

“Engineering identified that real-time validation would delay the launch by several weeks. We worked together on a staged approach that introduced server-side validation first and added inline feedback in the following release.”

This demonstrates flexibility and partnership.

Include implementation involvement

A strong product-design case study should not end at the handoff.

Show how you supported delivery:

  • design reviews;
  • annotations;
  • acceptance criteria;
  • prototype specifications;
  • accessibility review;
  • quality assurance;
  • implementation feedback;
  • post-launch iteration.

Example:

“I reviewed the implementation across desktop and mobile, documented interaction issues, and worked with engineering to resolve accessibility problems before release.”

This shows that you care about the shipped result, not just the design file.

Quantify the outcome

Metrics make case studies more credible.

Possible outcomes include:

  • conversion;
  • activation;
  • retention;
  • task completion;
  • error reduction;
  • support volume;
  • satisfaction;
  • adoption;
  • completion time;
  • revenue;
  • cost savings;
  • accessibility compliance;
  • design-system usage;
  • engineering speed.

Examples:

  • “Increased trial-to-paid conversion by 14%.”
  • “Reduced support tickets related to billing by 26%.”
  • “Improved task completion from 62% to 84%.”
  • “Cut average onboarding time by seven minutes.”
  • “Increased adoption of the new component library to 78% of product surfaces.”
  • “Reduced design-to-development rework during the following quarter.”

Use metrics honestly.

Do not present correlation as proof of causation unless the evidence supports it.

If the result was influenced by several factors, say so:

“Following the launch, activation increased by 11%. The design changes were one part of a broader onboarding initiative that also included email and pricing updates.”

Include qualitative outcomes

Not every project has reliable analytics.

Useful qualitative outcomes include:

  • strong usability-test performance;
  • positive customer feedback;
  • fewer reported points of confusion;
  • successful internal adoption;
  • improved stakeholder alignment;
  • reduced implementation ambiguity;
  • clearer accessibility behavior;
  • lessons from a pilot.

Be specific.

Weak:

“Users loved it.”

Stronger:

“Six of seven participants completed the revised workflow without assistance, compared with two of seven in the initial test.”

Explain when a project did not succeed

Not every case study needs a perfect ending.

A thoughtful case study about an imperfect result can demonstrate maturity.

You may explain:

  • what did not work;
  • which assumptions were wrong;
  • what the team learned;
  • how the design changed;
  • what you would do differently.

Example:

“The initial release improved completion speed but did not increase adoption among occasional users. Follow-up interviews showed that those users did not understand when the feature was relevant. We added contextual entry points and simplified the terminology in the next iteration.”

A credible lesson is often more valuable than a polished success story.

End with reflection

Reflection helps reviewers understand how you think beyond the final deliverable.

Useful questions include:

  • What would you change now?
  • Which assumption proved incorrect?
  • What did the project teach you?
  • What remains unresolved?
  • How would you approach the project with more time?
  • Which decision had the greatest impact?
  • Where did the team take on risk?

Keep reflection specific.

Weak:

“I learned that communication is important.”

Stronger:

“I learned to involve engineering before narrowing the interaction model. Earlier technical review would have prevented us from spending a week refining a direction that depended on unavailable event data.”

Adapt case-study depth to the project

Not every project needs a 3,000-word case study.

Use different levels of depth:

Flagship case study

A detailed project showing complexity, process, trade-offs, collaboration, and outcomes.

Standard case study

A concise explanation of the problem, process, decisions, and result.

Project snapshot

A short overview emphasizing a specific skill, visual outcome, or experiment.

This gives the portfolio variety without making every project exhausting to read.

Design for reviewers who skim

Most reviewers will skim before reading deeply.

Make the page easy to scan with:

  • descriptive headings;
  • short paragraphs;
  • clear captions;
  • highlighted outcomes;
  • visible role and timeline;
  • concise summaries;
  • generous spacing;
  • consistent hierarchy.

A reviewer should be able to understand the project by reading:

  • the opening summary;
  • the headings;
  • the captions;
  • the outcomes;
  • the reflection.

Do not hide essential information inside long paragraphs.

Write descriptive headings

Avoid generic headings such as:

  • Process
  • Research
  • Design
  • Results

Use headings that communicate the insight:

  • Dispatchers struggled most when plans changed
  • The initial flow exposed too many decisions at once
  • Testing shifted the design toward guided resolution
  • The redesign reduced planning time by 31%

Descriptive headings make the case study easier to understand quickly.

Use captions for every important image

Do not make reviewers guess what an image represents.

A good caption explains:

  • what the artifact is;
  • which stage it came from;
  • why it matters.

Example:

Early workflow exploration comparing persistent and step-based conflict resolution. Testing showed that the step-based model reduced missed validation errors.

Captions are especially important for:

  • wireframes;
  • diagrams;
  • journey maps;
  • prototypes;
  • before-and-after comparisons;
  • usability-testing results.

Keep images useful and legible

Avoid uploading enormous screenshots without context.

Crop images to emphasize the relevant detail.

Use:

  • readable text;
  • consistent image widths;
  • appropriate contrast;
  • zoomed details where needed;
  • captions;
  • device mockups sparingly.

Do not place every interface inside a dramatic perspective mockup. Those presentations often make the actual design harder to inspect.

Show enough of the real interface that reviewers can evaluate it.

Do not rely on animation

Motion can help explain an interaction, but it should not be required to understand the project.

Animations should:

  • load quickly;
  • have accessible alternatives;
  • not autoplay aggressively;
  • not hide important content;
  • not make the page difficult to navigate.

Use motion to clarify behavior, not to decorate the portfolio.

Make the site fast

A slow portfolio sends the wrong signal.

Optimize:

  • image formats;
  • dimensions;
  • compression;
  • video;
  • fonts;
  • scripts;
  • animations.

A portfolio should load well on average connections and mobile devices.

Common performance mistakes include:

  • uploading uncompressed images;
  • embedding several autoplay videos;
  • loading many font weights;
  • using large animation libraries for simple effects;
  • placing every project on one page.

The portfolio itself is part of the work sample.

Make it mobile-friendly

Recruiters and hiring managers may open your portfolio from a phone.

Test:

  • navigation;
  • type size;
  • image scaling;
  • captions;
  • buttons;
  • contact links;
  • résumé downloads;
  • embedded prototypes;
  • videos;
  • password prompts.

Do not assume every reviewer uses a desktop display.

Build accessibility into the portfolio

Accessibility is a design competency.

Your portfolio should include:

  • semantic headings;
  • keyboard navigation;
  • visible focus states;
  • sufficient contrast;
  • alt text;
  • readable type;
  • descriptive links;
  • captions or transcripts for media;
  • reduced-motion support;
  • appropriate form labels.

Avoid:

  • tiny gray text;
  • text over busy images;
  • hover-only navigation;
  • inaccessible custom cursors;
  • excessive animation;
  • auto-playing audio;
  • image-only project descriptions.

An inaccessible portfolio can undermine claims about user-centered design.

Keep navigation simple

Reviewers should be able to find:

  • your work;
  • your background;
  • your résumé;
  • your contact information.

A straightforward navigation might include:

  • Work
  • About
  • Résumé
  • Contact

Do not force the reviewer to learn a creative navigation system.

Novelty is not always usability.

Make contact information easy to find

Your portfolio should include:

  • professional email;
  • résumé link;
  • relevant professional profile;
  • location or time zone when useful;
  • availability for remote, hybrid, or relocation work when relevant.

Place contact information in more than one location, such as:

  • navigation;
  • footer;
  • about page;
  • final call to action.

Do not make a recruiter search for a way to contact you.

Link your résumé and portfolio together

Your résumé should contain the portfolio URL.

Your portfolio should include a downloadable résumé.

Keep role titles, dates, and project descriptions consistent across both.

Inconsistencies can create doubt.

Use a professional domain when possible

A custom domain can make your portfolio easier to remember, but it is not required.

Examples:

  • yourname.com
  • yourname.design
  • firstname-lastname.com

A well-organized hosted portfolio is better than an unfinished custom website.

Do not delay applying because you do not have a custom domain.

Avoid unnecessary personal branding

A personal brand can help, but it should not overpower the work.

You do not need:

  • an elaborate logo;
  • a lengthy manifesto;
  • a dramatic loading sequence;
  • several brand animations;
  • a fictional agency identity.

A clear name, role, and point of view are usually enough.

Write an effective homepage introduction

The homepage should quickly answer:

  • Who are you?
  • What kind of design work do you do?
  • What problems are you interested in?
  • Where can the reviewer see your projects?

Weak:

“I create delightful human-centered experiences that live at the intersection of empathy and innovation.”

Stronger:

“I’m a product designer focused on complex B2B workflows, design systems, and tools that help operational teams work more efficiently.”

The second version communicates a real specialization.

Include an About page that adds context

An About page should not repeat the résumé.

Use it to explain:

  • your design perspective;
  • the problems you enjoy;
  • relevant background;
  • how you work;
  • what you are looking for;
  • any unusual experience that strengthens your work.

Keep it concise and professional.

You may include a photo, but it is not required.

Tailor the portfolio for each application

You do not need to rebuild the website for every role.

You can still tailor the experience by:

  • reordering projects;
  • changing the homepage introduction;
  • highlighting relevant case studies;
  • adjusting the résumé;
  • using a role-specific landing page;
  • sending a direct link to the most relevant project.

For a design-systems role, lead with systems work.

For a mobile-product role, send the mobile case study first.

For a research-heavy role, emphasize discovery and validation.

Consider role-specific portfolio versions

If you apply to significantly different positions, one portfolio may not serve every audience well.

You might create different views for:

  • product design;
  • brand and visual design;
  • UX research;
  • design systems;
  • design engineering.

These can share the same site and content while changing emphasis.

Protect confidential work properly

Many designers work under nondisclosure agreements or with sensitive products.

Do not publish confidential information without permission.

Options include:

  • anonymizing the company;
  • changing or removing data;
  • recreating simplified examples;
  • describing the process without exposing proprietary details;
  • using a password-protected case study;
  • discussing the work live during interviews.

Explain the restriction clearly:

“This case study has been anonymized to protect confidential customer and business information.”

Do not imply that confidentiality prevents you from explaining your process entirely. You can usually discuss the problem, your role, decisions, and lessons at an appropriate level.

Use password protection carefully

Password-protected case studies can work, but they add friction.

Make access easy:

  • provide the password directly in the application;
  • place it near the portfolio link;
  • avoid requiring registration;
  • ensure the password works;
  • explain why the project is protected.

Do not protect the entire portfolio unless necessary.

Include concept work honestly

Concept projects can be valuable for students, career changers, and early-career designers.

Label them clearly.

Do not present an unsolicited redesign as if it were commissioned or validated by the company.

A strong concept project should still include:

  • a real problem;
  • reasonable research;
  • clear assumptions;
  • thoughtful decisions;
  • realistic constraints;
  • prototype testing;
  • reflection.

Avoid redesigning a famous product only because you dislike its appearance. Without business context, user research, or constraints, the work may demonstrate visual taste but little product judgment.

Early-career portfolios should show learning and rigor

If you have limited professional work, include:

  • internships;
  • student projects;
  • freelance work;
  • volunteer projects;
  • self-initiated projects;
  • hackathons;
  • open-source contributions;
  • research.

Focus on what you actually learned and contributed.

Do not inflate a classroom project into a fictional commercial success.

Hiring teams can appreciate early-career work when the thinking is honest and clear.

Senior portfolios should show scope and influence

Senior designers should demonstrate more than polished execution.

Relevant signals include:

  • framing ambiguous problems;
  • influencing product strategy;
  • aligning stakeholders;
  • managing trade-offs;
  • mentoring;
  • building systems;
  • driving adoption;
  • working across teams;
  • improving organizational quality;
  • connecting design to business outcomes.

A senior portfolio should show how you improve the work of others, not only the screens you personally created.

Design leaders should show organizational outcomes

Design managers and directors may include:

  • team building;
  • hiring;
  • coaching;
  • design operations;
  • process improvement;
  • strategy;
  • executive alignment;
  • cross-functional leadership;
  • team health;
  • design-system adoption;
  • quality improvements.

Make your leadership contribution clear.

Do not rely only on visual artifacts created by your team. Explain what you enabled.

Design engineers should show both craft and implementation

A design-engineering portfolio may include:

  • interactive prototypes;
  • production components;
  • design systems;
  • animations;
  • accessibility;
  • performance;
  • frontend architecture;
  • tooling;
  • collaboration between design and engineering.

Explain which parts you designed and which parts you implemented.

Include live examples or source code where appropriate.

UX researchers should adapt the case-study structure

A research portfolio should focus on:

  • research questions;
  • method selection;
  • participant criteria;
  • constraints;
  • synthesis;
  • insights;
  • influence;
  • outcomes.

Do not overwhelm reviewers with raw research artifacts.

Show how the work affected product decisions.

Content designers should show language decisions

A content-design case study may explain:

  • user intent;
  • information architecture;
  • terminology;
  • error messages;
  • onboarding;
  • localization;
  • content testing;
  • accessibility;
  • product outcomes.

Include before-and-after examples where possible.

Visual and brand designers should provide context

Visual work should still explain:

  • the brief;
  • the audience;
  • the constraints;
  • the concept;
  • the system;
  • production requirements;
  • rollout;
  • outcomes.

Do not present only isolated logos or mockups.

Show how the identity works across a real system.

Remove weak projects

Your portfolio may be judged by its weakest work.

Remove projects that:

  • no longer represent your ability;
  • lack context;
  • are unrelated to your target role;
  • contain unresolved quality problems;
  • duplicate stronger work;
  • cannot be explained clearly;
  • rely heavily on another designer’s contribution.

Fewer strong projects create a better impression than many uneven ones.

Watch for common portfolio mistakes

Showing only polished screens

Final screens do not reveal how you think.

Showing too much process

Process without decisions becomes noise.

Hiding your role

Reviewers need to know what you personally contributed.

Using vague outcomes

“Users liked it” is weaker than specific evidence.

Writing excessively long case studies

Depth is useful, but every section should earn its place.

Making the portfolio difficult to navigate

Creative navigation should not interfere with access.

Overusing device mockups

Mockups can obscure the actual interface.

Ignoring mobile and accessibility

The portfolio itself is a design sample.

Including broken links

Review every link before applying.

Using too much jargon

Clear writing signals clear thinking.

Failing to tailor the work

The portfolio should support the role you want.

Write like a real person

Avoid language such as:

  • leveraged;
  • synergized;
  • ideated;
  • utilized;
  • drove alignment;
  • crafted delightful experiences;
  • championed user-centricity.

Some of these terms are not always wrong, but they often obscure the actual work.

Prefer direct language.

Instead of:

“Leveraged cross-functional collaboration to ideate a user-centric solution.”

write:

“Worked with engineering and support to redesign the returns flow after customer interviews revealed repeated confusion.”

Clarity creates credibility.

Proofread carefully

A design portfolio with writing errors can weaken the impression of craft.

Check:

  • headings;
  • captions;
  • punctuation;
  • dates;
  • role descriptions;
  • image labels;
  • links;
  • capitalization;
  • terminology;
  • grammar.

Ask another person to review the site.

You are often too familiar with the work to notice missing context.

Test the portfolio with real reviewers

Ask someone to spend five minutes on your portfolio and then answer:

  • What kind of designer am I?
  • What was my strongest project?
  • What did I personally contribute?
  • What outcomes did I create?
  • What level do I appear to be?
  • What was confusing?
  • Would you interview me?

Do not explain the portfolio while they review it.

The site needs to communicate without you present.

Build a portfolio presentation version

Many design interviews include a portfolio presentation.

Do not assume the website alone will work well in a live meeting.

Create a presentation version that:

  • fits the available time;
  • focuses on one or two projects;
  • emphasizes decisions;
  • includes outcomes;
  • supports discussion;
  • avoids tiny screenshots;
  • allows questions.

A 45-minute presentation should not contain 100 dense slides.

Practice your timing.

Prepare to defend every project

Interviewers may ask:

  • Why did you choose this approach?
  • Which option did you reject?
  • What evidence supported the decision?
  • What did engineering push back on?
  • What would you change now?
  • How did you measure success?
  • Which part was yours?
  • What failed?
  • How did the design evolve after launch?

Your case study should help you answer these questions, but you should also prepare deeper detail for the interview.

Use AI carefully

AI can help you:

  • edit writing;
  • shorten case studies;
  • improve captions;
  • identify unclear sections;
  • reorganize content;
  • create presentation outlines.

Do not use AI to invent:

  • research;
  • metrics;
  • user quotes;
  • project outcomes;
  • responsibilities;
  • business context.

Your portfolio must remain an accurate record of your work.

A practical portfolio checklist

Before sending your portfolio, confirm that:

  • the homepage identifies your role clearly;
  • your strongest work appears first;
  • each case study explains the problem;
  • your personal contribution is explicit;
  • constraints are included;
  • process artifacts support real decisions;
  • trade-offs are discussed;
  • outcomes are credible;
  • the writing is concise and clear;
  • images are legible;
  • the site works on mobile;
  • pages load quickly;
  • navigation is simple;
  • accessibility basics are covered;
  • contact information is easy to find;
  • the résumé link works;
  • confidential information is protected;
  • every link has been tested;
  • the work matches the roles you are targeting.

Use this structure when building or revising a project page.

A short name that explains the work.

Summary

One paragraph covering the product, problem, role, team, timeline, and result.

Problem

A concise case-study example

Project

Improving subscription cancellation for a B2B analytics platform.

Problem

Customers had to contact support to change or cancel plans. This created frustration, delayed billing changes, and generated approximately 300 support requests per month.

Role

I led product design and worked with a product manager, two engineers, and a support lead.

Evidence

Support-ticket analysis showed that most customers wanted to downgrade rather than leave entirely. Interviews also revealed confusion about billing dates and data retention.

Exploration

We explored immediate cancellation, scheduled cancellation, and guided downgrade options. Testing showed that customers preferred clear control over retention and billing timing rather than persuasive retention messaging.

Solution

We introduced self-service plan changes, explicit billing previews, data-retention explanations, and a scheduled cancellation option.

Outcome

Cancellation-related support volume fell by 38%, and 17% of customers who entered the flow selected a lower plan instead of canceling.

Reflection

The initial design placed downgrade options too late in the flow. Earlier testing with customers at different account sizes would have revealed that sooner.

This structure is short, but it still demonstrates problem framing, evidence, decisions, collaboration, outcome, and learning.

The takeaway

A design portfolio that lands interviews is not a collection of your prettiest screens.

It is a focused body of evidence that shows:

  • how you understand problems;
  • how you use research and data;
  • how you make decisions;
  • how you handle constraints;
  • how you collaborate;
  • what you personally contributed;
  • what changed because of your work;
  • what you learned.

Curate ruthlessly. Lead with relevant case studies. Explain the problem before the solution. Show the decisions and trade-offs. Quantify outcomes when possible. Keep the experience fast, accessible, and easy to scan.

Your portfolio does not need to prove that every project was perfect.

It needs to prove that you can think clearly, design responsibly, and help a team create better outcomes.
