e:T3b1d,<p>Engineering compensation is one of the few business decisions that compounds for years.</p>
<p>Every offer you make becomes a reference point for future offers, promotions, raises, budgets, retention conversations, and hiring negotiations. If compensation decisions are made ad hoc, eventually someone discovers that two engineers doing nearly identical work are earning dramatically different salaries—and nobody can clearly explain why.</p>
<p>That creates frustration, damages trust, slows hiring, and makes promotion conversations emotional instead of objective.</p>
<p>The good news is that solving this doesn&#39;t require a massive HR department or enterprise software.</p>
<p>A small company with twenty engineers can build an excellent compensation framework in a weekend.</p>
<p>The goal isn&#39;t to remove human judgment.</p>
<p>The goal is to make compensation <strong>consistent, explainable, competitive, and fair.</strong></p>
<hr>
<h1>Why compensation frameworks matter</h1>
<p>Most founders think compensation exists to close offers.</p>
<p>Experienced engineering leaders know compensation exists to solve four different problems simultaneously:</p>
<ul>
<li>Attract excellent engineers</li>
<li>Retain great employees</li>
<li>Reward increasing impact</li>
<li>Maintain internal fairness</li>
</ul>
<p>Miss any one of those and the entire system starts breaking down.</p>
<p>Without a framework you&#39;ll eventually encounter:</p>
<ul>
<li>New hires earning more than loyal employees.</li>
<li>Engineers demanding promotions because someone else got one.</li>
<li>Managers making different decisions for similar people.</li>
<li>Offer negotiations based entirely on who negotiates hardest.</li>
<li>Difficulty forecasting payroll as the team grows.</li>
</ul>
<p>Good compensation systems prevent these problems before they happen.</p>
<hr>
<h1>What engineering levels actually represent</h1>
<p>One of the biggest misconceptions in software hiring is that levels measure experience.</p>
<p>They don&#39;t.</p>
<p>They measure <strong>scope, ownership, judgment, and impact.</strong></p>
<p>Years of experience are merely one possible input.</p>
<p>Someone with three years of experience may comfortably perform at Senior level.</p>
<p>Someone with ten years may still perform best as a Mid-level engineer.</p>
<p>The level should describe <strong>what someone consistently delivers</strong>, not how long they&#39;ve been employed.</p>
<p>Good engineering ladders evaluate:</p>
<ul>
<li>Technical ability</li>
<li>Decision making</li>
<li>Independence</li>
<li>Communication</li>
<li>System ownership</li>
<li>Mentorship</li>
<li>Business impact</li>
</ul>
<p>None of those are measured by birthdays.</p>
<hr>
<h1>A practical engineering leveling framework</h1>
<p>Most companies don&#39;t need twelve engineering levels.</p>
<p>Four to six clearly defined levels are enough for many organizations.</p>

<p>The names matter far less than the expectations.</p>
<p>What matters is that everyone understands the difference.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Define levels by observable behavior</h1>
<p>Every level should answer four questions.</p>
<h2>What problems do they solve?</h2>
<p>Example:</p>
<p>Junior:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Solves clearly defined engineering tasks.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Senior:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Solves ambiguous engineering problems with incomplete information.</p>
</blockquote>
<hr>
<h2>How much supervision do they need?</h2>
<p>Junior:</p>
<p>Requires frequent guidance.</p>
<p>Mid:</p>
<p>Requires occasional guidance.</p>
<p>Senior:</p>
<p>Works independently.</p>
<p>Staff:</p>
<p>Creates clarity for others.</p>
<hr>
<h2>What systems do they own?</h2>
<p>Junior:</p>
<p>Small features.</p>
<p>Mid:</p>
<p>Entire services.</p>
<p>Senior:</p>
<p>Critical systems.</p>
<p>Staff:</p>
<p>Multiple systems.</p>
<p>Principal:</p>
<p>Company architecture.</p>
<p>Ownership scales with level.</p>
<hr>
<h2>Who benefits from their work?</h2>
<p>Junior:</p>
<p>Their own work.</p>
<p>Mid:</p>
<p>Their immediate team.</p>
<p>Senior:</p>
<p>Multiple engineers.</p>
<p>Staff:</p>
<p>Entire engineering organization.</p>
<p>Principal:</p>
<p>Entire business.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Promotions should never be calendar events</h1>
<p>Many companies accidentally promote people simply because they&#39;ve &quot;been here long enough.&quot;</p>
<p>That creates title inflation.</p>
<p>Promotion should happen because someone has already been performing consistently at the next level—not because they&#39;re asking for it.</p>
<p>A useful rule:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Promote people <strong>after</strong> they&#39;ve demonstrated sustained next-level performance.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Not before.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Build compensation bands around every level</h1>
<p>Each engineering level should have a salary range rather than a single number.</p>
<p>Most companies use:</p>
<ul>
<li>Minimum</li>
<li>Midpoint</li>
<li>Maximum</li>
</ul>
<p>Example:</p>

<li>Funding stage</li>
</ul>
<p>The framework matters more than the exact numbers.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Why compensation bands overlap</h1>
<p>Many founders assume salary bands shouldn&#39;t overlap.</p>
<p>In reality they almost always should.</p>
<p>For example:</p>
<p>Top-performing Mid Engineer:</p>
<p>$165k</p>
<p>New Senior Engineer:</p>
<p>$160k</p>
<p>That&#39;s perfectly reasonable.</p>
<p>The senior title reflects broader expectations—not an automatic pay jump.</p>
<p>Otherwise companies end up promoting people simply to justify raises.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Positioning yourself against the market</h1>
<p>Every company competes differently.</p>
<p>Choose intentionally.</p>
<p>Common strategies include:</p>
<h3>Market Median (50th percentile)</h3>
<p>Suitable for:</p>
<ul>
<li>Bootstrapped companies</li>
<li>Stable businesses</li>
<li>Predictable hiring</li>
</ul>
<p>Advantages:</p>
<ul>
<li>Sustainable</li>
<li>Easy budgeting</li>
</ul>
<p>Tradeoffs:</p>
<ul>
<li>Harder to attract elite candidates.</li>
</ul>
<hr>
<h3>Above Market (75th percentile)</h3>
<p>Suitable for:</p>
<ul>
<li>Venture-backed startups</li>
<li>High-growth companies</li>
<li>Difficult recruiting markets</li>
</ul>
<p>Advantages:</p>
<ul>
<li>Stronger hiring</li>
<li>Lower turnover</li>
</ul>
<p>Tradeoffs:</p>
<ul>
<li>Higher payroll costs.</li>
</ul>
<hr>
<h3>Premium Compensation (90th+ percentile)</h3>
<p>Suitable for:</p>
<ul>
<li>Exceptional talent density</li>
<li>Critical engineering organizations</li>
<li>Companies where every engineer has enormous leverage</li>
</ul>
<p>Advantages:</p>
<ul>
<li>Access to elite candidates.</li>
</ul>
<p>Tradeoffs:</p>
<ul>
<li>Expensive.</li>
</ul>
<p>Requires exceptional hiring discipline.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Compensation is more than salary</h1>
<p>Engineers evaluate the complete package.</p>
<p>A competitive offer includes:</p>
<ul>
<li>Base salary</li>
<li>Annual bonus</li>
<li>Equity</li>
<li>Retirement contributions</li>
<li>Healthcare</li>
<li>Remote work support</li>
<li>Home office budget</li>
<li>Learning budget</li>
<li>Conference budget</li>
<li>Vacation policy</li>
<li>Flexible schedule</li>
<li>Paid parental leave</li>
</ul>
<p>Sometimes a company paying slightly below market still wins because everything else is significantly stronger.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Equity should be explained clearly</h1>
<p>Many startups mention equity without explaining it.</p>
<p>Candidates deserve to understand:</p>
<ul>
<li>Number of options</li>
<li>Percentage ownership</li>
<li>Current valuation</li>
<li>Strike price</li>
<li>Vesting schedule</li>
<li>Cliff period</li>
<li>Dilution expectations</li>
<li>Exercise window</li>
<li>What happens if they leave</li>
</ul>
<p>Transparency builds trust.</p>
<p>Confusion destroys it.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Raises should have clear reasons</h1>
<p>Raises generally fall into four categories.</p>
<h2>Market adjustment</h2>
<p>The market moved.</p>
<p>Everyone at a level receives an adjustment.</p>
<hr>
<h2>Performance increase</h2>
<p>Outstanding work within the current level.</p>
<p>No promotion required.</p>
<hr>
<h2>Promotion increase</h2>
<p>Moving to the next engineering level.</p>
<p>Usually the largest adjustment.</p>
<hr>
<h2>Retention adjustment</h2>
<p>Rare.</p>
<p>Sometimes necessary.</p>
<p>Shouldn&#39;t become the normal process.</p>
<hr>
<h1>How to avoid salary compression</h1>
<p>Salary compression happens when new hires earn more than existing engineers.</p>
<p>It&#39;s one of the fastest ways to lose experienced employees.</p>
<p>Prevent it by:</p>
<ul>
<li>Reviewing internal salaries before extending offers.</li>
<li>Refreshing bands annually.</li>
<li>Adjusting loyal employees alongside market movement.</li>
<li>Budgeting for compensation corrections.</li>
</ul>
<p>Retention is usually cheaper than replacement.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Build promotion rubrics</h1>
<p>Promotion conversations become dramatically easier when expectations already exist.</p>
<p>For every level define:</p>
<ul>
<li>Technical execution</li>
<li>Code quality</li>
<li>Architecture</li>
<li>Communication</li>
<li>Mentorship</li>
<li>Leadership</li>
<li>Business impact</li>
<li>Reliability</li>
<li>Decision making</li>
<li>Customer understanding</li>
</ul>
<p>Managers should be able to point to observable evidence.</p>
<p>Not opinions.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Managers should never negotiate levels</h1>
<p>One common mistake:</p>
<p>Candidate negotiates from Mid to Senior.</p>
<p>Levels aren&#39;t negotiation tools.</p>
<p>They&#39;re organizational expectations.</p>
<p>If someone genuinely meets Senior expectations...</p>
<p>Hire them as Senior.</p>
<p>If not...</p>
<p>Increase compensation within the appropriate band instead.</p>
<p>Titles should never be bargaining chips.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Refresh compensation annually</h1>
<p>Engineering salaries move.</p>
<p>Your compensation framework should too.</p>
<p>Review:</p>
<ul>
<li>Market data</li>
<li>Inflation</li>
<li>Competitive offers</li>
<li>Retention metrics</li>
<li>Offer acceptance rates</li>
<li>Internal equity</li>
<li>Promotion rates</li>
</ul>
<p>A yearly review keeps the system healthy.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Transparency builds trust</h1>
<p>You don&#39;t necessarily need to publish every employee&#39;s salary.</p>
<p>But engineers should understand:</p>
<ul>
<li>What each level means.</li>
<li>How promotions happen.</li>
<li>How raises work.</li>
<li>What skills they&#39;re expected to develop.</li>
<li>Approximately where compensation bands sit.</li>
</ul>
<p>People are surprisingly accepting of differences in pay when they understand <strong>why</strong> they exist.</p>
<p>They&#39;re far less accepting of mystery.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Common compensation mistakes</h1>
<p>Avoid these if possible:</p>
<ul>
<li>Negotiating every offer independently.</li>
<li>Rewarding tenure instead of impact.</li>
<li>Creating too many levels too early.</li>
<li>Giving promotions to avoid difficult compensation conversations.</li>
<li>Hiding compensation philosophy.</li>
<li>Ignoring market adjustments for years.</li>
<li>Letting new hires dramatically out-earn loyal employees.</li>
<li>Using equity to justify below-market salaries without transparency.</li>
<li>Making counteroffers only after someone resigns.</li>
</ul>
<p>Every one of these creates expensive long-term problems.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Advice for engineers</h1>
<p>Understanding compensation frameworks helps you navigate your own career more effectively.</p>
<p>When interviewing, ask thoughtful questions such as:</p>
<ul>
<li>How are engineering levels defined?</li>
<li>What distinguishes Senior from Staff?</li>
<li>How often are compensation bands reviewed?</li>
<li>How are promotions evaluated?</li>
<li>Are promotions tied to tenure or demonstrated impact?</li>
<li>Does the company publish salary ranges internally?</li>
<li>How often are performance reviews conducted?</li>
<li>Is compensation benchmarked against market data?</li>
</ul>
<p>Companies with clear answers usually have mature engineering organizations.</p>
<p>Companies with vague answers often make compensation decisions reactively.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Advice for founders and engineering leaders</h1>
<p>Your compensation philosophy is part of your employer brand.</p>
<p>The strongest engineering organizations are known for:</p>
<ul>
<li>Fair pay</li>
<li>Transparent promotions</li>
<li>Honest expectations</li>
<li>Consistent leveling</li>
<li>Competitive offers</li>
<li>Respectful negotiations</li>
</ul>
<p>That reputation compounds over time.</p>
<p>Engineers talk to one another.</p>
<p>A company known for fair compensation attracts better candidates with less effort.</p>
<hr>
<h1>The takeaway</h1>
<p>Engineering compensation should never depend on who negotiated hardest or which recruiter happened to write the offer.</p>
<p>A simple leveling framework, transparent salary bands, annual market reviews, and promotion criteria based on demonstrated impact create a hiring system that scales alongside your company. Candidates gain confidence, managers gain consistency, finance gains predictability, and engineers gain a clear understanding of how their careers can grow.</p>
<p>The best compensation systems don&#39;t eliminate difficult conversations—they make them objective, fair, and grounded in shared expectations rather than guesswork.</p>
