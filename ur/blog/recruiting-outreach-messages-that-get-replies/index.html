How to Write Recruiting Outreach That Developers Actually Reply To

How to Write Recruiting Outreach That Developers Actually Reply To

Every experienced developer has an inbox full of recruiting messages that all sound exactly the same.

"I came across your profile..."

"We have an exciting opportunity..."

"We're a fast-growing startup..."

"Do you have 30 minutes this week?"

Most are deleted in seconds.

The problem isn't that developers never change jobs—they do. The problem is that most outreach gives them no reason to spend time learning more. It asks for attention before providing value.

Great recruiting outreach isn't about clever wording or persuasive copywriting. It's about demonstrating that you've done your homework, explaining why the opportunity is genuinely relevant, and making it effortless for someone to continue the conversation.

Whether you're an internal recruiter, hiring manager, founder, or agency recruiter, these principles dramatically improve response rates while building a stronger employer reputation.

Why Most Recruiting Messages Fail

Developers receive dozens—or even hundreds—of recruiting messages every year.

After seeing enough of them, they can identify a mass template within the first sentence.

Typical messages usually contain:

  • Generic compliments
  • No evidence the sender read their profile
  • No salary information
  • No explanation of the product
  • No explanation of the actual work
  • A request for a phone call immediately

Imagine receiving the following:

"Hi Alex,

I was impressed with your profile. We have an exciting opportunity with a rapidly growing company using cutting-edge technologies. Would you have 30 minutes this week?"

Nothing about that message tells the recipient:

  • Why they were contacted
  • Why this role fits them
  • Why this company is different
  • Whether the compensation is competitive
  • Whether it's worth interrupting their day

Deleting it is the rational response.

Remember Who Is Evaluating Whom

Recruiters often think they're evaluating candidates.

Experienced developers know the reverse is equally true.

Every message becomes an early signal about your company.

Developers ask themselves:

  • Does this company understand engineering?
  • Does this recruiter understand what I actually do?
  • Is this role real or was I scraped from LinkedIn?
  • Will interviews be organized?
  • Is this another company hiding compensation?

The outreach itself becomes your first interview.

Research Before You Write

Personalization isn't writing someone's first name.

It's showing that you understand something meaningful about their work.

Good sources include:

  • GitHub repositories
  • Open-source contributions
  • Personal websites
  • Technical blogs
  • Conference talks
  • Engineering podcasts
  • LinkedIn articles
  • Stack Overflow activity
  • Public portfolio projects

You don't need to spend twenty minutes researching someone.

Two or three genuine minutes often produces enough context to write a message that feels human.

What Developers Actually Want to Read

Good outreach answers the questions developers naturally have.

Why me?

Mention something specific.

Examples:

  • a project
  • a library
  • a technical article
  • a conference presentation
  • an architecture discussion
  • a GitHub repository
  • an interesting pull request

Not:

"I love your experience."

Instead:

"I enjoyed reading your post about optimizing PostgreSQL indexes. We solve similar scaling problems."

Specificity earns attention.

What does your company actually build?

Skip marketing language.

Don't write:

"We're disrupting digital transformation."

Write:

"We build scheduling software used by over 12,000 healthcare providers."

Or:

"We process payment transactions for online retailers across North America."

Developers care about real products.

What problem would I solve?

Titles are weak.

Problems are compelling.

Instead of:

Senior Backend Engineer

Write:

You'll lead the redesign of our event ingestion platform processing over 5 billion events each day.

Or:

You'll own our offline synchronization engine for mobile devices used by field technicians.

Interesting work attracts engineers.

What's the technology stack?

Keep it concise.

Examples:

  • Rust
  • Go
  • TypeScript
  • React
  • PostgreSQL
  • Kubernetes
  • AWS

Don't overwhelm the message with twenty technologies.

Three to six is usually enough.

What's the salary?

One of the biggest reasons outreach is ignored is uncertainty.

Developers don't want to spend weeks interviewing only to discover the compensation doesn't align with their expectations.

Whenever possible include:

  • salary range
  • bonus
  • equity
  • benefits
  • remote policy

Transparency builds trust immediately.

A High-Performing Outreach Structure

Keep the message between 120–180 words.

Structure:

  1. Personalized opening
  2. Company description
  3. Role description
  4. Salary range
  5. Simple next step

Example:

Hi Sarah,

I recently read your article about reducing API latency using Rust, especially your section on async request batching.

Our engineering team at Acme is solving very similar performance challenges for a logistics platform processing millions of shipments every day.

We're hiring a Senior Backend Engineer to lead our event ingestion services.

Compensation is $180k–$220k plus equity, fully remote within North America.

If you're open to learning more, I'd be happy to send over the details. No pressure at all if the timing isn't right.

Notice what's missing:

  • exaggerated hype
  • buzzwords
  • pressure
  • unnecessary meetings

Keep It About Them

Most recruiting messages spend 90% of their words talking about the company.

Reverse that.

Instead of:

We're growing...

Try:

Based on your work with distributed systems, I thought this role might genuinely interest you.

Developers care about:

  • interesting problems
  • technical ownership
  • learning opportunities
  • impact
  • engineering quality

Your company is simply the context.

Don't Oversell

Developers are naturally skeptical.

Avoid:

  • revolutionary
  • world-changing
  • unicorn
  • rockstar
  • ninja
  • game-changing
  • disruptive
  • cutting-edge

Replace adjectives with facts.

Instead of:

Amazing engineering culture

Write:

We deploy multiple times per day with automated testing and small pull requests.

Facts are more persuasive than marketing.

Make Saying "Yes" Easy

Don't ask for a meeting immediately.

Lower the commitment.

Better closing questions include:

  • Want me to send more details?
  • Worth a quick look?
  • Would it be helpful if I shared the job description?
  • Interested in learning more?
  • Happy to answer questions if you're curious.

Small asks generate more responses.

Respect Their Time

Developers are busy.

Help them evaluate the opportunity quickly.

Include:

  • remote or onsite
  • salary
  • tech stack
  • product
  • mission
  • location
  • team size (if relevant)

Every unanswered question creates friction.

Reduce friction.

Increase replies.

Common Outreach Mistakes

Avoid these entirely.

Generic compliments

Bad:

I was impressed with your background.

Good:

Your open-source work on Redis clustering caught my attention.

Walls of text

Keep messages readable.

Short paragraphs.

White space.

Developers skim.

Huge requirement lists

The first message isn't the place for twenty bullet points.

Focus on:

  • problem
  • impact
  • opportunity

Immediate meeting requests

Don't ask strangers for thirty minutes.

Earn the conversation.

Hidden salary

This is one of the fastest ways to reduce response rates.

Transparency helps everyone.

Incorrect personalization

Nothing destroys credibility faster than:

  • wrong name
  • wrong company
  • wrong programming language
  • wrong specialty

Double-check everything.

AI Can Help—But Don't Let It Sound Like AI

AI tools can help recruiters:

  • improve grammar
  • shorten messages
  • rewrite paragraphs
  • summarize company information
  • create draft outreach

But never copy and paste AI-generated messages without editing them.

Developers increasingly recognize generic AI writing.

Use AI for:

  • editing
  • proofreading
  • improving clarity

Not for replacing genuine research.

The most effective outreach still includes one or two human-written sentences that reference the candidate's actual work.

Follow Up Professionally

Many developers simply miss the first message.

Following up once is reasonable.

A good follow-up:

  • is brief
  • references the earlier message
  • adds one new piece of information
  • doesn't pressure the recipient

Example:

Hi Sarah,

Just following up in case my previous message got buried.

We've since published a new engineering article explaining the platform I mentioned, so I thought I'd share it.

Happy to send details if you're interested.

That's enough.

If there's no response after a second message, move on respectfully.

Persistence is valuable.

Spam is not.

Build Long-Term Relationships

Not every conversation should end in an immediate hire.

Many outstanding developers respond with:

  • Maybe later.
  • Not this year.
  • Check back in six months.

Treat those responses as successes.

Keep notes.

Reconnect thoughtfully.

The candidate who says "no" today may become your best hire next year.

Great recruiting is relationship building, not transaction processing.

Measure the Right Metrics

Sending more messages isn't success.

Track outcomes instead.

Useful recruiting metrics include:

  • Response rate
  • Positive response rate
  • Qualified conversations
  • Interview conversion rate
  • Offer acceptance rate
  • Time-to-hire
  • Cost-per-hire
  • Candidate satisfaction
  • Referral rate

Small improvements in outreach quality often produce meaningful improvements across the entire hiring funnel.

Recruiting Outreach Checklist

Before pressing Send, ask yourself:

  • ✅ Did I reference something specific about the candidate?
  • ✅ Did I explain what the company actually builds?
  • ✅ Did I describe the real problem they'd solve?
  • ✅ Did I include compensation or a meaningful range?
  • ✅ Is the message under 180 words?
  • ✅ Did I avoid buzzwords and clichés?
  • ✅ Is the next step simple?
  • ✅ Would I reply if I received this message?

If you can't answer "yes" to every question, revise the message first.

Advice for Developers Receiving Outreach

Recruiting outreach isn't only something employers should improve.

Developers can also make themselves easier to discover.

Consider including:

  • Current technology stack
  • Preferred role
  • Remote or relocation preferences
  • Portfolio website
  • GitHub profile
  • Personal projects
  • Conference talks
  • Open-source contributions
  • Salary expectations (if you're comfortable sharing them)

A complete professional profile helps recruiters determine whether an opportunity is genuinely relevant, reducing unwanted messages and improving the quality of the ones you do receive.

The Takeaway

Developers don't ignore recruiting outreach because they're impossible to reach—they ignore it because most messages feel interchangeable.

The best recruiting messages are short, specific, transparent, and respectful. They explain why the recipient was chosen, what the company actually builds, the meaningful problems they'll solve, and the compensation they can expect. They make it easy to say "yes," just as importantly, easy to say "no."

Whether you're hiring one engineer or building an entire engineering organization, better outreach leads to better conversations, stronger employer branding, higher response rates, and ultimately better hires.
