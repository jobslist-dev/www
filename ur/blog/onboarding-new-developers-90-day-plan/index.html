How to Onboard a New Developer: A 90-Day Plan That Sets Them Up to Win

A practical 90-day developer onboarding plan covering preboarding, technical setup, early contributions, growing ownership, feedback, team integration, and long-term success.

The JobsList.dev Team·17 جون، 2026·35 blog.article.minuteRead
onboardinghiringretentiondeveloper-experienceengineering-management

Hiring a strong developer is only the beginning.

The value of that hire depends on what happens next.

A carefully selected engineer can still struggle when they arrive to:

  • a laptop that has not been configured;
  • missing repository access;
  • unclear responsibilities;
  • outdated documentation;
  • contradictory instructions;
  • an overloaded first-week calendar;
  • no assigned mentor;
  • no meaningful work;
  • no feedback;
  • a manager who assumes they will “figure it out.”

These problems are often treated as minor inconveniences.

They are not.

They shape how quickly the developer becomes productive, how confidently they ask questions, how much trust they place in the organization, and whether they begin imagining a future at the company.

A good onboarding program does more than teach someone how to run the application locally.

It helps them understand:

  • what the company is trying to accomplish;
  • how customers use the product;
  • how the engineering system works;
  • how decisions are made;
  • where to find information;
  • who owns what;
  • how quality is evaluated;
  • how to get help;
  • what success looks like;
  • how their work contributes to the larger mission.

The first 90 days should therefore be treated as a deliberate transition from promising candidate to trusted contributor.

This guide provides a practical framework for making that transition successful.

Onboarding begins before the start date

The new developer's experience starts before their first morning.

The period between accepting the offer and joining the company is sometimes treated as administrative downtime. In reality, it is part of onboarding.

The candidate may still be:

  • finishing work at their previous employer;
  • declining other opportunities;
  • wondering whether they made the right decision;
  • preparing for a new routine;
  • arranging equipment or workspace;
  • managing uncertainty about the team.

Thoughtful preboarding reduces that uncertainty.

Keep communication warm after acceptance

Do not allow communication to disappear between the signed offer and the start date.

Useful updates may include:

  • confirmation that the signed documents were received;
  • equipment-shipping information;
  • first-day timing;
  • workplace or remote-access instructions;
  • a brief welcome from the manager;
  • an introduction to the onboarding buddy;
  • a high-level first-week schedule;
  • any optional reading that would provide context.

Keep pre-start material optional unless it is part of paid working time.

A new hire should not be expected to complete unpaid training before joining.

Prepare a preboarding checklist

Assign clear ownership for every item.

Employment and administrative setup

Confirm:

  • signed agreements;
  • payroll information;
  • tax forms;
  • benefits enrollment instructions;
  • emergency contact procedures;
  • background checks where applicable;
  • work authorization documentation;
  • required policy acknowledgments.

Hardware

Prepare:

  • laptop;
  • monitors;
  • keyboard and mouse;
  • headset;
  • security key;
  • mobile device where required;
  • adapters;
  • other role-specific equipment.

The device should arrive early enough for problems to be resolved before the first working session.

Accounts and access

Create or prepare access for:

  • company identity provider;
  • email;
  • messaging;
  • calendar;
  • source repositories;
  • issue tracking;
  • documentation;
  • design tools;
  • cloud environments;
  • observability platforms;
  • password manager;
  • virtual private network;
  • development tools;
  • learning systems;
  • expense and benefits systems.

Use role-based, least-privilege access rather than copying another employee's entire permission set.

Security

Prepare:

  • multifactor authentication;
  • device-management enrollment;
  • secure password guidance;
  • acceptable-use policies;
  • data-handling expectations;
  • incident-reporting instructions;
  • phishing-awareness training;
  • production-access procedures;
  • secret-management practices.

Security onboarding should explain why controls exist, not merely present a list of prohibitions.

First-week plan

Create a written schedule that includes:

  • manager welcome;
  • team introduction;
  • onboarding-buddy meeting;
  • product overview;
  • architecture introduction;
  • security orientation;
  • development-environment setup;
  • first task;
  • protected learning time;
  • end-of-week check-in.

A plan reduces anxiety and prevents the first week from becoming an improvised collection of meetings.

Tell the team who is joining

The existing team should understand:

  • the person's name;
  • their role;
  • their start date;
  • what they will initially work on;
  • who their manager is;
  • who their onboarding buddy is;
  • how teammates can help.

Avoid sharing private personal information without permission.

A brief, respectful introduction helps the team prepare and ensures the new developer is not treated as a surprise arrival.

Assign an onboarding owner

Onboarding frequently fails because responsibility is distributed so widely that nobody owns the whole experience.

The onboarding owner may be:

  • the manager;
  • a dedicated onboarding coordinator;
  • an engineering leader;
  • a designated teammate.

This person should verify that:

  • equipment is ready;
  • access works;
  • meetings are scheduled;
  • the onboarding plan is current;
  • blockers are resolved;
  • feedback is collected;
  • progress is reviewed.

The onboarding owner does not need to teach every topic personally.

They need to ensure that the complete system works.

Assign an onboarding buddy

The manager and onboarding buddy serve different purposes.

The manager is responsible for:

  • expectations;
  • priorities;
  • performance;
  • feedback;
  • career support;
  • organizational context.

The buddy helps with everyday questions such as:

  • Where is this documented?
  • Which channel should I use?
  • Who owns this service?
  • Is this meeting optional?
  • How do we usually review changes?
  • What does this internal term mean?
  • How do I request access?
  • Who should I ask before deploying?

Choose a buddy who:

  • understands the team;
  • communicates patiently;
  • has enough calendar capacity;
  • enjoys helping others;
  • models healthy working habits;
  • will be available during the new hire's first weeks.

Do not assign the nearest available person without considering whether they have time to do the job well.

Build an onboarding document

A useful onboarding guide may include:

  • company mission;
  • product overview;
  • customer groups;
  • team structure;
  • organizational map;
  • development setup;
  • repository map;
  • architecture overview;
  • system ownership;
  • deployment process;
  • testing standards;
  • security practices;
  • observability;
  • incident response;
  • communication norms;
  • meeting guide;
  • glossary;
  • frequently asked questions;
  • useful contacts;
  • first 90-day plan.

The document should help the developer navigate the organization rather than replace human support.

No document can anticipate every question.

Make documentation easy to improve

New employees are uniquely qualified to identify onboarding gaps because they have not yet learned to work around them.

Encourage them to record:

  • missing setup steps;
  • confusing terminology;
  • outdated links;
  • undocumented permissions;
  • contradictory instructions;
  • unclear ownership;
  • assumptions that experienced employees no longer notice.

Allow the new developer to submit documentation improvements during onboarding.

This turns each onboarding cycle into an improvement opportunity for the next one.

Day one: create clarity, safety, and momentum

The first day should help the new developer answer five questions:

  1. Am I expected here?
  2. Do I have what I need?
  3. Who can help me?
  4. What will I be working on?
  5. What should I do next?

It should not attempt to teach the entire company in eight hours.

Begin with a human welcome

The manager should meet with the developer early.

Discuss:

  • why the company is glad they joined;
  • what stood out during the hiring process;
  • the purpose of the role;
  • how the first week will work;
  • who will support them;
  • how to raise concerns or blockers.

A personalized welcome connects the hiring process to the employee experience.

For example:

We were impressed by how you approached the debugging exercise and communicated trade-offs. That kind of production judgment is important for the platform work you will gradually own here.

Specific recognition is more meaningful than a generic welcome message.

Introduce the team with context

Do not simply read names from an organization chart.

Explain:

  • what each person owns;
  • how they will work with the new developer;
  • which subjects they can help with;
  • where important dependencies exist.

For example:

  • Priya owns the frontend platform and design-system integration.
  • Marcus leads the billing services.
  • Elena is the product manager for subscriptions.
  • Devon manages production infrastructure and deployment support.

Context makes names easier to remember.

Explain the company and product

A developer needs business context to make sound technical decisions.

Cover:

  • the company mission;
  • major customer groups;
  • the primary product;
  • how the company makes money;
  • important user workflows;
  • major business risks;
  • current strategic priorities;
  • how engineering contributes.

Avoid presenting only a marketing pitch.

Explain real constraints and current challenges.

Review the onboarding plan

Show the developer:

  • what is planned for the first week;
  • the goals for the first month;
  • the likely responsibilities for days 31–60;
  • the expected outcomes by day 90;
  • how the plan may change;
  • how progress will be discussed.

The plan should create direction without becoming an inflexible contract.

Confirm that the setup works

By the end of the first day, the developer should ideally be able to:

  • access communication tools;
  • access documentation;
  • access the required repositories;
  • authenticate securely;
  • begin configuring the development environment;
  • identify whom to contact for support.

Do not send the new hire from team to team to negotiate every permission independently.

The onboarding owner should coordinate access.

Avoid a full day of presentations

A long sequence of orientation meetings produces fatigue and weak retention.

Balance meetings with:

  • setup time;
  • reading time;
  • breaks;
  • informal conversation;
  • hands-on exploration;
  • a small task.

People learn through use, repetition, and context—not by receiving every available slide deck at once.

Week one: learn the environment and complete a real contribution

The first week should combine orientation with action.

The goal is not maximum output.

The goal is to help the developer experience the complete path from understanding a task to contributing a change.

Select a good first task

A useful first task is:

  • real;
  • small;
  • clearly scoped;
  • low risk;
  • documented enough to begin;
  • connected to the product;
  • reviewable by a teammate;
  • capable of reaching production or a real user.

Examples include:

  • correcting a small bug;
  • improving an error message;
  • adding a missing test;
  • updating documentation;
  • adding a metric;
  • improving a development script;
  • making a small accessibility fix;
  • resolving an internal-tool issue.

Avoid tasks that are:

  • meaningless busywork;
  • blocked by many dependencies;
  • buried in an unfamiliar legacy area;
  • operationally dangerous;
  • expected to take several weeks;
  • chosen solely because nobody else wants them.

The task should teach the workflow.

Use the first contribution to validate the system

The first task can reveal whether the developer can:

  • create a branch;
  • run the application;
  • run tests;
  • access required services;
  • open a pull request;
  • receive review;
  • use the deployment pipeline;
  • observe the result;
  • update documentation.

If any step is unnecessarily difficult, improve the onboarding process.

Pair during the first contribution

Pairing can help explain:

  • repository conventions;
  • local tooling;
  • test strategy;
  • review expectations;
  • architectural boundaries;
  • deployment;
  • terminology.

The experienced teammate should guide without taking over.

The objective is to help the new developer build a mental model.

Explain how code review works

Clarify:

  • who reviews changes;
  • expected response times;
  • required approvals;
  • automated checks;
  • how comments are resolved;
  • when to request synchronous discussion;
  • what counts as blocking;
  • how tone should be interpreted;
  • when changes may be merged.

Without this context, ordinary review comments can feel like unexpected criticism.

Review how software reaches production

Show the path from code to users.

Cover:

  • continuous integration;
  • test environments;
  • deployment approvals;
  • feature flags;
  • monitoring;
  • rollback;
  • incident response;
  • change communication.

Even if the new developer will not deploy immediately, understanding the lifecycle makes the codebase easier to reason about.

Protect time for exploration

Give the developer time to:

  • browse the repository;
  • run the application;
  • trace a request;
  • read architecture notes;
  • inspect logs;
  • review recent pull requests;
  • understand current priorities.

Do not fill every hour with meetings and then expect technical learning to happen after work.

End the first week with a check-in

Discuss:

  • What is clear?
  • What is confusing?
  • Which access is missing?
  • Which documentation was inaccurate?
  • Who has been especially helpful?
  • How did the first task feel?
  • Is the pace too slow, too fast, or appropriate?
  • What should change next week?

This conversation should be practical and psychologically safe.

Days 1–30: learn, connect, and establish early confidence

The first month should help the developer build context and complete several meaningful contributions.

The goal is not full independence.

The goal is informed participation.

First-month outcomes

By the end of day 30, the developer should generally be able to:

  • explain the company's product and primary users;
  • identify the team's major responsibilities;
  • navigate the main codebase;
  • run the relevant systems locally;
  • understand the ordinary development workflow;
  • submit and respond to code reviews;
  • complete small production changes;
  • identify key owners and stakeholders;
  • understand how to ask for help;
  • describe the next stage of their onboarding.

The exact outcomes should reflect the role and seniority.

Build a product mental model

Technical onboarding should include customer and product context.

Useful activities may include:

  • product demonstrations;
  • customer-support shadowing;
  • reviewing common user journeys;
  • reading customer feedback;
  • observing sales or implementation calls;
  • reviewing product analytics;
  • using the product as a customer would.

Developers make better decisions when they understand the people affected by their work.

Introduce the system architecture gradually

Do not try to teach every service in one meeting.

Begin with:

  • the most important user flow;
  • the components the developer will touch;
  • major data boundaries;
  • external dependencies;
  • security boundaries;
  • deployment model;
  • ownership map.

Then deepen the explanation as the developer encounters real work.

Architecture is easier to learn when connected to a concrete task.

Teach where information lives

Explain the purpose of each system.

For example:

  • Documentation: durable knowledge and procedures
  • Issue tracker: planned work and ownership
  • Chat: temporary coordination
  • Architecture records: significant technical decisions
  • Repository: code and local development instructions
  • Status page: customer-visible incidents
  • Observability platform: runtime evidence

New hires waste time when they do not know which source is authoritative.

Introduce communication norms

Clarify expectations around:

  • synchronous versus asynchronous communication;
  • response times;
  • status updates;
  • meeting participation;
  • direct messages versus shared channels;
  • escalation;
  • decision documentation;
  • availability;
  • focus time;
  • working across time zones.

Do not assume these norms are obvious.

Establish recurring one-to-ones

The manager should hold regular one-to-one conversations from the beginning.

Useful first-month topics include:

  • blockers;
  • learning;
  • workload;
  • relationships;
  • role clarity;
  • feedback;
  • concerns;
  • motivation;
  • support needs.

The meeting should not be limited to task status.

Project status can often be communicated elsewhere.

Give early feedback

Do not wait until day 90 to tell the developer how they are doing.

Provide specific feedback such as:

  • Your pull-request summaries make review easier.
  • You are asking useful questions before implementation.
  • Please raise environment blockers earlier rather than waiting until the next meeting.
  • The test coverage was strong, but the operational rollout plan needs more detail.
  • Your customer-support questions helped clarify the issue.

Early feedback helps the developer adjust before habits become established.

Invite feedback on the organization

Ask:

  • Which process is harder than expected?
  • Which documentation is missing?
  • Where did ownership feel unclear?
  • Which tool created unnecessary friction?
  • What surprised you?
  • What should we change for the next new hire?

Do not ask for feedback unless the team is willing to consider it seriously.

Calibrate workload carefully

A new developer may appear highly productive while working unsustainably long hours to make a strong impression.

Managers should reinforce that:

  • questions are expected;
  • learning takes time;
  • normal working hours are sufficient;
  • asking for help is not failure;
  • sustainable progress matters more than performance theater.

The company should not reward hidden overwork during onboarding.

Adapt for senior hires

Senior developers still need onboarding.

Their experience may allow them to learn faster, but it does not provide automatic knowledge of:

  • your customers;
  • your architecture;
  • your internal history;
  • your risk tolerance;
  • your decision process;
  • your organizational relationships.

A senior hire may need less instruction on coding and more context on:

  • technical strategy;
  • business priorities;
  • organizational constraints;
  • architecture history;
  • stakeholder relationships;
  • decision authority.

Do not confuse seniority with mind reading.

Days 31–60: increase ownership and deepen context

The second month should move the developer from guided contribution toward meaningful ownership.

They should begin to operate with less procedural support while still receiving active feedback.

Second-month outcomes

By the end of day 60, the developer may be expected to:

  • own a scoped feature or component;
  • estimate and plan routine work;
  • contribute to design discussions;
  • handle code review confidently;
  • understand the most relevant production signals;
  • collaborate with product and adjacent teams;
  • recognize common risks in the domain;
  • communicate progress and blockers independently;
  • improve at least one part of the development system.

Expectations should be adjusted for level.

Assign a scoped ownership area

A good second-month assignment may be:

  • a complete feature;
  • a service improvement;
  • an internal workflow;
  • a reliability initiative;
  • a documentation overhaul;
  • a testing improvement;
  • a small migration.

The assignment should have:

  • a clear purpose;
  • a known stakeholder;
  • defined boundaries;
  • an opportunity for technical judgment;
  • regular review;
  • measurable completion.

Do not assign ownership without clarifying authority.

The developer should know which decisions they can make independently and which require consultation.

Introduce planning and estimation

Show how the team:

  • breaks down work;
  • identifies dependencies;
  • evaluates risk;
  • estimates effort;
  • negotiates scope;
  • communicates uncertainty;
  • updates plans.

Do not judge the new hire harshly for inaccurate early estimates in an unfamiliar system.

Estimation improves with context.

Introduce production responsibility gradually

Depending on the role, this may include:

  • reading dashboards;
  • reviewing alerts;
  • observing incident response;
  • joining an on-call shadow rotation;
  • reviewing runbooks;
  • participating in deployment;
  • investigating noncritical issues.

Do not place a new hire into unsupported on-call responsibility merely because a calendar threshold has passed.

Production access should follow demonstrated readiness.

Deepen business context

Arrange conversations with:

  • product managers;
  • designers;
  • customer support;
  • sales;
  • security;
  • operations;
  • finance;
  • legal or compliance where relevant.

The purpose is not to schedule a tour of every department.

Focus on the relationships that affect the developer's work.

Encourage cross-functional questions

A developer should understand:

  • how requirements are created;
  • how customer feedback reaches the team;
  • how priorities are selected;
  • how incidents affect customers;
  • how technical work is evaluated;
  • how business trade-offs are made.

This context helps them make better independent decisions.

Ask the developer to improve something

By the second month, ask the new hire to identify one meaningful improvement.

Examples include:

  • correcting onboarding documentation;
  • simplifying a development command;
  • improving a test fixture;
  • adding missing monitoring;
  • clarifying ownership;
  • reducing a repeated manual step;
  • documenting a common incident.

This creates visible ownership and captures the value of a fresh perspective.

Review progress around day 45

Discuss:

  • progress against the plan;
  • confidence;
  • technical context;
  • relationships;
  • blockers;
  • feedback from both sides;
  • whether goals remain appropriate;
  • what ownership should increase next.

Do not treat the review as a hidden performance test.

The developer should already know how the manager perceives their progress.

Days 61–90: operate independently and prepare for long-term impact

The final month of the initial onboarding period should help the developer become a trusted contributor with clear next-stage goals.

“Fully onboarded” does not mean they understand every system or no longer need help.

It means they can contribute effectively within their scope and know how to navigate what they do not know.

Third-month outcomes

By day 90, a developer may be expected to:

  • deliver scoped work with appropriate independence;
  • navigate relevant systems confidently;
  • understand team priorities;
  • make sound routine technical decisions;
  • participate meaningfully in planning and review;
  • operate or support relevant production systems safely;
  • communicate risks and blockers early;
  • collaborate effectively across roles;
  • understand their performance expectations;
  • have a clear plan for the next three to six months.

Expand independent delivery

The developer should begin owning work end to end:

  • understanding the requirement;
  • clarifying ambiguity;
  • proposing an approach;
  • implementing;
  • testing;
  • coordinating review;
  • planning release;
  • observing the result;
  • documenting changes.

The manager should remain available without controlling every step.

Evaluate judgment, not just output

By this stage, look for whether the developer:

  • asks useful questions;
  • identifies risk;
  • chooses appropriate complexity;
  • tests meaningful behavior;
  • communicates trade-offs;
  • responds to feedback;
  • understands customer impact;
  • follows through after release.

A large number of completed tickets does not automatically mean successful onboarding.

Introduce longer-term responsibilities

Begin discussing:

  • system ownership;
  • roadmap involvement;
  • technical debt;
  • mentoring;
  • architecture;
  • production support;
  • skill development;
  • cross-team contribution.

The exact responsibilities should match the role's level.

Create a post-onboarding development plan

The 90-day point should lead into the next stage, not end support abruptly.

Set goals for the next period.

Examples include:

  • own a service;
  • lead a feature;
  • improve one reliability measure;
  • deepen knowledge of a domain;
  • mentor an intern;
  • contribute to architecture;
  • reduce deployment friction;
  • improve accessibility;
  • complete an agreed learning objective.

Goals should connect employee growth with team outcomes.

The 90-day review

The 90-day review should summarize learning, recognize progress, address gaps, and align on the future.

There should be no major surprise.

Review the original plan

Discuss each expected outcome:

  • completed;
  • progressing;
  • changed;
  • blocked;
  • no longer relevant.

The plan may have changed because:

  • priorities moved;
  • access was delayed;
  • the role evolved;
  • the developer learned faster or slower in certain areas;
  • the team discovered a better assignment.

Evaluate progress in context.

Ask the developer to reflect

Useful questions include:

  • What are you most proud of?
  • What do you understand well now?
  • Where do you still need context?
  • Which relationships have been most helpful?
  • What has been harder than expected?
  • Which process should the team improve?
  • What work would you like to own next?
  • What support would make you more effective?
  • Does the role match what was described during hiring?

This last question is especially important.

A mismatch between the promised role and actual work can quickly damage trust.

Provide evidence-based feedback

Discuss specific behaviors and outcomes.

Weak:

  • You are doing great.
  • You need to be more senior.
  • You should show more initiative.

Stronger:

  • You independently delivered the notification-preference feature and coordinated the rollout with support.
  • Your technical plans are clear, but risks should be raised earlier when another service is involved.
  • You improved the setup guide after identifying three missing steps.
  • You are contributing well in code review; the next growth area is taking clearer ownership of deployment verification.

Specific feedback creates action.

Confirm role expectations

Review:

  • scope;
  • level;
  • ownership;
  • communication;
  • quality;
  • production responsibility;
  • collaboration;
  • growth.

The developer should know how success will be evaluated beyond onboarding.

Celebrate progress

Recognition does not need to be elaborate.

Acknowledge:

  • first production contribution;
  • first completed feature;
  • documentation improvements;
  • useful questions;
  • customer impact;
  • team support;
  • successful ownership.

Celebrate progress without creating pressure for performative heroics.

A role-specific onboarding plan

A single onboarding template should not be applied identically to every developer.

Adjust the program for role, discipline, and level.

Frontend developer

Early learning may include:

  • product journeys;
  • design system;
  • component architecture;
  • accessibility;
  • browser support;
  • state management;
  • frontend testing;
  • performance monitoring;
  • collaboration with design.

A useful early task might be a small accessibility or user-interface improvement.

Backend developer

Early learning may include:

  • service architecture;
  • APIs;
  • data models;
  • queues;
  • failure handling;
  • authorization;
  • testing;
  • deployment;
  • observability;
  • incident procedures.

A useful early task might involve a small endpoint, validation improvement, or missing metric.

Platform or infrastructure developer

Early learning may include:

  • environments;
  • deployment architecture;
  • identity and access;
  • networking;
  • observability;
  • infrastructure as code;
  • cost;
  • incident response;
  • disaster recovery.

Production access should be introduced carefully.

Mobile developer

Early learning may include:

  • application architecture;
  • platform release process;
  • device support;
  • offline behavior;
  • analytics;
  • crash reporting;
  • accessibility;
  • store submission;
  • feature flags.

A small bug fix that passes through the full release pipeline may be a strong first contribution.

Data engineer

Early learning may include:

  • data sources;
  • schemas;
  • pipelines;
  • orchestration;
  • quality checks;
  • lineage;
  • access governance;
  • reporting consumers;
  • incident response.

The first task might improve a test, lineage record, or small pipeline component.

Security engineer

Early learning may include:

  • threat model;
  • product architecture;
  • incident process;
  • vulnerability handling;
  • identity;
  • data classification;
  • secure-development standards;
  • risk ownership;
  • disclosure procedures.

Security onboarding should clarify authority, escalation, and executive support.

Engineering manager

A manager's onboarding should include:

  • team history;
  • employee goals;
  • delivery commitments;
  • technical risks;
  • stakeholder relationships;
  • hiring plans;
  • performance processes;
  • organizational expectations;
  • management culture.

Do not evaluate a new manager only through immediate delivery metrics.

They need time to understand people and systems.

Staff or principal engineer

A senior individual contributor needs broad organizational context.

Focus on:

  • strategy;
  • architecture across teams;
  • decision history;
  • technical risk;
  • team boundaries;
  • stakeholder relationships;
  • influence mechanisms;
  • long-term initiatives.

Avoid forcing them to propose major changes before they understand why the current system exists.

Remote developer onboarding

Remote onboarding requires additional intentionality because the developer cannot absorb context through physical proximity.

Ship equipment early

Confirm:

  • delivery;
  • device condition;
  • required accessories;
  • connectivity;
  • return procedures;
  • support contact.

Do not schedule critical setup sessions before confirming the hardware arrived.

Document asynchronous workflows

Remote developers need clear information about:

  • communication channels;
  • response expectations;
  • working hours;
  • overlap requirements;
  • meeting norms;
  • decision records;
  • focus time;
  • escalation;
  • availability indicators.

Create planned social contact

Remote employees may need structured opportunities to build relationships.

Useful activities include:

  • buddy conversations;
  • small-group introductions;
  • optional social sessions;
  • pair programming;
  • team demonstrations;
  • cross-functional meetings;
  • informal manager check-ins.

Avoid filling the calendar with forced social events.

Connection should support work and belonging, not become another performance requirement.

Avoid remote invisibility

Managers should not infer that silence means progress or that frequent messages mean productivity.

Use:

  • clear goals;
  • written updates;
  • regular one-to-ones;
  • visible blockers;
  • documented decisions;
  • outcome-based evaluation.

Remote employees should not need to demonstrate constant online presence to prove they are working.

Record important sessions where appropriate

Recorded architecture or product walkthroughs can help with:

  • time-zone differences;
  • repetition;
  • review;
  • future onboarding.

Provide captions or transcripts where possible.

Do not record sensitive conversations automatically.

Hybrid and on-site onboarding

Hybrid onboarding can fail when the new hire comes to the office but the people they need are remote.

Coordinate attendance intentionally.

For important early sessions, confirm that:

  • the right teammates are available;
  • meeting rooms work;
  • remote participants can join equally;
  • the new hire knows which days are most useful on-site;
  • travel or commuting expectations are clear.

Avoid giving office-based employees access to more information simply because they overhear conversations.

Document important decisions for everyone.

International onboarding

International hires may require additional support around:

  • payroll;
  • benefits;
  • local holidays;
  • equipment shipping;
  • time zones;
  • currency;
  • tax documentation;
  • immigration;
  • employment agreements;
  • data access;
  • travel;
  • communication norms.

Do not assume policies designed for one country transfer cleanly to another.

Explain:

  • who handles local employment questions;
  • how leave works;
  • which holidays apply;
  • when compensation is paid;
  • how expenses are reimbursed;
  • which working hours overlap;
  • how support is requested.

Accessibility and inclusive onboarding

Onboarding should work for developers with different needs, communication styles, and circumstances.

Possible accommodations may include:

  • captioning;
  • screen-reader-compatible documents;
  • additional breaks;
  • alternative meeting formats;
  • written instructions;
  • flexible scheduling;
  • assistive technology;
  • adjusted lighting or workspace;
  • reduced sensory load;
  • ergonomic equipment.

Make the accommodation process visible and private.

Do not require the developer to repeatedly explain the same need to every teammate.

Avoid information delivery in only one format

Use a combination of:

  • written guides;
  • live demonstrations;
  • recorded walkthroughs;
  • diagrams;
  • hands-on exercises;
  • pair sessions;
  • reference documents.

People process information differently.

Multiple formats also make onboarding more resilient.

Explain unwritten cultural rules

Employees from different professional or cultural backgrounds may interpret workplace behavior differently.

Clarify:

  • how disagreement works;
  • when directness is expected;
  • how decisions are challenged;
  • how meeting participation works;
  • how feedback is delivered;
  • how urgency is communicated;
  • which social activities are optional;
  • how status and hierarchy operate.

Do not penalize someone for failing to infer norms the company never explained.

Psychological safety during onboarding

New developers need to feel safe admitting what they do not know.

A team can encourage this by:

  • welcoming questions;
  • acknowledging that documentation is incomplete;
  • admitting historical decisions were imperfect;
  • explaining acronyms;
  • rewarding early risk identification;
  • responding constructively to mistakes;
  • avoiding ridicule;
  • distinguishing learning errors from negligence.

Managers should model questions themselves.

For example:

I do not know the answer to that. Let us find the owner and document it.

This demonstrates that uncertainty is normal.

Common onboarding mistakes

Waiting until day one to prepare

The new hire spends valuable time without equipment, accounts, or direction.

Treating setup as the employee's problem

The company owns the system. The employee should not have to navigate organizational dysfunction alone.

Scheduling too many introductory meetings

The new hire receives names and slides but retains little context.

Giving no real work

The developer feels unnecessary and cannot validate the workflow.

Assigning a critical project immediately

The person is expected to make high-risk decisions before understanding the system.

Using outdated documentation

The developer learns to distrust official information.

Failing to assign a buddy

Routine questions become harder to ask and take longer to resolve.

Assuming senior hires need no onboarding

Experience does not provide company-specific context.

Providing no feedback

The developer has to guess whether expectations are being met.

Waiting 90 days to discuss problems

Correctable issues become established patterns.

Evaluating activity rather than progress

Meeting attendance, message volume, and visible busyness are mistaken for contribution.

Treating questions as weakness

The new hire learns to hide uncertainty.

Overloading the developer with access

Security and cognitive load both suffer.

Giving access too slowly

The person cannot perform expected work.

Failing to explain the business

Technical decisions remain disconnected from customer and company outcomes.

Ignoring belonging

A technically productive person may still feel isolated and leave.

Ending support abruptly after 90 days

Onboarding becomes a temporary program rather than a transition into ongoing management.

Measuring onboarding success

Measurement should help improve the system, not create pressure for the new hire to reach arbitrary velocity.

Useful measures may include:

  • time to working development environment;
  • time to first reviewed change;
  • time to first production contribution;
  • access-related delays;
  • documentation gaps;
  • onboarding-task completion;
  • manager check-in completion;
  • buddy engagement;
  • role clarity;
  • developer confidence;
  • early retention;
  • satisfaction with support;
  • readiness for expected responsibilities.

Time to first contribution

This can help identify process friction.

However, it should not become a competition between new hires.

Different roles and assignments require different ramp periods.

Access delay

Track how many working hours or days are lost to:

  • missing permissions;
  • hardware problems;
  • account creation;
  • security approvals;
  • environment setup.

This is a direct measure of onboarding-system quality.

Developer confidence

Ask the developer to rate or discuss confidence in areas such as:

  • understanding the product;
  • navigating the codebase;
  • knowing where to find information;
  • asking for help;
  • understanding expectations;
  • contributing to production;
  • collaborating with the team.

Confidence does not replace performance evidence, but it can reveal hidden uncertainty.

Role clarity

Ask whether the developer understands:

  • why the role exists;
  • what they own;
  • what success means;
  • what priorities matter;
  • who makes decisions;
  • how performance is evaluated.

Low role clarity should trigger management action.

Onboarding feedback

Collect feedback after:

  • the first week;
  • day 30;
  • day 60;
  • day 90.

Questions may include:

  • What helped most?
  • What slowed you down?
  • Which information arrived too early?
  • Which information arrived too late?
  • Which meeting was unnecessary?
  • Which document was missing?
  • Did the role match what was described?
  • What should change before the next person joins?

Early retention

A departure during the first months may reveal problems with:

  • role accuracy;
  • management;
  • onboarding;
  • compensation;
  • culture;
  • workload;
  • expectations;
  • hiring.

Do not assume every departure was caused by onboarding, but investigate respectfully.

A reusable 90-day onboarding template

Before day one

Company responsibilities

  • Complete employment setup.
  • Ship and configure equipment.
  • Create required accounts.
  • Prepare security access.
  • Publish the first-week schedule.
  • Assign the onboarding owner.
  • Assign the buddy.
  • Select the first task.
  • Notify the team.
  • Confirm manager availability.

New-hire communication

  • Confirm start date and time.
  • Explain first-day access.
  • Share equipment status.
  • Introduce the manager and buddy.
  • Provide optional context.
  • Explain who to contact with questions.

Day one

  • Manager welcome
  • Team introductions
  • Company and product overview
  • Role mission
  • 90-day plan
  • Security setup
  • Tool access
  • Development-environment start
  • Buddy check-in
  • End-of-day support check

Week one

  • Complete core setup.
  • Review the primary product flow.
  • Explore the relevant architecture.
  • Begin a small real task.
  • Open the first pull request.
  • Learn code-review expectations.
  • Review deployment and observability.
  • Record documentation gaps.
  • Hold the first manager one-to-one.
  • Conduct the end-of-week review.

Days 8–30

  • Complete several scoped contributions.
  • Learn relevant customer workflows.
  • Meet key stakeholders.
  • Deepen codebase understanding.
  • Participate in planning and review.
  • Receive early feedback.
  • Improve onboarding documentation.
  • Define second-month ownership.
  • Complete a 30-day review.

Days 31–60

  • Own a feature or technical area.
  • Plan and communicate routine work.
  • Join relevant production workflows.
  • Collaborate across functions.
  • Improve one team process or technical asset.
  • Deepen product and business knowledge.
  • Review progress around day 45.
  • Define third-month goals.
  • Complete a 60-day review.

Days 61–90

  • Deliver independently within the expected scope.
  • Participate meaningfully in planning.
  • Demonstrate sound routine judgment.
  • Support relevant production responsibilities.
  • Identify longer-term opportunities.
  • Establish post-onboarding development goals.
  • Complete the 90-day reflection.
  • Hold the formal 90-day review.
  • Celebrate progress.

A manager checklist

Before the start date:

  • I understand why the role exists.
  • I have defined 30-, 60-, and 90-day outcomes.
  • I have chosen an appropriate first task.
  • I have reserved time for onboarding.
  • I have assigned a buddy.
  • I have confirmed equipment and access.
  • I have told the team who is joining.
  • I have prepared role-specific context.

During the first month:

  • I meet with the developer regularly.
  • I provide specific feedback.
  • I remove blockers quickly.
  • I check whether the pace is appropriate.
  • I explain business context.
  • I verify that questions are welcomed.
  • I ask for feedback on onboarding.

During months two and three:

  • I increase ownership deliberately.
  • I avoid withdrawing support too quickly.
  • I clarify decision authority.
  • I connect the developer with relevant stakeholders.
  • I discuss long-term growth.
  • I ensure the role matches what was promised.
  • I document the next-stage plan.

An onboarding-buddy checklist

The buddy should:

  • introduce themselves before or on day one;
  • explain how they can help;
  • meet regularly during the first weeks;
  • answer routine questions without judgment;
  • introduce useful teammates;
  • explain communication norms;
  • review the first task where appropriate;
  • surface recurring blockers;
  • share useful documentation;
  • tell the onboarding owner when a systemic problem requires action.

The buddy should not become responsible for:

  • performance management;
  • all technical training;
  • resolving every access problem;
  • replacing the manager;
  • being available at all hours.

A new-developer checklist

During onboarding, the new developer can:

  • review the 90-day plan;
  • ask when expectations are unclear;
  • record missing or outdated documentation;
  • meet regularly with the buddy and manager;
  • request context before making high-risk changes;
  • communicate blockers early;
  • verify assumptions;
  • ask how decisions are documented;
  • seek feedback;
  • reflect on progress;
  • identify areas they would like to own;
  • explain any accommodation or support needs through the appropriate channel.

This checklist should guide participation, not shift responsibility away from the company.

A practical 30-, 60-, and 90-day scorecard

Day 30: foundation

Product

  • Understands the main product and user journey.
  • Can explain how the team contributes.

Technical

  • Can run the relevant system.
  • Can navigate the primary repository.
  • Has completed small contributions.
  • Understands the development workflow.

Team

  • Knows key collaborators.
  • Participates in routine communication.
  • Knows where and how to ask for help.

Manager support

  • Has received specific feedback.
  • Has clear second-month goals.
  • Has no unresolved critical access blocker.

Day 60: ownership

Product

  • Understands the business context of assigned work.
  • Can identify relevant stakeholders.

Technical

  • Owns a scoped feature or component.
  • Participates meaningfully in design and review.
  • Understands relevant operational signals.

Team

  • Communicates progress and blockers.
  • Collaborates across roles.
  • Contributes to team improvement.

Manager support

  • Has received updated feedback.
  • Understands the expected level of independence.
  • Has clear third-month goals.

Day 90: contribution

Product

  • Connects technical decisions to customer or business outcomes.

Technical

  • Delivers expected work with appropriate independence.
  • Makes sound routine decisions.
  • Understands relevant production responsibilities.

Team

  • Is a trusted collaborator.
  • Participates in planning and feedback.
  • Knows how to navigate uncertainty.

Growth

  • Understands performance expectations.
  • Has a development plan.
  • Has identified future ownership or learning goals.

Improving the onboarding system over time

Onboarding should be treated like a product.

It has:

  • users;
  • workflows;
  • friction;
  • defects;
  • documentation;
  • outcomes;
  • feedback.

After every onboarding cycle, review:

  • Which access was late?
  • Which instructions were wrong?
  • Which meeting added little value?
  • Which task was too large or too small?
  • Which explanation arrived at the wrong time?
  • Where did the developer depend on tribal knowledge?
  • Which person became an accidental bottleneck?
  • Which part should be automated?
  • What should be added to the template?
  • What should be removed?

Assign owners and deadlines to improvements.

Do not collect the same feedback from every new hire without fixing the underlying problem.

The takeaway

Developer onboarding is not a week of setup followed by an expectation of immediate productivity.

It is a structured process that helps a new teammate move through four stages:

  1. Prepared arrival
  2. Safe learning
  3. Guided contribution
  4. Increasing ownership

A strong 90-day onboarding program:

  • begins before day one;
  • prepares equipment and access;
  • gives the new hire a clear written plan;
  • assigns a manager and buddy;
  • explains the product and business;
  • connects architecture to real work;
  • creates an early meaningful contribution;
  • introduces context gradually;
  • provides regular feedback;
  • increases ownership deliberately;
  • supports accessibility and different working styles;
  • measures friction without turning onboarding into a race;
  • ends with clear long-term expectations and growth goals.

The goal is not to make a developer fully understand an entire company within 90 days.

The goal is to help them become a confident, informed, supported contributor who understands how to keep learning and where they can create lasting value.

Great recruiting gets someone to accept the offer.

Great onboarding helps them become successful after they arrive.
