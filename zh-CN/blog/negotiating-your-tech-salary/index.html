How to Negotiate Your Tech Salary (Without Losing the Offer)

A practical, low-stress guide to negotiating a technology job offer — including what to research, what to say, how to evaluate equity, and what to ask for beyond base salary.

The JobsList.dev Team·2026年2月26日·18 blog.article.minuteRead
job-seekersnegotiationcareersalarytech-jobs

You received the offer. Congratulations.

Now comes the part many candidates find more stressful than the interviews: deciding whether to negotiate.

A good negotiation does not need to be confrontational, manipulative, or drawn out. It can be a short, professional conversation about aligning the offer with the role, your experience, the market, and the value you expect to contribute.

Handled well, that conversation can improve your base salary, signing bonus, equity, paid time off, remote-work terms, title, or future compensation review. Those gains can compound across future raises and job changes.

The goal is not to extract every possible dollar from the company. The goal is to reach an agreement that both sides can feel good about before the employment relationship begins.

Companies usually expect some negotiation

Many employers anticipate that candidates will ask questions or request adjustments after receiving an offer. Compensation teams may have a target number, an approved range, and some discretion within that range.

A respectful request does not normally communicate disinterest. It often demonstrates that you:

  • understand the market;
  • take your career seriously;
  • can advocate for yourself;
  • communicate professionally;
  • evaluate opportunities thoughtfully.

Negotiations become risky when candidates use threats, dishonest claims, unexplained demands, or unrealistic ultimatums.

Compare these two approaches:

“I need another $20,000 or I'm walking.”

and:

“I'm genuinely excited about the role. Based on the scope, my experience, and the market for comparable positions, I was hoping we could discuss a base salary closer to $165,000. Is there flexibility there?”

The second approach is clear without being aggressive. It gives the employer a concrete request and room to respond.

Do not negotiate before you understand the opportunity

You need more than a job title before you can evaluate compensation properly.

A “Senior Software Engineer” may be expected to:

  • own a narrow application area;
  • lead major architectural decisions;
  • mentor several engineers;
  • participate in an on-call rotation;
  • manage production incidents;
  • work across multiple teams;
  • support customer-facing implementations;
  • influence hiring and technical strategy.

Those are very different jobs despite sharing the same title.

Before discussing final compensation, clarify:

  • the level and reporting structure;
  • expected ownership;
  • team size and seniority;
  • management or mentoring responsibilities;
  • on-call expectations;
  • travel requirements;
  • remote, hybrid, or office requirements;
  • performance expectations;
  • promotion criteria;
  • the company's stage and financial condition;
  • whether the role is newly created or a replacement.

The more responsibility the company expects you to carry, the stronger your basis for requesting compensation that reflects it.

Research the market before choosing a number

Do not build your request around your current salary or what you personally hope to earn. Build it around the market value of the role.

Useful inputs include:

  • the salary range published with the job;
  • compensation data for similar roles and levels;
  • offers reported by trusted peers;
  • recruiters who understand the relevant market;
  • location-specific salary information;
  • remote-work compensation policies;
  • company stage and funding;
  • industry;
  • company size;
  • required technical specialization;
  • management responsibilities;
  • scarcity of the skill set.

A backend engineer working on internal business systems may be compensated differently from a backend engineer building high-scale financial infrastructure, even when both use similar languages.

Specialized experience may increase your leverage. Examples include:

  • distributed systems;
  • security engineering;
  • machine learning infrastructure;
  • database internals;
  • low-latency systems;
  • cloud platform engineering;
  • regulated healthcare or financial systems;
  • large-scale migrations;
  • engineering leadership;
  • difficult-to-hire language or platform expertise.

Use several sources rather than relying on a single salary website. Compensation data may be outdated, self-reported, based on small samples, or inconsistent about whether it includes bonuses and equity.

Define your three numbers

Before the offer conversation, determine three compensation points.

1. Your walk-away number

This is the minimum total package you would realistically accept.

It should account for:

  • your current compensation;
  • benefits you would lose;
  • commuting or relocation costs;
  • increased workload;
  • reduced flexibility;
  • job stability;
  • equity risk;
  • health insurance costs;
  • family considerations;
  • other opportunities available to you.

Do not reveal this number casually. It is primarily a decision tool for you.

2. Your target

This is the package you believe fairly reflects the role and would make you comfortable accepting.

Your target should be supported by evidence, not simply ambition.

3. Your opening request

This should be above your target but still credible and defensible.

You need room for the company to move without your final agreement falling below what you actually want.

For example:

  • walk-away base: $145,000;
  • target base: $160,000;
  • opening request: $168,000.

The right spacing depends on the published range, the company, the role, and your negotiating leverage. An opening request far above the approved range may make you appear disconnected from the market.

Evaluate total compensation, not only base salary

A technology offer may include several components:

  • base salary;
  • annual cash bonus;
  • commission;
  • signing bonus;
  • equity;
  • retirement contributions;
  • health benefits;
  • paid time off;
  • remote-work support;
  • learning budget;
  • relocation assistance;
  • severance protection;
  • paid parental leave;
  • equipment allowance.

A higher base salary is usually more valuable than an equivalent one-time bonus because future raises, bonuses, and job offers may be influenced by your recurring salary. However, a signing bonus can still be useful when the company cannot move the base.

Create a simple first-year and recurring-compensation comparison.

Example

Offer A

  • Base: $155,000
  • Annual bonus target: 10%
  • Signing bonus: $10,000
  • Estimated annual equity value: $20,000

Approximate first-year compensation:

$155,000 + $15,500 + $10,000 + $20,000 = $200,500

Approximate recurring annual compensation before future equity grants:

$155,000 + $15,500 + $20,000 = $190,500

Do not treat uncertain components as guaranteed. A target bonus may not be paid in full, and private-company equity may ultimately be worth nothing.

Let the company reveal its range when possible

Candidates are often asked about salary expectations early in the process.

You can respond without committing prematurely:

“I'd like to understand the level, scope, and total package before settling on a number. What compensation range has been approved for the role?”

Another option:

“I'm flexible depending on the responsibilities and overall package. Could you share the budgeted range so I can confirm we're aligned?”

If the employer has already published a range, ask how leveling affects placement within it:

“I saw the posted base range of $145,000 to $180,000. How does the company determine where a candidate falls within that range?”

If you must provide a number, give a researched range rather than an unsupported minimum:

“Based on comparable senior platform roles and the scope we've discussed, I'm targeting a base in the $165,000 to $180,000 range, depending on equity, bonus, and the rest of the package.”

Be careful with wide ranges. Employers may focus on the bottom. Do not state a lower bound you would be unhappy to accept.

Wait for the written offer

Your strongest negotiating position is usually after the company has decided it wants to hire you but before you accept.

Ask for the complete offer in writing, including:

  • title;
  • level;
  • base salary;
  • bonus structure;
  • equity details;
  • benefits;
  • start date;
  • work location expectations;
  • contingencies;
  • expiration date.

A verbal summary is useful, but you need the full package before evaluating it.

Do not resign from your current job based only on a verbal offer.

Ask for time to review

You do not need to respond immediately.

A professional response might be:

“Thank you. I'm excited about the opportunity and appreciate the offer. I'd like a little time to review the full package carefully. Could I get back to you by Thursday?”

A few business days is commonly reasonable, although timelines vary. If the company requires a faster decision, ask whether the deadline can be extended.

Avoid using delay as a tactic without purpose. Decide what information you need, evaluate the offer, and respond promptly.

Build a business case for your request

The strongest negotiation is based on evidence and relevance.

Your justification may include:

  • experience directly related to the company's needs;
  • leadership responsibilities;
  • a history of delivering measurable results;
  • specialized technical expertise;
  • the scope of the proposed role;
  • market compensation for the level;
  • competing offers;
  • compensation you are giving up;
  • unusually strong alignment with difficult requirements.

Use outcomes where possible.

Instead of:

“I'm a very experienced engineer.”

say:

“In my current role, I led the migration of a high-traffic service that reduced infrastructure costs by 28% and improved deployment reliability. Because this position includes similar platform ownership, I was hoping we could move the base closer to $170,000.”

Your previous salary is generally a weaker argument than the market value of the new role. The company is hiring you for what you can contribute next, not merely compensating you based on what another employer paid.

Make one clear, prioritized request

Do not send a list of ten unrelated demands.

Identify the most important one or two changes and communicate them clearly.

A strong script:

“I'm excited about the team and would like to make this work. After reviewing the offer and the scope of the role, I was hoping for a base salary of $170,000. That reflects the platform ownership, on-call responsibility, and my experience leading similar systems. Is there flexibility to move the base closer to that number?”

Then stop.

You do not need to fill the silence. Give the recruiter or hiring manager time to respond.

Use collaborative language

Useful phrases include:

  • “Is there flexibility on the base?”
  • “Could we explore a signing bonus?”
  • “What options do we have to close the gap?”
  • “Would the team consider additional equity?”
  • “Could we document an earlier compensation review?”
  • “How much flexibility is available within the approved level?”
  • “What would be easiest for the company to adjust?”

This frames the negotiation as a problem both sides can solve.

Negotiate more than salary

When base pay cannot move, other terms may still be flexible.

Signing bonus

A signing bonus can help compensate for:

  • a forfeited annual bonus;
  • unvested equity;
  • relocation costs;
  • a base salary gap;
  • a delayed start date;
  • benefits lost during a transition.

You might say:

“If the base is fixed, would the company consider a $15,000 signing bonus to help bridge the difference?”

Check whether the bonus must be repaid if you leave within a certain period.

Equity

Equity can be meaningful, but it requires careful evaluation.

For a public company, ask about:

  • grant value;
  • number of shares or units;
  • vesting schedule;
  • grant date;
  • refresh grants;
  • stock-price assumptions.

For a private company, ask about:

  • number of options or shares;
  • percentage ownership on a fully diluted basis;
  • strike price;
  • latest preferred share price;
  • latest valuation;
  • vesting schedule;
  • exercise window after leaving;
  • dilution;
  • liquidity prospects;
  • whether the company offers tender programs.

Do not evaluate private-company equity only by multiplying the option count by the latest preferred share price. Taxes, strike price, dilution, liquidation preferences, and the absence of a liquidity event can dramatically change the outcome.

Treat equity as a potentially valuable but uncertain part of compensation—not guaranteed cash.

Bonus

Ask:

  • Is the bonus guaranteed or discretionary?
  • What is the target percentage?
  • What was the historical payout?
  • Is it based on personal, team, or company performance?
  • Are new employees prorated?
  • When is it paid?
  • Must you still be employed on the payout date?

Paid time off

You may be able to negotiate:

  • additional vacation days;
  • credit for prior experience;
  • an already-planned trip;
  • an earlier eligibility date.

Get exceptions in writing.

Remote and hybrid terms

Clarify:

  • required office days;
  • whether the policy is contractual or discretionary;
  • approved work location;
  • restrictions on working from another state or country;
  • travel expectations;
  • equipment or coworking reimbursement;
  • whether compensation changes if you relocate.

A verbal promise of flexibility may not survive a management or policy change. Ask for important arrangements to be documented.

Title and level

Title can affect:

  • internal scope;
  • future promotion;
  • equity bands;
  • bonus targets;
  • your next job search.

Do not chase a title with no accompanying authority or compensation. Ask how the company defines the level and what expectations come with it.

Start date

A later start date may help you:

  • receive a pending bonus;
  • vest additional equity;
  • complete a project;
  • relocate;
  • take time off between jobs.

Earlier performance review

If the company says it cannot meet your target because it needs to see you perform first, ask for a written review milestone:

“Would the company be willing to schedule a formal compensation review after six months, based on agreed performance goals?”

Try to define:

  • the review date;
  • who conducts it;
  • measurable goals;
  • what adjustment becomes possible.

A vague promise to “revisit later” is not the same as a written review commitment.

Severance

For executive, high-risk, or startup roles, you may be able to discuss severance terms. This may be especially relevant if you are leaving a stable position for a company with uncertain funding.

Consider your leverage honestly

Leverage may come from:

  • another written offer;
  • rare or specialized expertise;
  • a difficult-to-fill role;
  • strong interview performance;
  • direct experience with the company's problem;
  • the urgency of the hire;
  • willingness to walk away;
  • low personal pressure to accept quickly.

Leverage does not require aggression. It means you have credible alternatives or unusually strong alignment.

Do not invent competing offers. A bluff can damage trust and may collapse if the company asks for a decision timeline.

If you have a real competing offer, you can say:

“I want to be transparent that I'm considering another offer with a decision deadline on Friday. JobsList.dev remains especially compelling because of the team and product scope. Is there any possibility of revisiting the base before then?”

You usually do not need to disclose the other company's name.

Decide whether to negotiate by email or phone

Both can work.

Email advantages

  • You can choose your words carefully.
  • The request is documented.
  • You avoid reacting emotionally.
  • The recruiter can forward the request internally.

Phone advantages

  • Tone is easier to communicate.
  • Questions can be resolved quickly.
  • The conversation may feel more collaborative.
  • You can learn what is actually flexible.

A useful approach is to schedule a call, discuss the request, and then confirm the agreed changes by email.

Sample negotiation email

Subject: JobsList.dev offer

Hi [Name],

Thank you again for the offer. I'm genuinely excited about the opportunity to join the team and contribute to [specific product, mission, or challenge].

After reviewing the scope of the role and the compensation package, I'd like to discuss the base salary. Based on the ownership expected in this position, my experience with [relevant expertise], and compensation for comparable roles, I was targeting a base salary closer to $170,000.

Is there flexibility to move the base toward that level?

I remain very enthusiastic about the role and appreciate your consideration.

Best,
[Your Name]

Sample phone script

“Thank you again for the offer. I'm excited about the position and the team. I reviewed the package carefully, and I'd like to discuss the base salary. Given the scope of ownership and my experience with large-scale platform systems, I was targeting $170,000. Is there flexibility to move closer to that number?”

If the recruiter says no:

“I understand. Is the base completely fixed, or is there any room elsewhere in the package, such as a signing bonus, additional equity, or an earlier compensation review?”

What to do when they ask, “Will you accept if we meet that number?”

Do not make a promise unless it is true.

A safe response:

“That adjustment would resolve the main concern I have with the package. I would still want to review the updated written offer, but I expect it would put us in a strong position to finalize.”

If you know you will accept:

“Yes. If the base is adjusted to $170,000 and the rest of the terms remain the same, I would be prepared to accept after reviewing the updated written offer.”

What if the company says the offer is final?

A final offer is not automatically a bad offer.

Evaluate it against:

  • your walk-away number;
  • the role's quality;
  • growth potential;
  • team and manager;
  • company stability;
  • work-life balance;
  • learning opportunities;
  • your alternatives;
  • total compensation.

You can accept graciously:

“Thank you for checking. I understand the constraints, and I appreciate the transparency. I'm happy to move forward with the offer as presented.”

Or decline professionally:

“Thank you for the offer and for the time the team invested in the process. After careful consideration, I've decided not to move forward because the package does not align with what I would need to make the transition. I have a great deal of respect for the team and appreciate the opportunity.”

Do not continue pushing after the employer has clearly reached its limit unless new information materially changes the discussion.

Warning signs during negotiation

Pay attention to how the company behaves.

Potential concerns include:

  • pressure to accept immediately;
  • refusal to provide the complete offer in writing;
  • unexplained changes to previously discussed terms;
  • hostility toward reasonable questions;
  • vague or inconsistent equity information;
  • promises that cannot be documented;
  • a title that does not match the responsibilities;
  • unclear bonus rules;
  • resistance to clarifying remote-work expectations;
  • requests to resign before contingencies are complete.

The negotiation process can reveal how the company communicates and handles power differences.

Mistakes to avoid

Apologizing for negotiating

You are not doing something wrong.

Avoid opening with:

“I'm sorry to ask, and I know this is probably unreasonable…”

Be polite, but do not undermine your request before making it.

Giving an ultimatum you do not mean

Never threaten to walk away unless you are genuinely prepared to do so.

Making an emotional argument

Your rent, debt, family expenses, or desired lifestyle may matter greatly to you, but they do not establish the market value of the role.

Focus on scope, experience, market data, and the value you can contribute.

Negotiating every detail

Too many requests can dilute your strongest priorities. Focus on the changes that matter most.

Accepting verbally before negotiating

Once you say you accept, reopening compensation can damage trust. Ask questions and negotiate before formally accepting.

Using fake market data

Use credible evidence. Do not quote suspicious salary figures merely because they support your preferred number.

Ignoring taxes and repayment terms

Signing bonuses, equity exercises, relocation assistance, and other benefits may have tax or repayment implications. Review the relevant documents and seek professional advice where needed.

Resigning too early

Wait until:

  • you have the final written offer;
  • you have accepted it;
  • required checks or contingencies are sufficiently resolved;
  • the start date is confirmed.

Review the final written offer carefully

Before accepting, confirm that the document reflects every agreed change.

Check:

  • legal employer name;
  • title and level;
  • manager or reporting line;
  • base salary;
  • bonus target;
  • signing bonus;
  • equity grant;
  • vesting schedule;
  • start date;
  • work location;
  • remote arrangement;
  • PTO;
  • benefits eligibility;
  • contingencies;
  • repayment clauses;
  • review commitments;
  • offer expiration.

If something is missing, ask for clarification before signing.

A simple decision framework

Score the opportunity across several categories:

Category Questions to ask
Compensation Is the recurring package competitive?
Role scope Will I own meaningful work?
Manager Will this person help me grow?
Team Are expectations and collaboration healthy?
Product Do I believe in what the company is building?
Stability Is the organization financially and operationally sound?
Flexibility Does the working model fit my life?
Growth Will this improve my skills and future opportunities?
Risk What could realistically go wrong?
Alternatives Is this better than staying or choosing another offer?

Do not let a small salary difference distract you from serious concerns about leadership, stability, workload, or role quality. Likewise, do not undervalue a meaningful recurring compensation gap merely because you like the team.

The takeaway

A strong salary negotiation is usually calm, specific, evidence-based, and brief.

The basic process is:

  1. Understand the role and level.
  2. Research the market.
  3. Define your walk-away number, target, and opening request.
  4. Review the complete written package.
  5. Express enthusiasm.
  6. Ask clearly for the most important adjustment.
  7. Negotiate other components if base salary is constrained.
  8. Confirm every agreed change in writing.
  9. Accept or decline professionally.

You do not need to become a hard-charging negotiator. You need to know what the opportunity is worth, communicate your request clearly, and remain willing to evaluate the answer honestly.

One professional conversation can improve not only your first-year earnings, but also future raises, bonus calculations, retirement contributions, and the baseline used in your next career move.
