3:T1271,<p>Ad-hoc compensation is one of the quietest ways a growing company creates future pain. Pay people whatever it takes to close each offer, and within a year you have engineers doing identical work for wildly different salaries, no answer to &quot;how do I get a raise?&quot;, and a pay-equity problem waiting to surface. Levels and bands solve this. You don&#39;t need a big HR function to build a simple, defensible system — here&#39;s the pragmatic version.</p>
<h2>What leveling actually is</h2>
<p>A <strong>level</strong> describes the scope and impact expected of an engineer, independent of years served. A typical early ladder:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>L1 — Junior:</strong> executes well-defined tasks with guidance; growing.</li>
<li><strong>L2 — Mid:</strong> owns features end-to-end with little oversight.</li>
<li><strong>L3 — Senior:</strong> owns ambiguous problems and systems; multiplies others.</li>
<li><strong>L4 — Staff:</strong> drives impact across teams; sets technical direction.</li>
</ul>
<p>Keep it to a handful of levels early on. Over-engineering the ladder before you have 30 engineers creates more politics than clarity.</p>
<h2>Define levels by scope, not tenure</h2>
<p>The most important discipline: levels are about <strong>what you can own and the impact you have</strong>, not how long you&#39;ve been coding. A brilliant three-year engineer can be Senior; a coasting ten-year one may be Mid. Write a short paragraph per level covering scope of work, autonomy, and influence on others. Concrete, observable behaviors — &quot;resolves ambiguity without being handed a spec&quot; — beat vague adjectives like &quot;expert.&quot;</p>
<h2>Attach a pay band to each level</h2>
<p>A <strong>band</strong> is a salary range for a level — say, a floor, midpoint, and ceiling. Set them using real market data for your role, location strategy, and stage:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Floor:</strong> someone new to the level.</li>
<li><strong>Midpoint:</strong> solidly performing at the level.</li>
<li><strong>Ceiling:</strong> top of the level; sustained excellence, or ready to be promoted.</li>
</ul>
<p>Bands overlap between levels on purpose — a top-of-band Mid may out-earn a new Senior, which is fine. When someone consistently earns their ceiling and operates a level up, that&#39;s the signal to promote, not to keep stretching the band.</p>
<h2>Decide your market position</h2>
<p>Pick a deliberate posture and apply it consistently: are you targeting the 50th percentile of market, the 75th, top of market? There&#39;s no single right answer — a well-funded startup competing for scarce talent may aim high; a bootstrapped company may pay mid-market and compete on ownership and flexibility. What matters is that the choice is intentional and the bands reflect it, rather than each offer being a one-off improvisation.</p>
<h2>Refresh the data regularly</h2>
<p>Markets move. Bands set two years ago are probably below market now, which quietly turns into attrition and lowball offers that don&#39;t close. Revisit bands at least annually against fresh data, and adjust. Budget for it — falling behind market is more expensive than staying current once you count the cost of backfilling people who left.</p>
<h2>How this makes hiring easier</h2>
<p>With levels and bands in place, hiring gets dramatically calmer:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Offers are fast and consistent</strong> — you know the band before you extend.</li>
<li><strong>Negotiation is bounded and honest:</strong> &quot;this role is Senior, here&#39;s the band, here&#39;s where your offer sits and why.&quot; Candidates respect transparency.</li>
<li><strong>You avoid the compression trap</strong> where new hires out-earn loyal existing engineers doing the same work.</li>
<li><strong>Internal equity is defensible</strong> if anyone asks why two people are paid differently.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Be transparent internally</h2>
<p>You don&#39;t have to publish every salary, but engineers should know the ladder exists, roughly what each level means, and how to move up. Opaque comp breeds resentment and rumor; a visible framework — &quot;here&#39;s what Senior looks like and how you get there&quot; — turns comp from a source of anxiety into a growth conversation.</p>
<h2>Start simple, iterate</h2>
<p>Four levels, a band per level anchored to market data, a clear market posture, and an annual refresh will serve a company well past its first hundred engineers. Resist the urge to build a sprawling matrix on day one. The goal isn&#39;t a perfect system — it&#39;s a fair, fast, defensible one that replaces &quot;whatever it took to close the offer&quot; with something you can stand behind.</p>
