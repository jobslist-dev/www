e:Ted7e,<p>Your first engineering hire is not simply the first person who writes code for your startup.</p>
<p>They may influence:</p>
<ul>
<li>what gets built;</li>
<li>how quickly it ships;</li>
<li>which technical risks you accept;</li>
<li>how customer feedback becomes product decisions;</li>
<li>how future engineers are interviewed;</li>
<li>how incidents are handled;</li>
<li>how technical debt is managed;</li>
<li>whether your company can scale beyond one developer;</li>
<li>what kind of engineering culture forms around the product.</li>
</ul>
<p>A strong first engineer can become a force multiplier. They turn incomplete ideas into working software, identify risks the founder cannot see, establish sensible technical practices, and help recruit the people who follow.</p>
<p>A poor fit can create the opposite outcome.</p>
<p>You may spend months building the wrong product, accumulate architecture that cannot support the business, depend on undocumented knowledge held by one person, or lose runway replacing work that should never have been built that way.</p>
<p>The stakes are high, but the answer is not to create a six-week interview process or search endlessly for a mythical “10x engineer.”</p>
<p>The answer is to define the problem clearly, understand which kind of engineer you need, evaluate candidates with relevant evidence, communicate honestly, and create an environment in which the person can succeed.</p>
<p>This guide covers the complete process, including how to hire responsibly when you are not a technical founder.</p>
<h1>Why the first engineering hire is different</h1>
<p>Later engineering hires usually join an existing structure.</p>
<p>They may inherit:</p>
<ul>
<li>a codebase;</li>
<li>a development workflow;</li>
<li>architecture decisions;</li>
<li>a product roadmap;</li>
<li>technical leadership;</li>
<li>deployment systems;</li>
<li>coding standards;</li>
<li>team norms;</li>
<li>an established interview process.</li>
</ul>
<p>Your first engineer may inherit almost none of those things.</p>
<p>They may need to create the structure while simultaneously shipping the product.</p>
<p>That means the role often includes responsibilities that would be divided among several people at a larger company:</p>
<ul>
<li>product engineering;</li>
<li>architecture;</li>
<li>infrastructure;</li>
<li>deployment;</li>
<li>testing;</li>
<li>security basics;</li>
<li>technical planning;</li>
<li>customer support;</li>
<li>vendor evaluation;</li>
<li>documentation;</li>
<li>hiring preparation.</li>
</ul>
<p>This is why hiring a strong specialist with an impressive résumé can still fail.</p>
<p>The person may be excellent at their specialty but poorly suited to the breadth, ambiguity, and commercial proximity of an early-stage company.</p>
<h1>Start with the business problem, not the job title</h1>
<p>“We need a developer” is not a hiring plan.</p>
<p>Before writing a job description, define what must become true during the next 6 to 12 months.</p>
<p>Examples:</p>
<ul>
<li>Launch the first usable version of the product.</li>
<li>Replace a fragile prototype with production-ready foundations.</li>
<li>Improve reliability before onboarding larger customers.</li>
<li>Build the core technical capability that differentiates the company.</li>
<li>Move development away from an unreliable agency.</li>
<li>Establish a repeatable product-development process.</li>
<li>Prepare the platform for a second and third engineering hire.</li>
<li>Integrate with a regulated or technically complex external system.</li>
<li>Validate whether the product can be built economically.</li>
</ul>
<p>Your hiring decision should follow from the outcome.</p>
<p>A useful starting statement is:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>We need someone who can take ownership of [specific product or technical problem] and produce [specific business outcome] within approximately [time period].</p>
</blockquote>
<p>For example:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>We need someone who can take ownership of our customer-facing web application, replace the prototype with a reliable first production version, and establish a development foundation that two more engineers can join next year.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>That is more useful than:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>We need a full-stack rockstar.</p>
</blockquote>
<h1>Decide whether you need an employee at all</h1>
<p>A full-time engineering hire is not always the right next step.</p>
<p>Before recruiting, compare the available options.</p>
<h1>Full-time engineer</h1>
<p>A full-time hire may be appropriate when:</p>
<ul>
<li>software is central to the company;</li>
<li>the work is ongoing;</li>
<li>product knowledge will compound over time;</li>
<li>technical decisions affect the company&#39;s long-term value;</li>
<li>you expect to build an engineering team;</li>
<li>the role requires continuous collaboration with customers and founders.</li>
</ul>
<p>The advantage is durable ownership.</p>
<p>The cost is salary, equity, recruiting time, management responsibility, and the risk of hiring before the product direction is clear.</p>
<h1>Contractor</h1>
<p>A contractor may be appropriate when:</p>
<ul>
<li>the work is clearly defined;</li>
<li>the project has a finite scope;</li>
<li>you need short-term specialist expertise;</li>
<li>you are still testing whether a permanent role exists;</li>
<li>speed matters more than building internal capability immediately.</li>
</ul>
<p>A contractor is not automatically cheaper.</p>
<p>A strong contractor may charge significantly more per hour than an employee, but you avoid some long-term commitments.</p>
<p>The key question is whether you need a deliverable or an enduring owner.</p>
<h1>Agency</h1>
<p>An agency may help when you need:</p>
<ul>
<li>a small multidisciplinary team;</li>
<li>design and engineering together;</li>
<li>a defined launch;</li>
<li>immediate capacity;</li>
<li>structured project management.</li>
</ul>
<p>Agencies can also create dependency when:</p>
<ul>
<li>the code is poorly documented;</li>
<li>ownership is unclear;</li>
<li>the agency controls infrastructure or accounts;</li>
<li>knowledge remains outside the company;</li>
<li>changes require expensive new statements of work.</li>
</ul>
<p>When using an agency, make sure your company owns:</p>
<ul>
<li>source-code repositories;</li>
<li>cloud accounts;</li>
<li>domains;</li>
<li>deployment credentials;</li>
<li>documentation;</li>
<li>data;</li>
<li>intellectual property;</li>
<li>design files;</li>
<li>vendor accounts.</li>
</ul>
<h1>Technical co-founder</h1>
<p>A technical co-founder is not simply an engineer paid mostly in equity.</p>
<p>A co-founder relationship generally implies:</p>
<ul>
<li>shared company-building responsibility;</li>
<li>meaningful ownership;</li>
<li>long-term commitment;</li>
<li>influence over strategy;</li>
<li>acceptance of founder-level risk;</li>
<li>involvement beyond assigned engineering work.</li>
</ul>
<p>Do not use the title “co-founder” merely because you cannot afford market compensation.</p>
<p>That creates confusion about authority, ownership, and expectations.</p>
<h1>No-code or low-code solution</h1>
<p>You may not need a custom system yet.</p>
<p>A no-code or low-code approach can be appropriate when:</p>
<ul>
<li>you are testing demand;</li>
<li>workflows are still changing rapidly;</li>
<li>manual operations remain manageable;</li>
<li>custom technology is not yet the main differentiator;</li>
<li>the immediate goal is learning rather than scale.</li>
</ul>
<p>Do not hire an engineer to automate a product nobody wants.</p>
<p>First confirm that the next technical investment supports a meaningful business question.</p>
<h1>The four common first-engineer profiles</h1>
<p>Most early-stage companies need one of four broad profiles.</p>
<p>They are not interchangeable.</p>
<h1>1. The generalist product builder</h1>
<p>This person can work across the product stack and turn incomplete requirements into usable software.</p>
<p>They are usually strongest at:</p>
<ul>
<li>shipping customer-facing features;</li>
<li>moving between frontend and backend work;</li>
<li>making pragmatic architecture decisions;</li>
<li>working directly with founders and users;</li>
<li>handling ambiguity;</li>
<li>choosing simple tools;</li>
<li>identifying what does not need to be built yet.</li>
</ul>
<p>This is often the right choice for a pre-product-market-fit software company.</p>
<p>A strong generalist is not someone who knows every technology.</p>
<p>They are someone who can own the complete path from problem to production without becoming paralyzed by missing structure.</p>
<h1>2. The technical specialist</h1>
<p>This person is hired because the company&#39;s main risk depends on a specific technical capability.</p>
<p>Examples include:</p>
<ul>
<li>machine-learning systems;</li>
<li>video processing;</li>
<li>computer vision;</li>
<li>security;</li>
<li>cryptography;</li>
<li>robotics;</li>
<li>payments;</li>
<li>low-latency trading;</li>
<li>geospatial systems;</li>
<li>embedded software;</li>
<li>medical-device integration;</li>
<li>large-scale data infrastructure.</li>
</ul>
<p>A specialist is appropriate when the specialized problem is central to the product&#39;s value.</p>
<p>Do not hire a specialist simply because the technology sounds impressive.</p>
<p>A deep machine-learning engineer is not the right first hire when the real problem is building a basic workflow and talking to customers.</p>
<h1>3. The founding technical lead</h1>
<p>This person combines hands-on engineering with the ability to establish technical direction and help build the team.</p>
<p>They may be responsible for:</p>
<ul>
<li>architecture;</li>
<li>delivery;</li>
<li>hiring;</li>
<li>engineering standards;</li>
<li>vendor decisions;</li>
<li>security foundations;</li>
<li>future team structure;</li>
<li>technical communication with investors or customers.</li>
</ul>
<p>This profile is appropriate when:</p>
<ul>
<li>the company is funded;</li>
<li>the product has meaningful scope;</li>
<li>several engineering hires are expected soon;</li>
<li>technical decisions have long-term consequences;</li>
<li>the role must grow into leadership.</li>
</ul>
<p>Be clear about whether this is an individual-contributor role, a management role, or a temporary combination.</p>
<p>“Founding engineer” often hides conflicting expectations.</p>
<h1>4. The contractor or fractional technical leader</h1>
<p>This person provides temporary capability while the company defines its longer-term needs.</p>
<p>A fractional technical leader may help with:</p>
<ul>
<li>architecture review;</li>
<li>hiring;</li>
<li>agency oversight;</li>
<li>security planning;</li>
<li>vendor selection;</li>
<li>technical due diligence;</li>
<li>interviewing the first employee.</li>
</ul>
<p>This can be valuable for a non-technical founder who needs informed oversight before committing to a permanent hire.</p>
<h1>Write a first-engineer scorecard</h1>
<p>Before reviewing candidates, define how you will evaluate them.</p>
<p>A scorecard prevents the role from changing every time you meet someone impressive.</p>
<p>Use five to seven attributes tied to the actual work.</p>
<p>Example:</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Attribute</th>
<th>What good evidence looks like</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody><tr>
<td>Product delivery</td>
<td>Has shipped usable software from incomplete requirements</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Technical judgment</td>
<td>Chooses proportionate solutions and explains trade-offs</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Ownership</td>
<td>Takes responsibility from problem definition through production</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Communication</td>
<td>Makes technical decisions understandable to non-engineers</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Customer orientation</td>
<td>Wants to understand users and business outcomes</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Breadth</td>
<td>Can work across the areas required by the first year</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Team-building potential</td>
<td>Documents, collaborates, and can help future engineers succeed</td>
</tr>
</tbody></table>
<p>For each attribute, define what would count as:</p>
<ul>
<li>strong evidence;</li>
<li>acceptable evidence;</li>
<li>insufficient evidence.</li>
</ul>
<p>This creates a more consistent process than deciding whether someone “feels like a startup person.”</p>
<h1>Define the role in writing</h1>
<p>A serious candidate will want to know what they are joining.</p>
<p>Your job description should explain:</p>
<ul>
<li>what the company does;</li>
<li>who the users are;</li>
<li>current product status;</li>
<li>existing technology;</li>
<li>current traction;</li>
<li>funding or profitability context;</li>
<li>runway where appropriate;</li>
<li>the role&#39;s primary outcome;</li>
<li>what the first six months should accomplish;</li>
<li>whether the engineer will work alone;</li>
<li>who owns product decisions;</li>
<li>which technical decisions they can make;</li>
<li>how much customer contact the role includes;</li>
<li>expected hiring growth;</li>
<li>compensation and equity;</li>
<li>work location and schedule;</li>
<li>interview process.</li>
</ul>
<h1>A stronger opening</h1>
<p>Weak:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>We are looking for a passionate full-stack developer to join our fast-paced startup and help disrupt an exciting industry.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Stronger:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>We are building scheduling and payment software for independent physical-therapy clinics. The current product is a working prototype used by 14 clinics, and our next goal is to turn it into a reliable product we can support across the first 100 customers. We are hiring our first full-time engineer to own that transition across the web application, API, data model, deployment process, and technical foundations for future hires.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The second version helps candidates evaluate the actual opportunity.</p>
<h1>State the honest context</h1>
<p>Your strongest candidates will discover the risks eventually.</p>
<p>Tell them early.</p>
<p>Examples:</p>
<ul>
<li>The prototype was built by an agency and has limited automated testing.</li>
<li>The founder currently manages product requirements through direct customer conversations.</li>
<li>The company has 14 months of runway at current spending.</li>
<li>The engineer will initially be the only full-time technical employee.</li>
<li>Some requirements are still being validated.</li>
<li>The product needs reliability work before it needs sophisticated scale.</li>
<li>The current system contains technical debt.</li>
<li>The company does not yet have formal engineering processes.</li>
</ul>
<p>Honesty will reduce the number of applicants who want a more established environment.</p>
<p>That is useful.</p>
<p>Your goal is not the largest applicant pool. It is alignment.</p>
<h1>Be specific about authority</h1>
<p>Accountability without authority is a common reason founding engineers leave.</p>
<p>Clarify which decisions the engineer will own.</p>
<p>Examples:</p>
<ul>
<li>framework and architecture choices;</li>
<li>cloud infrastructure;</li>
<li>technical vendors;</li>
<li>release process;</li>
<li>engineering workflow;</li>
<li>code quality standards;</li>
<li>security priorities;</li>
<li>technical hiring recommendations.</li>
</ul>
<p>Also clarify which decisions remain with the founder:</p>
<ul>
<li>company strategy;</li>
<li>budget;</li>
<li>product positioning;</li>
<li>pricing;</li>
<li>customer commitments;</li>
<li>hiring approvals.</li>
</ul>
<p>Some decisions should be shared.</p>
<p>Write down how disagreements will be resolved.</p>
<h1>Decide how technical the founder will be</h1>
<p>A non-technical founder does not need to become a software engineer.</p>
<p>They do need enough understanding to ask useful questions and manage the business consequences of technical decisions.</p>
<p>You should be able to discuss:</p>
<ul>
<li>what problem is being solved;</li>
<li>why a solution was chosen;</li>
<li>major alternatives;</li>
<li>approximate cost;</li>
<li>delivery risk;</li>
<li>security implications;</li>
<li>dependencies;</li>
<li>what could go wrong;</li>
<li>what will be measured;</li>
<li>whether the decision is reversible.</li>
</ul>
<p>A strong first engineer should make those subjects understandable without using confusion as a form of authority.</p>
<h1>Compensation: pay for the role you actually need</h1>
<p>Underpaying the first engineer is often a false economy.</p>
<p>The company saves money on salary while increasing the risk of:</p>
<ul>
<li>poor architecture;</li>
<li>slow delivery;</li>
<li>security mistakes;</li>
<li>weak documentation;</li>
<li>failed hiring;</li>
<li>expensive rework;</li>
<li>early departure;</li>
<li>contractor dependency.</li>
</ul>
<p>You do not need to outbid the largest technology companies.</p>
<p>You do need to offer a package that makes sense for the responsibility and risk.</p>
<h1>Publish a salary range</h1>
<p>A transparent range helps candidates decide whether the opportunity is viable before investing hours in the process.</p>
<p>Include:</p>
<ul>
<li>base salary range;</li>
<li>currency;</li>
<li>bonus, if any;</li>
<li>equity range or approach;</li>
<li>benefits;</li>
<li>location adjustments;</li>
<li>employment type;</li>
<li>on-call expectations;</li>
<li>expected working hours.</li>
</ul>
<p>Avoid:</p>
<ul>
<li>“competitive salary”;</li>
<li>enormous ranges spanning several levels;</li>
<li>a maximum you would never approve;</li>
<li>presenting speculative equity as guaranteed wealth.</li>
</ul>
<h1>Understand the equity conversation</h1>
<p>Equity matters, especially for a first engineer, but it should be explained honestly.</p>
<p>Candidates may ask:</p>
<ul>
<li>What percentage of the company does this represent?</li>
<li>Is the percentage fully diluted?</li>
<li>What is the vesting schedule?</li>
<li>Is there a one-year cliff?</li>
<li>What is the current strike price?</li>
<li>What was the most recent valuation?</li>
<li>What type of equity is offered?</li>
<li>What happens if I leave?</li>
<li>How long is the exercise window?</li>
<li>How much dilution should I expect?</li>
<li>Has an option pool already been created?</li>
<li>Are there acceleration provisions?</li>
<li>What happens during an acquisition?</li>
</ul>
<p>Do not present only the number of shares.</p>
<p>“50,000 options” is meaningless without the total capitalization.</p>
<p>Use percentage context where permitted and appropriate.</p>
<h1>Equity is not cash</h1>
<p>Equity may become valuable.</p>
<p>It may also become worth nothing.</p>
<p>Present it as risk-bearing ownership, not delayed salary.</p>
<p>A candidate who needs stable income should not be pressured to accept a package they cannot sustain.</p>
<p>The strongest arrangement is one both sides can live with for several years.</p>
<h1>Think about internal equity early</h1>
<p>Even when the first engineer is the only engineer, the offer establishes a precedent.</p>
<p>Future hires may compare:</p>
<ul>
<li>salary;</li>
<li>title;</li>
<li>equity;</li>
<li>authority;</li>
<li>promotion expectations.</li>
</ul>
<p>Document why the package is appropriate.</p>
<p>This will make later compensation decisions easier.</p>
<h1>Where to find the first engineer</h1>
<p>The best channel depends on the role, but founder involvement matters.</p>
<p>Candidates are more likely to respond when the founder explains:</p>
<ul>
<li>the real problem;</li>
<li>why it matters;</li>
<li>what has already been learned;</li>
<li>what ownership the engineer will receive;</li>
<li>why their background appears relevant.</li>
</ul>
<h1>Your extended network</h1>
<p>Ask for introductions from:</p>
<ul>
<li>founders;</li>
<li>former coworkers;</li>
<li>customers;</li>
<li>investors;</li>
<li>advisors;</li>
<li>designers;</li>
<li>product leaders;</li>
<li>experienced engineers;</li>
<li>industry communities.</li>
</ul>
<p>Make the request specific.</p>
<p>Weak:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Do you know any developers?</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Stronger:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>We are looking for a senior product-minded engineer who has shipped B2B web applications, is comfortable owning both TypeScript frontend and backend work, and wants to join as the first technical employee. Do you know someone who fits that profile or someone who might introduce us?</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Specific requests produce better referrals.</p>
<h1>Developer-focused job boards</h1>
<p>A technical job board can help you reach people actively evaluating engineering roles.</p>
<p>Your post still needs to compete on clarity.</p>
<p>Lead with:</p>
<ul>
<li>salary;</li>
<li>ownership;</li>
<li>product problem;</li>
<li>current traction;</li>
<li>honest risk;</li>
<li>interview process.</li>
</ul>
<p>Do not rely on the startup label alone.</p>
<h1>Technical and industry communities</h1>
<p>Look in communities related to:</p>
<ul>
<li>your technology;</li>
<li>your customer domain;</li>
<li>open-source projects you use;</li>
<li>startup engineering;</li>
<li>local developer groups;</li>
<li>professional associations;</li>
<li>technical newsletters.</li>
</ul>
<p>Do not join a community and immediately spam a job link.</p>
<p>Participate, learn, and approach people respectfully.</p>
<h1>Your existing users</h1>
<p>Users sometimes reveal strong technical talent by:</p>
<ul>
<li>reporting detailed bugs;</li>
<li>suggesting integrations;</li>
<li>building unofficial tools;</li>
<li>contributing documentation;</li>
<li>discussing your product intelligently.</li>
</ul>
<p>A user who already understands the problem may become a strong candidate.</p>
<p>Do not assume every engaged user wants a job. Ask respectfully.</p>
<h1>Contractors and agency engineers</h1>
<p>Someone who has already contributed to the product may be a possible hire when:</p>
<ul>
<li>their contract permits it;</li>
<li>the agency relationship is respected;</li>
<li>ownership and compensation are appropriate;</li>
<li>they genuinely want the startup environment.</li>
</ul>
<p>Review contractual restrictions before recruiting an agency employee directly.</p>
<h1>Open-source contributors</h1>
<p>Contributors can demonstrate:</p>
<ul>
<li>technical ability;</li>
<li>communication;</li>
<li>follow-through;</li>
<li>collaboration;</li>
<li>work in unfamiliar codebases.</li>
</ul>
<p>Look for meaningful contribution, not merely famous repository names.</p>
<h1>Founder-written outreach</h1>
<p>A concise founder message can outperform generic recruiter outreach.</p>
<p>Example:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Hi [Name] — I found your work on [specific project or experience], especially [specific detail].</p>
<p>We are building [plain description] for [users]. We currently have [traction or product state], and we are hiring our first full-time engineer to own [specific problem].</p>
<p>The role is hands-on across [scope], with meaningful authority over the technical foundation and future team. The base range is [range], plus [equity summary], and the role is [location arrangement].</p>
<p>Your experience with [relevant detail] looked unusually close to what we need. Would you be open to seeing the full role description?</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The message is specific, transparent, and easy to answer.</p>
<h1>How candidates should present themselves for a first-engineer role</h1>
<p>Founders are often looking for evidence that differs from a standard corporate application.</p>
<p>Candidates can strengthen their résumé and profile by showing:</p>
<ul>
<li>complete products shipped;</li>
<li>direct ownership;</li>
<li>work under ambiguity;</li>
<li>customer collaboration;</li>
<li>pragmatic technical decisions;</li>
<li>production operations;</li>
<li>measurable business impact;</li>
<li>ability to work across boundaries;</li>
<li>mentoring or hiring contributions;</li>
<li>clear written communication.</li>
</ul>
<h1>Resume signals founders should look for</h1>
<p>Strong evidence may include bullets such as:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Built and launched the company&#39;s first self-service onboarding flow across React, Node.js, and PostgreSQL, reducing manual setup from three days to under one hour.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote>
<p>Replaced a fragile agency-built deployment process with automated previews, production checks, and rollback support.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote>
<p>Interviewed 12 customers, translated recurring workflow problems into product requirements, and shipped the first version within six weeks.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote>
<p>Took ownership of a payment-integration failure, restored service, reimbursed affected customers, and implemented idempotency controls to prevent recurrence.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>These bullets show ownership and consequences.</p>
<h1>Weak résumé signals</h1>
<p>Be cautious with applications containing only:</p>
<ul>
<li>technology inventories;</li>
<li>vague responsibilities;</li>
<li>team accomplishments with no personal contribution;</li>
<li>inflated leadership language;</li>
<li>impressive architecture without business context;</li>
<li>projects that were never shipped;</li>
<li>no evidence of production responsibility.</li>
</ul>
<p>This does not mean rejecting the candidate immediately.</p>
<p>It means asking for clarification.</p>
<h1>Evaluate what they built, not only where they worked</h1>
<p>A candidate from a famous company may have worked within a narrow area supported by large teams.</p>
<p>A candidate from a small company may have:</p>
<ul>
<li>owned the product;</li>
<li>handled customers;</li>
<li>managed deployment;</li>
<li>fixed incidents;</li>
<li>made architecture decisions;</li>
<li>trained new hires.</li>
</ul>
<p>Neither background is automatically better.</p>
<p>Evaluate the work relevant to your environment.</p>
<h1>Assessing candidates without a technical co-founder</h1>
<p>A non-technical founder should not pretend to evaluate technical depth alone.</p>
<p>Bring in qualified help.</p>
<p>Possible evaluators include:</p>
<ul>
<li>a trusted engineering advisor;</li>
<li>a fractional CTO;</li>
<li>an experienced founder;</li>
<li>a senior engineer from your network;</li>
<li>a technical investor;</li>
<li>a paid independent consultant.</li>
</ul>
<p>The evaluator should be:</p>
<ul>
<li>independent;</li>
<li>experienced in the relevant area;</li>
<li>able to explain their judgment;</li>
<li>free from conflicts of interest;</li>
<li>evaluating against your role rather than their personal preferences.</li>
</ul>
<h1>Do not outsource the entire decision</h1>
<p>A technical advisor can assess:</p>
<ul>
<li>engineering fundamentals;</li>
<li>code quality;</li>
<li>architecture judgment;</li>
<li>experience depth;</li>
<li>security awareness;</li>
<li>technical communication.</li>
</ul>
<p>The founder still needs to assess:</p>
<ul>
<li>mission alignment;</li>
<li>customer curiosity;</li>
<li>commercial judgment;</li>
<li>working relationship;</li>
<li>ownership;</li>
<li>communication;</li>
<li>appetite for uncertainty;</li>
<li>expectations about authority;</li>
<li>whether the candidate actually wants this job.</li>
</ul>
<p>The advisor should inform the decision, not make it alone.</p>
<h1>A practical first-engineer interview process</h1>
<p>Keep the process focused.</p>
<p>A reasonable structure might include four stages.</p>
<h1>Stage 1: Founder conversation</h1>
<p>Duration: 30–45 minutes</p>
<p>Explore:</p>
<ul>
<li>what the candidate wants;</li>
<li>why the company interests them;</li>
<li>what they have built;</li>
<li>how they operate with ambiguity;</li>
<li>what ownership means to them;</li>
<li>salary and equity alignment;</li>
<li>location and availability;</li>
<li>what they need from a founder.</li>
</ul>
<p>This is also your opportunity to sell the role honestly.</p>
<h1>Stage 2: Past-work deep dive</h1>
<p>Duration: 60 minutes</p>
<p>Ask the candidate to walk through one substantial project.</p>
<p>Explore:</p>
<ul>
<li>the original problem;</li>
<li>business context;</li>
<li>their personal contribution;</li>
<li>technical decisions;</li>
<li>constraints;</li>
<li>alternatives;</li>
<li>what failed;</li>
<li>what they would change;</li>
<li>how the system was operated;</li>
<li>measurable results.</li>
</ul>
<p>A candidate should be able to make the work understandable to you.</p>
<p>Ask simple questions without embarrassment.</p>
<p>Useful prompts include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Why did you choose that approach?</li>
<li>What other option did you consider?</li>
<li>What was the biggest risk?</li>
<li>What did customers notice?</li>
<li>What broke after launch?</li>
<li>How did you know it was working?</li>
<li>Which part did you personally own?</li>
<li>What would you simplify now?</li>
</ul>
<h1>Stage 3: Technical evaluation</h1>
<p>Duration: 60–120 minutes</p>
<p>Run this with a trusted technical evaluator.</p>
<p>Possible formats include:</p>
<ul>
<li>architecture discussion;</li>
<li>code review;</li>
<li>pair-debugging exercise;</li>
<li>relevant skills assessment;</li>
<li>walkthrough of existing code;</li>
<li>small realistic design exercise.</li>
</ul>
<p>Avoid irrelevant algorithm puzzles when the role is primarily about product delivery.</p>
<p>The evaluator should score predefined attributes and provide written evidence.</p>
<h1>Stage 4: Working session or paid trial</h1>
<p>A bounded paid trial can provide useful evidence for both sides.</p>
<p>Possible tasks include:</p>
<ul>
<li>review a sanitized technical plan;</li>
<li>diagnose a contained bug;</li>
<li>design a small non-production feature;</li>
<li>improve a sample codebase;</li>
<li>collaborate on product requirements;</li>
<li>create a technical implementation proposal.</li>
</ul>
<p>The task should not be unpaid production work.</p>
<p>Do not ask several candidates to build parts of your real product for free.</p>
<h1>Paid trial guidelines</h1>
<p>A responsible trial should have:</p>
<ul>
<li>a clear scope;</li>
<li>a written agreement;</li>
<li>fair payment;</li>
<li>an expected time limit;</li>
<li>no dependence on hidden information;</li>
<li>no requirement to work extreme hours;</li>
<li>clarity about intellectual property;</li>
<li>no promise of employment;</li>
<li>a scheduled review afterward.</li>
</ul>
<p>A trial should test collaboration and judgment, not endurance.</p>
<h1>What to evaluate in the technical round</h1>
<p>A first engineer does not need to know every technology.</p>
<p>Look for durable qualities.</p>
<h1>Problem framing</h1>
<p>Do they ask questions before proposing a solution?</p>
<p>Do they understand the business problem?</p>
<h1>Proportionality</h1>
<p>Do they choose technology appropriate to the current stage?</p>
<p>Or do they introduce distributed systems for a product with 20 users?</p>
<h1>Trade-off reasoning</h1>
<p>Can they explain what a decision gains and what it costs?</p>
<h1>Reliability</h1>
<p>Do they think about:</p>
<ul>
<li>data loss;</li>
<li>retries;</li>
<li>backups;</li>
<li>error handling;</li>
<li>observability;</li>
<li>recovery;</li>
<li>security?</li>
</ul>
<h1>Maintainability</h1>
<p>Will another engineer be able to understand and change the system?</p>
<h1>Delivery judgment</h1>
<p>Can they distinguish:</p>
<ul>
<li>necessary work;</li>
<li>valuable work;</li>
<li>optional polish;</li>
<li>premature optimization?</li>
</ul>
<h1>Communication</h1>
<p>Can they make technical decisions understandable without condescension?</p>
<h1>Ownership</h1>
<p>Do they discuss what happened after launch?</p>
<p>Shipping is not complete when code is merged.</p>
<h1>Questions founders should ask</h1>
<h1>“Tell me about something you shipped from an unclear starting point.”</h1>
<p>Listen for:</p>
<ul>
<li>questions asked;</li>
<li>assumptions tested;</li>
<li>customer input;</li>
<li>scope decisions;</li>
<li>iteration;</li>
<li>measurable outcomes.</li>
</ul>
<h1>“What did you deliberately not build?”</h1>
<p>Strong engineers know that omission is part of product strategy.</p>
<h1>“Tell me about an architecture decision you later changed.”</h1>
<p>Look for humility and evidence that experience updates their judgment.</p>
<h1>“What is the simplest version of our product you would build first?”</h1>
<p>This tests prioritization and whether the candidate understands the business.</p>
<h1>“How would you decide when to replace an early shortcut?”</h1>
<p>Look for thresholds, evidence, and awareness of migration cost.</p>
<h1>“Tell me about a production incident you owned.”</h1>
<p>Listen for:</p>
<ul>
<li>calm diagnosis;</li>
<li>accountability;</li>
<li>customer impact;</li>
<li>remediation;</li>
<li>prevention;</li>
<li>documentation.</li>
</ul>
<h1>“How do you work when the founder changes priorities?”</h1>
<p>The answer should include clarification, trade-offs, and communication—not blind compliance or contempt.</p>
<h1>“What do you need from me to do your best work?”</h1>
<p>This surfaces expectations before they become conflict.</p>
<h1>“How would you prepare the codebase for a second engineer?”</h1>
<p>Look for:</p>
<ul>
<li>documentation;</li>
<li>tests;</li>
<li>setup automation;</li>
<li>ownership boundaries;</li>
<li>code review;</li>
<li>deployment clarity.</li>
</ul>
<h1>Questions candidates should ask founders</h1>
<p>Strong candidates will evaluate you too.</p>
<p>Expect questions such as:</p>
<ul>
<li>What has been validated with customers?</li>
<li>Why is the company hiring now?</li>
<li>How much runway remains?</li>
<li>Who currently makes product decisions?</li>
<li>What technical work already exists?</li>
<li>Who owns the code and infrastructure?</li>
<li>What should be accomplished in six months?</li>
<li>How will disagreements be resolved?</li>
<li>How much autonomy will I have?</li>
<li>What happens when a customer request conflicts with the roadmap?</li>
<li>How many engineers do you expect to hire next?</li>
<li>What does equity represent on a fully diluted basis?</li>
<li>What are the working-hour expectations?</li>
<li>Is there an on-call expectation?</li>
<li>Who will review my technical decisions?</li>
<li>What would make this hire unsuccessful?</li>
</ul>
<p>Answer directly.</p>
<p>Evasive answers signal greater risk than the startup itself.</p>
<h1>Red flags in candidates</h1>
<p>No single signal should automatically decide the process, but investigate patterns such as:</p>
<ul>
<li>dismissing customer needs;</li>
<li>treating non-technical people with contempt;</li>
<li>refusing to explain decisions;</li>
<li>introducing complexity without justification;</li>
<li>claiming every past decision was correct;</li>
<li>blaming former teams for every failure;</li>
<li>focusing only on title and equity while ignoring the work;</li>
<li>wanting complete authority without accountability;</li>
<li>refusing documentation or testing as “too corporate”;</li>
<li>no interest in the business model;</li>
<li>inability to identify personal contribution;</li>
<li>secrecy around past work that goes beyond legitimate confidentiality;</li>
<li>promising unrealistic delivery dates to impress you.</li>
</ul>
<h1>Red flags candidates may see in founders</h1>
<p>Founders should recognize behaviors that drive strong engineers away:</p>
<ul>
<li>refusing to share a compensation range;</li>
<li>promising equity will “definitely” become valuable;</li>
<li>expecting permanent nights and weekends;</li>
<li>describing every requirement as urgent;</li>
<li>changing direction without acknowledging cost;</li>
<li>wanting senior judgment but resisting disagreement;</li>
<li>asking for unpaid production work;</li>
<li>providing no runway or funding context;</li>
<li>hiding technical debt;</li>
<li>using “we move fast” to excuse chaos;</li>
<li>treating security and testing as obstacles;</li>
<li>expecting one person to perform five full-time jobs;</li>
<li>refusing to pay for technical evaluation help;</li>
<li>presenting a prototype as nearly finished when it needs rebuilding.</li>
</ul>
<p>Candidate experience is part of your reputation.</p>
<h1>Check references properly</h1>
<p>Reference checks should validate how the person worked over time.</p>
<p>Ask for references who directly observed the candidate&#39;s work.</p>
<p>Useful questions include:</p>
<ul>
<li>What did they own?</li>
<li>What kind of environment helped them succeed?</li>
<li>Where did they need support?</li>
<li>How did they respond when something failed?</li>
<li>How did they work with non-technical stakeholders?</li>
<li>What was their effect on the people around them?</li>
<li>Would you hire or work with them again?</li>
<li>What role would you trust them with today?</li>
<li>What should their next manager know?</li>
</ul>
<p>The strongest reference question remains:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Would you work with this person again, and why?</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Listen to the clarity and enthusiasm of the answer, not only the words.</p>
<h1>Keep the interview process fast</h1>
<p>Strong candidates may be speaking with several companies.</p>
<p>You do not need to rush the decision carelessly, but you should avoid unnecessary delay.</p>
<p>A disciplined process should:</p>
<ul>
<li>publish the stages in advance;</li>
<li>schedule promptly;</li>
<li>avoid duplicated interviews;</li>
<li>collect feedback immediately;</li>
<li>make decisions quickly;</li>
<li>communicate delays;</li>
<li>close every candidate respectfully.</li>
</ul>
<p>For a small startup, a well-run process can often move from first call to decision within two weeks.</p>
<h1>Make the offer clearly</h1>
<p>A verbal offer should be followed by written terms.</p>
<p>Include:</p>
<ul>
<li>title;</li>
<li>reporting relationship;</li>
<li>base salary;</li>
<li>equity;</li>
<li>vesting;</li>
<li>bonus;</li>
<li>benefits;</li>
<li>location;</li>
<li>expected schedule;</li>
<li>start date;</li>
<li>employment conditions;</li>
<li>any probationary period;</li>
<li>intellectual-property terms;</li>
<li>confidentiality terms;</li>
<li>deadline for responding.</li>
</ul>
<p>Give the candidate reasonable time to review the offer.</p>
<p>Do not create false urgency.</p>
<h1>Be ready to answer equity questions</h1>
<p>Provide appropriate documentation and encourage the candidate to seek independent legal or financial advice.</p>
<p>Do not pressure them to interpret complex equity terms immediately during a celebratory call.</p>
<h1>Expect negotiation</h1>
<p>Negotiation does not mean the candidate lacks commitment.</p>
<p>They may discuss:</p>
<ul>
<li>salary;</li>
<li>equity;</li>
<li>title;</li>
<li>start date;</li>
<li>remote flexibility;</li>
<li>equipment;</li>
<li>benefits;</li>
<li>vesting;</li>
<li>exercise window;</li>
<li>severance;</li>
<li>review timing.</li>
</ul>
<p>Decide before the offer which terms are flexible.</p>
<h1>Do not withdraw an offer because the candidate asked reasonable questions</h1>
<p>A first engineer should be willing to examine risk.</p>
<p>Punishing diligence is an early warning about the working relationship.</p>
<h1>Onboarding starts before day one</h1>
<p>Once the candidate accepts, reduce uncertainty.</p>
<p>Send:</p>
<ul>
<li>final documents;</li>
<li>start-date confirmation;</li>
<li>equipment details;</li>
<li>access requirements;</li>
<li>company overview;</li>
<li>product materials;</li>
<li>customer context;</li>
<li>first-week schedule;</li>
<li>names of relevant advisors and partners.</li>
</ul>
<p>Stay in contact during their notice period without overwhelming them.</p>
<h1>The first week</h1>
<p>The first week should help the engineer understand the company before being expected to redesign it.</p>
<p>Cover:</p>
<ul>
<li>product vision;</li>
<li>current customers;</li>
<li>business model;</li>
<li>current roadmap;</li>
<li>financial constraints;</li>
<li>product assumptions;</li>
<li>technical assets;</li>
<li>vendor relationships;</li>
<li>existing accounts;</li>
<li>security risks;</li>
<li>legal or regulatory requirements;</li>
<li>unresolved questions.</li>
</ul>
<p>Give them access to:</p>
<ul>
<li>repositories;</li>
<li>cloud infrastructure;</li>
<li>domains;</li>
<li>analytics;</li>
<li>support tools;</li>
<li>documentation;</li>
<li>design files;</li>
<li>deployment systems;</li>
<li>relevant customer feedback.</li>
</ul>
<h1>Ship something small early</h1>
<p>A small production improvement in the first week or two helps verify:</p>
<ul>
<li>local setup;</li>
<li>access;</li>
<li>deployment;</li>
<li>review;</li>
<li>monitoring;</li>
<li>communication.</li>
</ul>
<p>It should not be a test designed to make the new hire fail.</p>
<p>Choose something useful and bounded.</p>
<h1>The first 30 days</h1>
<p>A good first-month plan may include:</p>
<ul>
<li>audit the current product and infrastructure;</li>
<li>understand customer workflows;</li>
<li>document the largest risks;</li>
<li>establish local setup;</li>
<li>improve deployment safety;</li>
<li>ship one or two small improvements;</li>
<li>propose a 90-day technical plan;</li>
<li>identify which work should not be done yet;</li>
<li>establish how founder-engineer decisions will be recorded.</li>
</ul>
<h1>The first 60 days</h1>
<p>The engineer may begin:</p>
<ul>
<li>owning a major product area;</li>
<li>reducing critical technical risk;</li>
<li>introducing basic testing;</li>
<li>improving observability;</li>
<li>clarifying architecture;</li>
<li>documenting systems;</li>
<li>replacing unsafe shortcuts;</li>
<li>establishing release practices;</li>
<li>contributing to future hiring plans.</li>
</ul>
<h1>The first 90 days</h1>
<p>By the end of the first quarter, you should have evidence about:</p>
<ul>
<li>delivery;</li>
<li>technical judgment;</li>
<li>communication;</li>
<li>customer understanding;</li>
<li>reliability;</li>
<li>founder collaboration;</li>
<li>ability to establish sustainable practices.</li>
</ul>
<p>Review the original scorecard together.</p>
<p>Do not evaluate success only by lines of code or feature count.</p>
<h1>Give ownership of outcomes, not only tickets</h1>
<p>A founding engineer should understand:</p>
<ul>
<li>the customer problem;</li>
<li>the business objective;</li>
<li>the constraints;</li>
<li>the success measure;</li>
<li>the deadline;</li>
<li>the decision authority.</li>
</ul>
<p>Weak assignment:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Build these twelve screens exactly as described.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Stronger assignment:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Reduce the time it takes a clinic administrator to onboard a new practitioner from two days to under thirty minutes. Here is what we know from customer interviews. Work with me to define and ship the smallest reliable solution.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Outcome ownership makes the engineer&#39;s judgment useful.</p>
<h1>Write down decisions</h1>
<p>Early-stage companies often make important decisions verbally and forget why.</p>
<p>Create lightweight written records for:</p>
<ul>
<li>architecture choices;</li>
<li>product trade-offs;</li>
<li>security decisions;</li>
<li>vendor selection;</li>
<li>deferred work;</li>
<li>customer commitments;</li>
<li>incidents;</li>
<li>major changes.</li>
</ul>
<p>Documentation should support speed, not become bureaucracy.</p>
<p>A short decision record can prevent months of confusion.</p>
<h1>Protect against key-person risk</h1>
<p>Your first engineer will naturally know more about the system than anyone else.</p>
<p>Do not allow that to become permanent dependency.</p>
<p>Require company ownership of:</p>
<ul>
<li>repositories;</li>
<li>cloud accounts;</li>
<li>billing;</li>
<li>domains;</li>
<li>deployment;</li>
<li>certificates;</li>
<li>app-store accounts;</li>
<li>database credentials;</li>
<li>monitoring;</li>
<li>third-party vendors;</li>
<li>backups.</li>
</ul>
<p>Encourage:</p>
<ul>
<li>documentation;</li>
<li>infrastructure as code;</li>
<li>password management;</li>
<li>access reviews;</li>
<li>reproducible setup;</li>
<li>incident runbooks.</li>
</ul>
<p>This is not distrust.</p>
<p>It is responsible company operation.</p>
<h1>Do not confuse chaos with startup speed</h1>
<p>Speed comes from:</p>
<ul>
<li>clear priorities;</li>
<li>fast feedback;</li>
<li>direct communication;</li>
<li>small releases;</li>
<li>reversible decisions;</li>
<li>customer access.</li>
</ul>
<p>Chaos comes from:</p>
<ul>
<li>constantly changing requirements;</li>
<li>no decision owner;</li>
<li>hidden customer promises;</li>
<li>no written priorities;</li>
<li>every request being urgent;</li>
<li>technical shortcuts with no follow-up;</li>
<li>founders overriding engineering decisions without discussion.</li>
</ul>
<p>A strong first engineer may tolerate uncertainty.</p>
<p>They will not thrive under permanent incoherence.</p>
<h1>Plan for the second hire</h1>
<p>The first engineer should not remain alone indefinitely when the workload requires a team.</p>
<p>Discuss:</p>
<ul>
<li>expected headcount;</li>
<li>timing;</li>
<li>which skills the second hire should add;</li>
<li>whether the first engineer wants management;</li>
<li>what leadership path exists;</li>
<li>which responsibilities need separation.</li>
</ul>
<p>Do not automatically turn the first engineer into a manager.</p>
<p>Excellent founding engineers may prefer to remain hands-on.</p>
<p>Management should be a deliberate role, not a reward or default promotion.</p>
<h1>Common founder mistakes</h1>
<h1>Hiring for the wrong stage</h1>
<p>A large-company architect may be poorly suited to hands-on product building.</p>
<p>A fast generalist may be poorly suited to a safety-critical technical problem.</p>
<p>Match the person to the stage.</p>
<h1>Hiring too early</h1>
<p>When the product direction changes weekly and nothing has been validated, a full-time engineer may only help you build the wrong thing faster.</p>
<h1>Hiring too late</h1>
<p>When the founder spends all day managing contractors, fixing technical misunderstandings, and avoiding product changes because the system is fragile, delaying ownership can also be expensive.</p>
<h1>Searching for a mythical candidate</h1>
<p>A job requiring world-class frontend, backend, mobile, infrastructure, security, AI, product, design, and management skill is not realistic.</p>
<p>Prioritize what matters most.</p>
<h1>Using equity to hide unaffordable salary</h1>
<p>Equity can increase alignment.</p>
<p>It cannot replace a livable compensation package for everyone.</p>
<h1>Overvaluing pedigree</h1>
<p>Prestigious employers do not guarantee startup suitability.</p>
<h1>Undervaluing communication</h1>
<p>The first engineer will need to explain difficult things repeatedly.</p>
<p>Communication is not a secondary skill.</p>
<h1>Ignoring references</h1>
<p>Reference checks can expose patterns an interview misses.</p>
<h1>Running an unpaid multi-day project</h1>
<p>You are selecting partly for free time and willingness to tolerate poor process.</p>
<h1>Hiring someone who never challenges you</h1>
<p>You need a partner who can disagree constructively.</p>
<h1>Hiring someone who challenges everything contemptuously</h1>
<p>Healthy pushback still requires respect and forward movement.</p>
<h1>Giving responsibility without authority</h1>
<p>The engineer cannot own delivery while the founder makes every technical decision unilaterally.</p>
<h1>Providing no product clarity</h1>
<p>Ambiguity is normal.</p>
<p>Total absence of priorities is not.</p>
<h1>Judging productivity by visible busyness</h1>
<p>The best technical decision may be to delete work, simplify scope, or delay an unnecessary system.</p>
<h1>A reusable first-engineer job-description template</h1>
<h2>Founding Software Engineer at [Company]</h2>
<p><strong>Location:</strong> [Remote region / city / hybrid arrangement]
<strong>Compensation:</strong> [Base range] plus [equity range or description]
<strong>Reports to:</strong> [Founder title]
<strong>Current team:</strong> [Team size and functions]</p>
<h3>About the company</h3>
<p>[Company] builds [plain product description] for [users].</p>
<p>We currently have [traction, customers, revenue, pilots, or product status]. The company is [bootstrapped / funded / profitable], with [appropriate funding or runway context].</p>
<h3>Why we are hiring</h3>
<p>We are hiring our first full-time engineer to own [specific technical and product mandate].</p>
<p>During the first 6 to 12 months, success means:</p>
<ul>
<li>[Outcome one]</li>
<li>[Outcome two]</li>
<li>[Outcome three]</li>
<li>[Foundation for future hiring]</li>
</ul>
<h3>Current product and technology</h3>
<p>The current product is [prototype / production system / agency-built application / no-code workflow].</p>
<p>Our current stack includes [relevant technologies], but we are open to changing tools when there is a clear reason.</p>
<p>The honest context: [one or two sentences about debt, gaps, risk, or uncertainty].</p>
<h3>What you will do</h3>
<ul>
<li>Own product engineering from problem definition through production.</li>
<li>Work directly with founders and customers.</li>
<li>Make proportionate architecture and technology decisions.</li>
<li>Improve reliability, testing, security, and deployment practices.</li>
<li>Document the system so future engineers can contribute.</li>
<li>Help define and interview future engineering hires.</li>
<li>Operate what you build and respond to production problems.</li>
</ul>
<h3>What we are looking for</h3>
<ul>
<li>Evidence of shipping and maintaining real products.</li>
<li>Strong judgment under ambiguity.</li>
<li>Ability to work across [required areas].</li>
<li>Clear communication with technical and non-technical people.</li>
<li>Comfort making trade-offs between speed, quality, and risk.</li>
<li>Interest in customers and business outcomes.</li>
<li>Ability to take ownership without requiring constant direction.</li>
</ul>
<h3>Helpful but not required</h3>
<ul>
<li>Experience in [domain].</li>
<li>Experience as an early engineer.</li>
<li>Experience hiring or mentoring.</li>
<li>Experience with [important technical area].</li>
</ul>
<p>You do not need experience with every tool in our current stack.</p>
<h3>Authority and working relationship</h3>
<p>You will own [technical decisions and responsibilities].</p>
<p>The founder will own [business and product responsibilities].</p>
<p>Major product and architecture decisions will be made through [shared process].</p>
<h3>Interview process</h3>
<ol>
<li>Founder conversation — [duration].</li>
<li>Past-work walkthrough — [duration].</li>
<li>Technical evaluation with [advisor or role] — [duration].</li>
<li>Paid working session — [scope and duration].</li>
<li>Reference checks and final discussion.</li>
</ol>
<p>We aim to complete the process within [number] business days.</p>
<h3>How to apply</h3>
<p>Send:</p>
<ul>
<li>your résumé or JobsList.dev profile;</li>
<li>a short note about something you built and owned;</li>
<li>links to relevant projects or writing when available.</li>
</ul>
<p>[Company] is an equal opportunity employer.</p>
<h1>Founder hiring checklist</h1>
<p>Before opening the role:</p>
<ul>
<li>Define the 6-to-12-month outcome.</li>
<li>Decide whether you need an employee, contractor, agency, or co-founder.</li>
<li>Choose the correct first-engineer profile.</li>
<li>Create a role scorecard.</li>
<li>Establish the salary range.</li>
<li>Define the equity approach.</li>
<li>Confirm who owns technical evaluation.</li>
<li>Write the honest company and product context.</li>
<li>Define decision authority.</li>
<li>Plan the interview stages.</li>
<li>Prepare a paid trial when appropriate.</li>
<li>Confirm repository, infrastructure, and intellectual-property ownership.</li>
<li>Build a 30-, 60-, and 90-day onboarding plan.</li>
</ul>
<p>Before making the offer:</p>
<ul>
<li>Collect independent interview feedback.</li>
<li>Review evidence against the scorecard.</li>
<li>Complete references.</li>
<li>Confirm compensation and location alignment.</li>
<li>Explain equity clearly.</li>
<li>Discuss working hours and on-call expectations.</li>
<li>Discuss management expectations.</li>
<li>Clarify authority and reporting.</li>
<li>Address candidate concerns directly.</li>
<li>Confirm that both sides understand the risk.</li>
</ul>
<p>After acceptance:</p>
<ul>
<li>Prepare equipment and access.</li>
<li>Transfer company-owned technical accounts.</li>
<li>Share customer and business context.</li>
<li>Schedule founder working sessions.</li>
<li>Define the first small production change.</li>
<li>Agree on a 30-day audit.</li>
<li>Record important decisions.</li>
<li>Review progress against outcomes rather than activity.</li>
<li>Begin planning for reduced key-person risk.</li>
<li>Revisit the next hiring need.</li>
</ul>
<h1>Advice for candidates applying as a first engineer</h1>
<p>A founding-engineer application should make ownership easy to see.</p>
<p>Your résumé should answer:</p>
<ul>
<li>What did you personally build?</li>
<li>What reached production?</li>
<li>Who used it?</li>
<li>What business problem did it solve?</li>
<li>What failed?</li>
<li>What did you learn?</li>
<li>Which decisions did you make?</li>
<li>How did you operate the system?</li>
<li>How did you work with customers or founders?</li>
<li>How did you make future engineers more effective?</li>
</ul>
<h1>Candidate résumé example</h1>
<p>Weak:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Worked on a React and Node.js application in a fast-paced environment.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Stronger:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Joined as the second engineer and took ownership of the customer onboarding workflow across React, Node.js, and PostgreSQL. Shipped the first self-service version in eight weeks, reduced manual setup by 70%, and documented the architecture and deployment process for three later hires.</p>
</blockquote>
<h1>Candidate profile summary example</h1>
<blockquote>
<p>Product-minded software engineer with six years of experience building and operating B2B web applications. I work primarily with TypeScript, React, Node.js, and PostgreSQL, but my strongest skill is taking an unclear customer problem from discovery through production. At my current company, I joined as the third engineer, launched the first self-service onboarding system, and helped establish code review, deployment, and incident practices as the team grew to nine engineers. I am looking for an early-stage role with meaningful product ownership and direct customer context.</p>
</blockquote>
<h1>What candidates should evaluate before accepting</h1>
<p>Candidates should verify:</p>
<ul>
<li>the company&#39;s runway;</li>
<li>the product&#39;s actual status;</li>
<li>customer evidence;</li>
<li>code and infrastructure ownership;</li>
<li>founder expectations;</li>
<li>working-hour expectations;</li>
<li>decision authority;</li>
<li>compensation sustainability;</li>
<li>equity terms;</li>
<li>future hiring plans;</li>
<li>technical support or advisor access;</li>
<li>whether the founder can receive disagreement;</li>
<li>whether the role is genuinely one job rather than several impossible jobs.</li>
</ul>
<p>The founder is evaluating the engineer.</p>
<p>The engineer should evaluate the founder with equal seriousness.</p>
<h1>Frequently asked questions</h1>
<h1>Should the first engineer be senior?</h1>
<p>Usually, the role requires enough experience to operate independently.</p>
<p>That often means senior-level judgment, though titles vary.</p>
<p>A junior engineer may be capable and talented but should not be placed alone in a role requiring architecture, production ownership, security decisions, and company-wide technical judgment without support.</p>
<h1>Should the first engineer become CTO?</h1>
<p>Not automatically.</p>
<p>“First engineer,” “technical lead,” “head of engineering,” and “CTO” are different roles.</p>
<p>A CTO generally carries company-level technical strategy, executive responsibility, leadership, and often external communication.</p>
<p>Give the title that matches the actual work and authority.</p>
<h1>Should the first engineer receive equity?</h1>
<p>Equity is common and often appropriate because of the role&#39;s risk and influence.</p>
<p>The amount depends on:</p>
<ul>
<li>company stage;</li>
<li>salary;</li>
<li>responsibility;</li>
<li>existing technical progress;</li>
<li>founder status;</li>
<li>market;</li>
<li>expected leadership;</li>
<li>risk.</li>
</ul>
<p>Obtain qualified legal and compensation advice.</p>
<h1>Can a non-technical founder manage an engineer?</h1>
<p>Yes, but the founder must manage:</p>
<ul>
<li>business context;</li>
<li>priorities;</li>
<li>outcomes;</li>
<li>communication;</li>
<li>resources;</li>
<li>decisions;</li>
<li>working relationship.</li>
</ul>
<p>Use an experienced technical advisor for specialized evaluation and oversight where needed.</p>
<h1>Should we use a coding test?</h1>
<p>Only when it measures relevant skills and adds evidence not available elsewhere.</p>
<p>For a first engineer, a realistic architecture conversation, code review, skills assessment, or paid working session may be more useful than generic algorithm puzzles.</p>
<h1>How long should the process take?</h1>
<p>Move carefully but promptly.</p>
<p>A focused process can often be completed within one to two weeks once interviews begin.</p>
<p>Long delays increase candidate loss and suggest indecision.</p>
<h1>Should we hire a friend?</h1>
<p>A friend can be a strong hire, but the relationship must survive:</p>
<ul>
<li>authority;</li>
<li>compensation;</li>
<li>performance feedback;</li>
<li>disagreement;</li>
<li>possible termination;</li>
<li>equity;</li>
<li>company pressure.</li>
</ul>
<p>Use the same clarity and written agreements you would use with anyone else.</p>
<h1>What if we cannot afford market salary?</h1>
<p>Options include:</p>
<ul>
<li>delay the hire;</li>
<li>reduce scope;</li>
<li>use a contractor;</li>
<li>raise funding;</li>
<li>hire part-time specialist help;</li>
<li>continue validating with no-code tools;</li>
<li>offer a consciously negotiated salary-equity trade-off.</li>
</ul>
<p>Do not misrepresent the financial reality.</p>
<h1>What if the candidate is stronger technically than I can evaluate?</h1>
<p>That is normal for a non-technical founder.</p>
<p>Bring in independent expertise and judge whether the candidate can make their decisions understandable.</p>
<h1>What if two technical advisors disagree?</h1>
<p>Ask each to explain:</p>
<ul>
<li>the evidence;</li>
<li>the role requirement;</li>
<li>the risk;</li>
<li>whether the concern is coachable;</li>
<li>which interview answer supports the conclusion.</li>
</ul>
<p>Return to the scorecard.</p>
<p>Do not resolve the disagreement by choosing the advisor with the most impressive title.</p>
<h1>What if the candidate wants more authority?</h1>
<p>Clarify whether the requested authority matches the accountability you expect.</p>
<p>A first engineer responsible for the entire technical system should have meaningful influence over technical decisions.</p>
<p>The founder should retain responsibility for company-level strategy and budget.</p>
<h1>The takeaway</h1>
<p>Hiring your first engineer begins long before the first interview.</p>
<p>You need to decide:</p>
<ol>
<li>Which business outcome the hire must create.</li>
<li>Whether a full-time employee is the right solution.</li>
<li>Which first-engineer profile fits the company&#39;s stage.</li>
<li>What authority and responsibility the role carries.</li>
<li>What compensation and equity you can defend.</li>
<li>How you will evaluate technical ability.</li>
<li>How the founder-engineer relationship will work.</li>
<li>What success should look like during the first 90 days.</li>
<li>How you will prevent permanent dependency on one person.</li>
<li>How the role may evolve as the team grows.</li>
</ol>
<p>Pay close to market where you can.</p>
<p>Explain equity honestly.</p>
<p>Borrow technical judgment instead of pretending to possess it.</p>
<p>Evaluate candidates through relevant work, structured evidence, clear communication, and references—not pedigree alone.</p>
<p>Run a focused, respectful process.</p>
<p>Once the engineer joins, give them a complete problem, the customer context, and enough authority to exercise the judgment you hired.</p>
<p>Your first engineer should not merely produce code.</p>
<p>They should help convert the company&#39;s uncertainty into learning, the product&#39;s weaknesses into a workable foundation, and the founder&#39;s vision into something customers can use.</p>
