3:T12a7,<p>Most bad hires and most missed great ones trace back to the same root cause: an unstructured process where interviewers wing the questions, take vague notes, and then decide in a hallway conversation dominated by whoever spoke loudest. Scorecards and structured debriefs fix this. They cost almost nothing to introduce and consistently produce better, fairer, faster decisions. Here&#39;s how to run them.</p>
<h2>Why gut feel fails</h2>
<p>&quot;I just had a good feeling about them&quot; usually means the candidate was likable, confident, or similar to the interviewer — none of which predict job performance. Unstructured interviews are among the weakest predictors of success, and they quietly amplify bias toward people who look and sound like the existing team. Structure doesn&#39;t remove judgment; it channels it toward the things that actually matter.</p>
<h2>Define the rubric before you interview</h2>
<p>Start from the role. What does success in this job require? Turn that into <strong>4–6 attributes</strong> you&#39;ll actually assess — for example:</p>
<ul>
<li>Technical depth in [the core skill]</li>
<li>Problem-solving / how they think through ambiguity</li>
<li>Communication and collaboration</li>
<li>Ownership and follow-through</li>
<li>Relevant domain experience</li>
</ul>
<p>Assign each interviewer specific attributes to focus on so you get coverage instead of five people all probing the same thing. This is the whole game: everyone knows what they&#39;re measuring before anyone walks in.</p>
<h2>The scorecard itself</h2>
<p>A good scorecard, filled out right after each interview, captures:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>A rating per assigned attribute</strong> on a simple scale (e.g. 1–4, deliberately even so there&#39;s no lazy &quot;middle&quot;).</li>
<li><strong>Evidence</strong> for each rating — the actual thing the candidate said or did. &quot;Strong: walked through the concurrency bug and explained the trade-off unprompted&quot; beats &quot;seemed smart.&quot;</li>
<li><strong>An overall recommendation:</strong> strong yes / yes / no / strong no. Avoid &quot;maybe&quot; — it forces a real judgment.</li>
</ul>
<p>The evidence matters more than the number. Ratings without examples are just feelings with a score attached.</p>
<h2>Submit before you discuss</h2>
<p>The most important rule: <strong>every interviewer records their rating before the debrief and before seeing anyone else&#39;s.</strong> This prevents anchoring, where one confident voice sways the room and independent signals collapse into groupthink. Independent judgments, aggregated, are far more reliable than a conversation that converges on the loudest opinion.</p>
<h2>Running the debrief</h2>
<p>Keep it tight and evidence-driven:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Collect recommendations first</strong> — a quick round so everyone&#39;s initial call is on the table.</li>
<li><strong>Dig into disagreement.</strong> Splits are the most valuable moments: one interviewer saw something the others didn&#39;t. Explore <em>why</em>, using evidence, not vibes.</li>
<li><strong>Anchor on the rubric</strong>, not on likability. &quot;Does the evidence show they can do this job?&quot; — not &quot;would I want to grab a beer with them?&quot;</li>
<li><strong>Name the risks</strong> explicitly, and decide whether they&#39;re addressable.</li>
<li><strong>Reach a clear decision</strong> with an owner. A debrief that ends in a shrug wasted everyone&#39;s time.</li>
</ol>
<h2>Guard against the classic traps</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Halo effect:</strong> one impressive thing (a famous employer, a slick demo) coloring everything else. Rate attributes independently.</li>
<li><strong>Similarity bias:</strong> rating people higher because they remind you of yourself. Ask, &quot;am I scoring skill or familiarity?&quot;</li>
<li><strong>Recency:</strong> the last interview looming largest. Written scorecards fix this.</li>
<li><strong>The confident-but-wrong veto.</strong> A strong &quot;no&quot; should still come with evidence; seniority isn&#39;t an argument.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Keep it lightweight</h2>
<p>Structure fails when it becomes bureaucracy. Scorecards should take five minutes, not an essay. The goal is consistent signal and a fair, defensible decision — not paperwork. A one-page rubric, ratings submitted independently, and a 30-minute evidence-based debrief is enough to dramatically outperform gut feel.</p>
<p>Teams that adopt this stop having the &quot;I don&#39;t know, what did you think?&quot; conversation and start making decisions they can explain — to themselves, to the candidate, and to the new hire&#39;s future manager. That clarity is how you hire better people and treat every candidate more fairly at the same time.</p>
