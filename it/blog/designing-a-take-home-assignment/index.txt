e:T3736,<h1>How to Design a Take-Home Assignment Candidates Won&#39;t Resent</h1>
<p>Take-home assignments are one of the most controversial parts of technical hiring. Some candidates love them because they can work in a familiar environment without the pressure of live coding. Others avoid companies that require them because they&#39;ve spent entire weekends completing assignments that were never reviewed.</p>
<p>Both perspectives are understandable.</p>
<p>A well-designed take-home assignment can reveal far more about how someone engineers software than an hour of algorithm puzzles. A poorly designed one communicates something equally important—that the company doesn&#39;t value a candidate&#39;s time.</p>
<p>The goal isn&#39;t simply to filter applicants.</p>
<p>It&#39;s to create a hiring experience that is fair, respectful, predictive of job performance, and enjoyable enough that strong candidates actually complete it.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Why companies use take-home assignments</h1>
<p>The best software engineering happens over hours—not minutes.</p>
<p>Developers research documentation, write tests, refactor, step away from the keyboard, rethink decisions, and improve their work over time.</p>
<p>Take-home projects allow candidates to work the same way they would during a normal workday.</p>
<p>Unlike whiteboard interviews, they reveal things like:</p>
<ul>
<li>Project organization</li>
<li>Code readability</li>
<li>Testing habits</li>
<li>Documentation quality</li>
<li>Error handling</li>
<li>API design</li>
<li>Git usage</li>
<li>Architecture decisions</li>
<li>Attention to detail</li>
<li>Trade-off thinking</li>
</ul>
<p>These are the exact skills developers use every day.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Why candidates dislike them</h1>
<p>Candidates don&#39;t dislike take-home assignments.</p>
<p>They dislike bad ones.</p>
<p>The biggest complaints include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Assignments requiring an entire weekend</li>
<li>Vague requirements</li>
<li>Projects resembling unpaid consulting work</li>
<li>Never receiving feedback</li>
<li>Spending hours only to receive an automated rejection</li>
<li>Discovering the company never even opened the repository</li>
</ul>
<p>Respecting candidates starts by understanding these frustrations.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Decide whether you actually need one</h1>
<p>Before creating a take-home assignment, ask whether another interview format would provide the same information.</p>
<p>A take-home works well when:</p>
<ul>
<li>The role requires significant independent work.</li>
<li>You want to evaluate engineering habits rather than interview performance.</li>
<li>Candidates are geographically distributed.</li>
<li>You want to reduce live interview stress.</li>
<li>You care about software quality more than memorized algorithms.</li>
</ul>
<p>Consider alternatives when:</p>
<ul>
<li>A 90-minute pairing session would answer the same questions.</li>
<li>The role depends heavily on collaboration.</li>
<li>Time-to-hire is critical.</li>
<li>Your hiring team doesn&#39;t have capacity to review submissions carefully.</li>
</ul>
<p>Many companies now let candidates choose between a take-home assignment and a live pairing session.</p>
<p>Offering both creates a more inclusive process while respecting different working styles.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Keep it under three hours</h1>
<p>This is the single biggest improvement most hiring teams can make.</p>
<p>A take-home assignment should require approximately <strong>two to three hours</strong> for a qualified candidate.</p>
<p>Longer projects don&#39;t produce significantly better hiring signals—they simply eliminate candidates who have families, demanding jobs, caregiving responsibilities, or multiple interviews.</p>
<p>Before publishing an assignment:</p>
<ol>
<li>Have multiple engineers complete it.</li>
<li>Measure the actual time.</li>
<li>Average the results.</li>
<li>Add a small buffer.</li>
<li>Publish the expected completion time.</li>
</ol>
<p>If your engineers consistently take four hours, don&#39;t advertise it as a two-hour exercise.</p>
<p>Candidates notice.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Tell candidates exactly what you&#39;re evaluating</h1>
<p>One of the biggest reasons assignments become unnecessarily large is uncertainty.</p>
<p>Candidates don&#39;t know whether you&#39;ll judge:</p>
<ul>
<li>UI polish</li>
<li>Architecture</li>
<li>Testing</li>
<li>Documentation</li>
<li>Performance</li>
<li>Accessibility</li>
<li>Security</li>
<li>Git history</li>
<li>Deployment</li>
</ul>
<p>So they attempt everything.</p>
<p>Instead, be explicit.</p>
<p>Example:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>We are evaluating:</p>
<ul>
<li>Code organization</li>
<li>Readability</li>
<li>Error handling</li>
<li>Basic automated tests</li>
<li>Clear documentation</li>
</ul>
<p>We are <strong>not</strong> evaluating:</p>
<ul>
<li>Fancy UI design</li>
<li>Authentication</li>
<li>CI/CD</li>
<li>Production deployment</li>
<li>Pixel-perfect styling</li>
</ul>
</blockquote>
<p>Removing ambiguity helps everyone.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Give realistic requirements</h1>
<p>Good assignments resemble real work.</p>
<p>Examples include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Extending an existing API</li>
<li>Fixing a bug</li>
<li>Building a small feature</li>
<li>Reviewing an existing pull request</li>
<li>Refactoring poorly written code</li>
<li>Designing a database schema</li>
<li>Creating a REST endpoint</li>
<li>Writing integration tests</li>
<li>Consuming a public API</li>
</ul>
<p>Avoid abstract programming puzzles.</p>
<p>If candidates would never perform the task on the job, reconsider including it.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Never assign production work</h1>
<p>Candidates should never wonder whether they&#39;re completing free consulting.</p>
<p>Avoid assignments like:</p>
<ul>
<li>Designing a feature your company plans to ship</li>
<li>Fixing a real production bug</li>
<li>Writing code that enters your codebase</li>
<li>Building an internal tool</li>
<li>Creating marketing assets</li>
</ul>
<p>Instead, use fictional domains or archived sample projects.</p>
<p>Candidates should leave knowing their work existed solely for evaluation.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Allow candidates to use AI</h1>
<p>Modern software development includes AI assistants.</p>
<p>Pretending otherwise creates an artificial interview environment.</p>
<p>Instead of banning AI:</p>
<ul>
<li>Encourage candidates to use their normal workflow.</li>
<li>Focus your evaluation on understanding.</li>
<li>Ask them to explain decisions afterward.</li>
<li>Discuss trade-offs.</li>
<li>Modify requirements during the debrief.</li>
</ul>
<p>Developers who genuinely understand their code can explain it.</p>
<p>Those who blindly accepted generated code usually cannot.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Ask for a README</h1>
<p>The README often tells you more than the code.</p>
<p>Ask candidates to include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Assumptions</li>
<li>Design decisions</li>
<li>Trade-offs</li>
<li>Known limitations</li>
<li>Future improvements</li>
<li>Setup instructions</li>
<li>Testing instructions</li>
<li>Time spent</li>
<li>Areas they intentionally skipped</li>
</ul>
<p>Excellent engineers explain their thinking.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Evaluate engineering judgment</h1>
<p>Don&#39;t score only the finished product.</p>
<p>Evaluate how candidates approached the work.</p>
<p>Look for:</p>
<h2>Simplicity</h2>
<p>Did they solve the problem cleanly?</p>
<p>Or over-engineer it?</p>
<hr>
<h2>Maintainability</h2>
<p>Would another developer understand the project?</p>
<hr>
<h2>Testing</h2>
<p>Did they test meaningful behavior?</p>
<p>Or chase arbitrary coverage percentages?</p>
<hr>
<h2>Error handling</h2>
<p>What happens when things go wrong?</p>
<hr>
<h2>Documentation</h2>
<p>Would another engineer know how to run the project?</p>
<hr>
<h2>Trade-offs</h2>
<p>Did they make reasonable decisions given the time constraints?</p>
<hr>
<h2>Scope control</h2>
<p>Did they build exactly what was requested?</p>
<p>Or spend ten extra hours polishing unnecessary features?</p>
<p>Professional engineers know when to stop.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Use a scoring rubric</h1>
<p>Review every submission using the same criteria.</p>
<p>For example:</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Category</th>
<th>Weight</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody><tr>
<td>Correctness</td>
<td>25%</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Code quality</td>
<td>20%</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Testing</td>
<td>15%</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Architecture</td>
<td>15%</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Documentation</td>
<td>10%</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Error handling</td>
<td>10%</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Communication</td>
<td>5%</td>
</tr>
</tbody></table>
<p>A structured rubric creates consistency between interviewers and reduces unconscious bias.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Always review the submission live</h1>
<p>Never reject someone based only on repository contents.</p>
<p>Schedule a short follow-up conversation.</p>
<p>Ask questions like:</p>
<ul>
<li>Why did you choose this architecture?</li>
<li>What would you improve with another day?</li>
<li>What was the hardest decision?</li>
<li>What trade-offs did you make?</li>
<li>How would you scale this?</li>
<li>What assumptions did you make?</li>
<li>What would you test next?</li>
<li>Where do you think your solution is weakest?</li>
</ul>
<p>This discussion often reveals more than the assignment itself.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Give useful feedback</h1>
<p>Candidates invested their time.</p>
<p>Return the favor.</p>
<p>Even a few sentences help:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Your project was well organized and easy to read. We especially liked your testing strategy and documentation. We ultimately moved forward with another candidate who demonstrated more experience designing scalable APIs, but we appreciate the thoughtful submission.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Constructive feedback leaves candidates with a positive impression—even if they aren&#39;t hired.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Candidate tips: How to succeed on take-home assignments</h1>
<p>If you&#39;re applying for developer roles, remember that companies are evaluating how you think—not how many hours you can spend polishing a project.</p>
<p>Some practical advice:</p>
<h3>Read everything first</h3>
<p>Understand the entire assignment before writing code.</p>
<hr>
<h3>Clarify assumptions</h3>
<p>If something is ambiguous, ask.</p>
<p>Good communication is part of engineering.</p>
<hr>
<h3>Don&#39;t gold-plate</h3>
<p>Follow the requested scope.</p>
<p>Shipping a complete, focused solution is better than building half of an ambitious one.</p>
<hr>
<h3>Write a strong README</h3>
<p>Explain:</p>
<ul>
<li>Why you made certain decisions</li>
<li>What you&#39;d improve</li>
<li>What you intentionally skipped</li>
<li>How to run the project</li>
</ul>
<p>Hiring managers appreciate thoughtful documentation.</p>
<hr>
<h3>Commit logically</h3>
<p>Small, meaningful Git commits demonstrate professional workflow and make your reasoning easier to follow.</p>
<hr>
<h3>Test important behavior</h3>
<p>You don&#39;t need exhaustive test coverage.</p>
<p>Instead, test the code that carries the most risk.</p>
<hr>
<h3>Be ready to discuss your work</h3>
<p>Review your project before the follow-up interview.</p>
<p>Be prepared to explain:</p>
<ul>
<li>Architecture</li>
<li>Trade-offs</li>
<li>Bugs</li>
<li>Performance considerations</li>
<li>Future improvements</li>
</ul>
<p>The conversation often determines the final hiring decision.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Common mistakes companies make</h1>
<p>Avoid these common pitfalls:</p>
<ul>
<li>Projects requiring an entire weekend</li>
<li>Hidden requirements</li>
<li>No published time expectation</li>
<li>Rejecting candidates without feedback</li>
<li>Reviewing submissions inconsistently</li>
<li>Expecting production-quality software</li>
<li>Using the assignment before speaking with a candidate</li>
<li>Evaluating style instead of engineering judgment</li>
<li>Penalizing candidates for using modern development tools</li>
<li>Letting assignments sit unread for weeks</li>
</ul>
<p>Every interaction contributes to your employer brand.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Common mistakes candidates make</h1>
<p>Candidates can also improve their chances by avoiding:</p>
<ul>
<li>Ignoring the README</li>
<li>Overengineering simple requirements</li>
<li>Skipping tests entirely</li>
<li>Copying code they can&#39;t explain</li>
<li>Forgetting error handling</li>
<li>Missing setup instructions</li>
<li>Rushing through documentation</li>
<li>Spending twenty hours on a three-hour assignment</li>
<li>Submitting without running the application one final time</li>
</ul>
<p>Remember that professionalism matters just as much as technical skill.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Building trust through the interview process</h1>
<p>The strongest hiring processes leave candidates feeling respected regardless of the outcome.</p>
<p>When companies publish realistic expectations, provide clear requirements, review submissions carefully, give thoughtful feedback, and communicate quickly, they build trust that extends beyond a single hiring decision.</p>
<p>Candidates talk to one another.</p>
<p>A respectful interview process attracts future applicants, strengthens your employer brand, and improves acceptance rates for top candidates.</p>
<hr>
<h1>Final thoughts</h1>
<p>Take-home assignments should never feel like unpaid labor.</p>
<p>They should resemble real engineering work, have a clearly defined scope, fit comfortably within two to three hours, and evaluate the qualities that matter most: thoughtful design, clean code, communication, testing, and sound engineering judgment.</p>
<p>For candidates, the assignment is an opportunity to demonstrate how they solve real problems—not simply whether they can memorize algorithms.</p>
<p>For employers, it&#39;s an opportunity to identify engineers who write maintainable software, collaborate effectively, and make thoughtful technical decisions.</p>
<p>When both sides respect each other&#39;s time, take-home assignments become one of the most valuable stages of the hiring process.</p>
