How to Answer 'What's Your Greatest Weakness?' (Without a Cliché)

The JobsList.dev Team·21 luglio 2026·3 min read

"What's your greatest weakness?" is the interview question everyone dreads and almost everyone answers badly. The classic dodge — "I'm a perfectionist" or "I just work too hard" — fools no one and quietly signals that you either lack self-awareness or don't trust the interviewer with a real answer. The good news: a genuine, well-framed answer is one of the easiest ways to stand out, because so few people give one.

What the interviewer is actually asking

They are not trying to trap you. Interviewers ask this to learn three things:

  • Do you know yourself? Self-aware engineers are easier to manage and grow.
  • Can you talk about a shortcoming without spiraling or getting defensive?
  • Do you do anything about it? Growth mindset beats a flawless facade.

Once you see it as "show me you can reflect and improve," the pressure drops. You're not confessing a fatal flaw — you're demonstrating maturity.

The structure that works

A strong answer has three beats:

  1. A real, specific weakness — one that's genuine but not disqualifying for the role.
  2. A concrete example of when it caused a problem.
  3. What you're actively doing about it, ideally with evidence it's working.

That last part is where most people stop short. The improvement is the whole point of the answer.

Choosing a weakness that's honest but safe

Pick something true that isn't core to the job you're interviewing for. A backend engineer admitting "design polish isn't my strength" is fine; "I struggle to write correct code under deadline" is not. Good, common examples:

  • Delegating instead of doing everything yourself.
  • Getting into the weeds and losing sight of the deadline.
  • Being hesitant to give critical feedback to peers.
  • Public speaking or presenting to large groups.
  • Saying yes to too much and overcommitting.

Avoid two extremes: the fake weakness (perfectionism), and the confession that would make anyone nervous (missing deadlines, poor collaboration, honesty problems).

An example answer

"Early on, I had a habit of taking on too much myself instead of delegating — I'd rather just fix a bug than explain it to someone else. On one project that became a real bottleneck: I was the blocker for three people because I'd kept too much in my own head. Since then I've forced myself to write things down and hand work off. I started keeping a short doc for anything only I knew how to do, and I've been deliberately pairing juniors on tasks I'd normally grab. Our bus factor is better and honestly my weeks are calmer."

Notice: specific, owns the impact, and ends on active, evidenced improvement.

Mistakes to avoid

  • The humble-brag. "I care too much." Interviewers have heard it a thousand times.
  • Trauma-dumping. Keep it professional and bounded; this isn't therapy.
  • No resolution. A weakness with no plan reads as a red flag.
  • Too many. Give one, go deep. A laundry list sounds anxious.
  • Blaming others. "My weakness is I have low tolerance for incompetent coworkers" is a self-report you don't want to give.

Practice out loud

Write your answer down, then say it a few times until it sounds like you, not a script. Time it — 45 to 75 seconds is plenty. If you have a version that's true, specific, and ends on growth, you'll walk into that part of the interview relaxed instead of bracing for it.

The candidates who handle this question well aren't the ones with no weaknesses. They're the ones who clearly know themselves and are visibly getting better. That's exactly the person teams want to hire.
