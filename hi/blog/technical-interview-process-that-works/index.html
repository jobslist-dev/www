Designing a Technical Interview Process That Actually Predicts Performance

A practical guide to building a fair, efficient, and job-relevant technical interview process that evaluates real engineering ability, reduces bias, respects candidates, and improves hiring decisions.

The JobsList.dev Team·21 मई 2026·42 blog.article.minuteRead
interviewinghiringengineeringtechnical-interviewsdeveloper-hiring

The purpose of a technical interview is not to discover who performs best under artificial pressure.

It is not to reward the candidate who has memorized the largest collection of algorithm patterns, remembers the most obscure language details, or speaks with the greatest confidence.

The purpose is much simpler:

Predict whether this person can perform the work, collaborate effectively, exercise sound judgment, and grow within the role.

Many technical interview processes fail at that goal.

They test candidates on problems unrelated to the position, allow interviewers to improvise inconsistent questions, rely on vague impressions, and stretch the process across so many rounds that strong candidates withdraw before a decision is made.

The result is costly:

  • capable developers are rejected for weak reasons;
  • candidates skilled at interviewing are mistaken for strong employees;
  • interviewers disagree because they evaluated different qualities;
  • bias enters through undefined concepts such as “fit”;
  • candidates receive a poor impression of the company;
  • open roles remain unfilled;
  • existing engineers lose time to repetitive interviews;
  • hiring decisions become harder rather than clearer.

A better process does not require lowering the technical bar.

It requires defining the bar more accurately and collecting evidence that relates to the actual work.

Begin with the decision the interview must support

Before designing interview stages, clarify what the company needs to decide.

A useful technical interview process should answer questions such as:

  • Can the candidate perform the core technical work?
  • Can they break down an unfamiliar problem?
  • Can they write or review maintainable code?
  • Can they test and debug software?
  • Can they reason about trade-offs?
  • Can they communicate assumptions and uncertainty?
  • Can they work effectively with teammates?
  • Can they operate at the expected level of independence?
  • Can they learn unfamiliar tools or systems?
  • Can they contribute safely in a production environment?
  • Can they create the outcomes required by the role?

These questions are more useful than:

  • Did the candidate solve the puzzle quickly?
  • Did they use the interviewer's preferred algorithm?
  • Did they appear confident?
  • Would the interviewer enjoy having lunch with them?
  • Did they know the exact framework already?
  • Did they attend a prestigious school?
  • Did their answer feel senior?

The first set focuses on evidence.

The second relies heavily on proxies and personal impressions.

Define success in the role before evaluating candidates

A technical interview cannot predict performance when the role itself is unclear.

Before publishing the position, define:

  • the mission of the role;
  • the expected level;
  • the major responsibilities;
  • the systems involved;
  • the outcomes expected during the first year;
  • the decisions the person will own;
  • the degree of autonomy required;
  • the people and teams they will collaborate with;
  • the technical risks they will manage;
  • the skills required immediately;
  • the skills that can be learned after joining.

Without this preparation, every interviewer may evaluate a different version of the job.

One interviewer may focus on algorithmic speed. Another may emphasize system design. Another may evaluate personality. A fourth may reject candidates who lack experience with one preferred tool.

The resulting feedback cannot be compared meaningfully because the interviewers were not answering the same question.

Create a role scorecard

A role scorecard converts a broad job description into observable outcomes and competencies.

For example:

Role mission

Build and operate reliable backend services supporting customer identity, authorization, and account access.

Expected first-year outcomes

  • Take ownership of at least one production service.
  • Reduce recurring authentication-related incidents.
  • Improve test coverage and observability.
  • Contribute to authorization architecture.
  • Lead or support one meaningful service migration.
  • Document operational procedures.
  • Mentor less-experienced engineers.

Technical competencies

  • Backend development
  • API design
  • Data modeling
  • Testing
  • Debugging
  • Security
  • Reliability
  • System design

Working competencies

  • Communication
  • Collaboration
  • Ownership
  • Learning
  • Judgment
  • Prioritization
  • Response to feedback

Every interview stage should collect evidence for one or more items on this scorecard.

If a question does not help evaluate a defined competency, reconsider whether it belongs in the process.

Separate essential skills from learnable tools

Many interview loops overvalue exact stack experience.

A company using Go, PostgreSQL, Kubernetes, and AWS may assume every candidate must already have production experience with all four.

That may unnecessarily exclude strong developers with transferable experience.

Separate requirements into three groups.

Essential before joining

Skills the candidate genuinely needs to perform the role safely.

Examples:

  • ability to write production-quality backend code;
  • understanding of HTTP and API design;
  • relational data-modeling fundamentals;
  • testing and debugging ability;
  • basic security awareness;
  • ability to explain technical decisions.

Learnable after joining

Skills a capable engineer can acquire with reasonable support.

Examples:

  • your exact cloud provider;
  • a particular queueing product;
  • internal deployment tooling;
  • your observability vendor;
  • company-specific architecture conventions;
  • internal business terminology.

Helpful but optional

Experience that may provide an advantage but should not automatically determine the outcome.

Examples:

  • experience in the same industry;
  • familiarity with a secondary language;
  • prior startup experience;
  • open-source maintenance;
  • public speaking.

The interview should test transferable engineering ability rather than rewarding only candidates whose previous company used the same tools.

Match the evaluation to the work

The strongest assessments resemble the job.

If the role involves reviewing pull requests, include code review.

If the role involves debugging production services, include debugging.

If the role involves designing APIs, ask the candidate to design an API.

If the role involves building accessible interfaces, evaluate accessibility.

If the role involves infrastructure automation, test infrastructure reasoning.

A candidate for a backend product role should not be judged primarily through obscure graph algorithms unless those algorithms are genuinely important to the work.

A candidate for a frontend role should not spend the entire interview implementing a balanced tree while accessibility, browser behavior, state management, and component design remain unevaluated.

The closer the assessment is to the job, the easier it is to interpret the result.

Use work samples rather than abstract proxies

A work sample gives the candidate an opportunity to perform a smaller version of the actual job.

Useful formats include:

  • a live coding exercise;
  • a debugging exercise;
  • a code-review exercise;
  • a pair-programming session;
  • an API-design task;
  • a data-modeling discussion;
  • a system-design exercise;
  • a project walkthrough;
  • a short take-home assignment;
  • an incident-analysis exercise.

The correct format depends on what the role requires.

No single assessment is ideal for every engineering position.

Live coding exercises

Live coding can reveal:

  • problem decomposition;
  • communication;
  • coding fundamentals;
  • testing habits;
  • response to feedback;
  • ability to work through uncertainty.

However, live coding can also produce artificial pressure.

To make it more predictive:

  • use a realistic problem;
  • allow normal documentation;
  • explain the evaluation criteria;
  • let candidates ask questions;
  • avoid trick requirements;
  • provide a familiar editor where possible;
  • treat the session as collaboration;
  • allow reasonable accommodations;
  • evaluate the process, not only completion.

Do not expect candidates to behave exactly as they would during ordinary work while being watched by several strangers.

Account for the unusual environment when interpreting performance.

Pair-programming exercises

Pair programming can be especially valuable for roles that require close collaboration.

A good pairing session allows the interviewer and candidate to:

  • clarify the problem together;
  • discuss possible approaches;
  • write or modify code;
  • test the result;
  • review trade-offs;
  • respond to new requirements.

The interviewer should act like a teammate, not an adversary.

Useful behaviors to observe include:

  • whether the candidate asks clarifying questions;
  • how they explain their reasoning;
  • whether they respond constructively to suggestions;
  • whether they notice errors;
  • how they decide what to test;
  • whether they can revise their approach.

Do not intentionally withhold essential information simply to see whether the candidate fails.

Debugging exercises

Debugging is one of the most common activities in software engineering, yet many interview processes barely test it.

A useful debugging exercise can evaluate whether the candidate:

  1. Clarifies the expected behavior.
  2. Reproduces the problem.
  3. Reads logs and errors carefully.
  4. Forms a hypothesis.
  5. Gathers evidence.
  6. Changes one variable at a time.
  7. Verifies the solution.
  8. Adds regression protection.
  9. Considers production impact.
  10. Communicates uncertainty.

The objective should not be to hide an absurdly obscure bug.

Use a realistic failure that rewards a disciplined investigation.

Code-review exercises

Professional developers often spend significant time reading and reviewing code.

A code-review exercise can reveal:

  • correctness;
  • maintainability;
  • testing judgment;
  • security awareness;
  • communication;
  • prioritization;
  • ability to distinguish critical issues from preferences.

Give the candidate a small pull request and ask them to review it.

Observe whether they identify:

  • functional errors;
  • missing tests;
  • unclear naming;
  • security risks;
  • performance concerns;
  • unnecessary complexity;
  • maintainability issues.

Also evaluate how they communicate feedback.

A strong reviewer can explain why a change matters without turning every personal preference into a blocking issue.

API-design exercises

An API-design exercise may evaluate:

  • requirements clarification;
  • resource modeling;
  • request and response design;
  • validation;
  • authentication;
  • authorization;
  • idempotency;
  • errors;
  • versioning;
  • pagination;
  • observability.

A useful prompt might ask the candidate to design part of a job-application or payment workflow.

Do not score the candidate against one hidden endpoint structure.

Evaluate whether the design is coherent, justified, secure, and appropriate for the stated requirements.

Data-modeling exercises

For roles involving databases, ask candidates to model a realistic domain.

Evaluate:

  • entity boundaries;
  • relationships;
  • constraints;
  • indexes;
  • normalization;
  • consistency;
  • migration concerns;
  • query patterns;
  • retention;
  • privacy;
  • scaling assumptions.

Do not reward complexity for its own sake.

A simple relational model may be the strongest solution when the requirements do not justify a distributed data platform.

System-design interviews

System design should evaluate judgment, not diagram performance.

A strong candidate will usually begin by clarifying:

  • users;
  • use cases;
  • scale;
  • latency;
  • availability;
  • consistency;
  • security;
  • privacy;
  • cost;
  • operational constraints.

They may then discuss:

  • interfaces;
  • data models;
  • major components;
  • request flows;
  • failure modes;
  • caching;
  • queues;
  • observability;
  • deployment;
  • evolution.

Good system design does not mean naming the most technologies.

It means choosing an appropriate level of complexity and explaining the consequences.

Useful follow-up questions include:

  • What happens when this dependency fails?
  • How would retries affect the operation?
  • Which data must remain consistent?
  • What becomes the first bottleneck?
  • How would you deploy this safely?
  • What would you monitor?
  • Which security boundary matters most?
  • At what scale would the architecture need to change?
  • Which parts would you postpone until they are needed?

Project walkthroughs

A detailed discussion of prior work can provide strong evidence without requiring the candidate to produce new unpaid work.

Ask the candidate to select a meaningful project and explain:

  • the problem;
  • the users;
  • their role;
  • the team;
  • the constraints;
  • the architecture;
  • important decisions;
  • alternatives considered;
  • failures;
  • testing;
  • deployment;
  • monitoring;
  • results;
  • lessons.

Useful follow-up questions include:

  • What did you personally own?
  • Which decision was most difficult?
  • What did another teammate own?
  • What went wrong?
  • How did you measure success?
  • Which assumption proved incorrect?
  • What would you change now?
  • How did the system behave in production?
  • How did the work affect customers or the business?

A credible candidate should be able to move beyond the compressed résumé bullet and explain the real work.

Take-home assignments

Take-home exercises can reveal:

  • code organization;
  • independent decision-making;
  • testing;
  • documentation;
  • maintainability;
  • written communication.

They also create significant risks:

  • excessive unpaid labor;
  • unequal access to free time;
  • unclear authorship;
  • candidate withdrawal;
  • over-polished submissions;
  • delays in the hiring process.

Use take-home work carefully.

A reasonable assignment should be:

  • clearly time-boxed;
  • related to the role;
  • small enough to complete without sacrificing a weekend;
  • evaluated through a rubric;
  • accompanied by clear instructions;
  • followed by a discussion;
  • compensated when substantial work is required.

Tell candidates that unfinished work can still be discussed.

Do not quietly reward the person who spent 15 hours on a stated two-hour assignment.

Consider paid assessments

When an assignment requires several hours of meaningful work, compensation demonstrates respect and expands access.

Paid assessments can reduce the disadvantage faced by candidates who:

  • have current jobs;
  • provide care for others;
  • work multiple jobs;
  • have limited free time;
  • cannot afford extended unpaid exercises.

Payment does not solve every issue, but it establishes that the company values the candidate's labor.

Allow alternatives where practical

One assessment format may not work equally well for every candidate.

Where possible, provide options such as:

  • live pairing;
  • short take-home work;
  • code review;
  • project walkthrough;
  • modified assessment with accommodations.

The objective is to collect comparable evidence, not to require everyone to demonstrate ability in one rigid format.

Decide what each stage evaluates

Do not ask every interviewer to evaluate the entire candidate.

Assign specific competencies to each stage.

For example:

Interview stage Primary evidence
Initial conversation Motivation, logistics, role alignment
Coding exercise Coding, testing, problem decomposition
Code review Maintainability, judgment, communication
System design Architecture, trade-offs, reliability
Project walkthrough Ownership, technical depth, impact
Behavioral interview Collaboration, learning, leadership
Hiring-manager conversation Scope, expectations, candidate questions

This reduces repetition and makes feedback easier to interpret.

Keep the interview loop proportional

The process should match the role's complexity and risk.

A junior engineer does not need seven interviews with executives.

A staff engineer may require broader evaluation than a mid-level developer.

A practical loop for many product-engineering roles might include:

  1. Initial alignment conversation
  2. One job-relevant technical assessment
  3. Consolidated technical and behavioral interviews
  4. Candidate questions
  5. Prompt decision

The total process should collect enough evidence without exhausting candidates or interviewers.

A sample process for a product engineer

Stage 1: Initial alignment

Approximately 30 minutes.

Evaluate:

  • relevant experience;
  • target role;
  • compensation alignment;
  • location and availability;
  • basic communication;
  • candidate questions.

Stage 2: Practical technical exercise

Approximately 60–90 minutes.

Evaluate:

  • problem decomposition;
  • coding;
  • testing;
  • debugging;
  • communication;
  • job-relevant judgment.

Stage 3: Project depth and collaboration

Approximately 60 minutes.

Evaluate:

  • prior ownership;
  • technical decisions;
  • collaboration;
  • lessons;
  • measurable impact.

Stage 4: Hiring-manager conversation

Approximately 45 minutes.

Discuss:

  • expectations;
  • team context;
  • role scope;
  • growth;
  • candidate questions.

Stage 5: Same-day debrief

Make a decision using submitted evidence.

This process may be completed within one or two weeks, depending on candidate availability.

A sample process for a senior engineer

A senior role may require evidence across a wider scope.

Possible stages include:

  1. Hiring-manager conversation
  2. Technical project walkthrough
  3. System-design interview
  4. Practical coding, debugging, or code-review exercise
  5. Collaboration and leadership interview
  6. Candidate conversation with future teammates
  7. Structured debrief

These interviews can be grouped into one coordinated block rather than spread across several weeks.

A sample process for a staff or principal engineer

Staff-level evaluation should focus less on isolated coding speed and more on:

  • organizational influence;
  • architecture across systems;
  • long-term technical strategy;
  • decision-making under ambiguity;
  • risk management;
  • cross-team alignment;
  • enabling other engineers;
  • business and technical trade-offs.

A suitable process may include:

  • deep project and impact discussion;
  • cross-system design;
  • technical strategy exercise;
  • influence and leadership interview;
  • partnership discussion with product or business leadership;
  • candidate questions with senior engineering leaders.

Do not simply add more rounds to the senior-engineer process.

Change the evidence to match the scope.

Build structured rubrics

A rubric defines what different levels of performance look like.

Without one, interviewers often score candidates according to personal expectations.

For example, a coding rubric might include:

Below expectations

  • does not clarify core requirements;
  • produces code that does not work;
  • cannot explain the approach;
  • ignores important edge cases;
  • cannot respond to feedback;
  • does not verify the result.

Meets expectations

  • clarifies the main requirements;
  • produces a workable solution;
  • explains the approach;
  • tests important behavior;
  • handles common errors;
  • responds constructively to suggestions;
  • identifies reasonable trade-offs.

Exceeds expectations

  • identifies hidden requirements;
  • chooses an appropriately simple design;
  • writes clear and maintainable code;
  • tests meaningful boundaries;
  • discusses production concerns;
  • communicates uncertainty precisely;
  • improves the solution after feedback;
  • distinguishes immediate needs from future enhancements.

The rubric should reflect the expected level.

A junior candidate and a senior candidate may complete the same exercise but be evaluated for different scope and judgment.

Define observable evidence

Avoid competencies that cannot be evaluated consistently.

Weak:

  • Smart
  • Confident
  • Good fit
  • Passionate
  • Senior presence
  • Strong personality

Stronger:

  • Clarifies ambiguous requirements.
  • Explains technical decisions.
  • Identifies production failure modes.
  • Responds constructively to feedback.
  • Distinguishes personal contribution from team work.
  • Connects technical choices to user or business outcomes.
  • Identifies when additional evidence is needed.
  • Changes direction when assumptions are disproven.

Observable behaviors are easier to compare and defend.

Ask consistent core questions

Candidates do not need identical conversations, but they should receive comparable opportunities to demonstrate the required competencies.

For example, every senior backend candidate might be asked to:

  • walk through a production service they owned;
  • explain a difficult incident;
  • design a service under the same core constraints;
  • review a similar code sample;
  • discuss a technical disagreement.

Interviewers may ask follow-ups based on each answer.

The shared core creates comparability without making the conversation robotic.

Train interviewers

A strong interview process depends on prepared interviewers.

Training should cover:

  • the role scorecard;
  • assigned competencies;
  • approved questions;
  • scoring rubrics;
  • evidence-based note-taking;
  • handling candidate questions;
  • providing hints consistently;
  • recognizing bias;
  • accessibility accommodations;
  • inappropriate or unlawful questions;
  • feedback deadlines;
  • debrief expectations.

Do not assume that being a strong engineer automatically makes someone a strong interviewer.

Interviewing is a skill.

Run mock interviews

Before using a new exercise, test it internally.

Ask several engineers to complete or observe it.

Review:

  • how long it takes;
  • whether instructions are clear;
  • whether the intended competencies appear;
  • whether multiple solutions are possible;
  • whether hidden assumptions exist;
  • whether the environment works;
  • whether the rubric matches the task.

A prompt that seems obvious to its author may be confusing to candidates.

Pilot the assessment before using it in hiring decisions.

Calibrate interviewer scoring

Have interviewers review sample responses and discuss how they would score them.

Calibration helps align expectations around:

  • correctness;
  • technical depth;
  • level;
  • communication;
  • use of hints;
  • partial completion;
  • production awareness;
  • collaboration.

Without calibration, one interviewer may treat a small syntax mistake as a major failure while another ignores missing tests or security concerns.

Collect evidence-based notes

Interview feedback should describe what the candidate did.

Weak notes include:

  • very smart;
  • not senior enough;
  • great culture fit;
  • seemed nervous;
  • lacked confidence;
  • good energy;
  • something felt off.

Stronger notes include:

  • clarified throughput and consistency requirements before choosing storage;
  • identified retry and duplicate-delivery risks;
  • wrote tests for empty and invalid input;
  • could not explain personal ownership of the migration;
  • revised the design after a new latency constraint;
  • discussed security but did not address authorization boundaries;
  • responded constructively when the interviewer challenged the approach.

Specific notes make debriefs faster and more reliable.

Submit feedback independently

Each interviewer should submit notes and a score before seeing other feedback.

This reduces the risk that:

  • the first speaker controls the discussion;
  • a senior employee's opinion changes everyone else's score;
  • interviewers rewrite their interpretation after the fact;
  • group consensus replaces independent evidence.

A useful process is:

  1. Conduct the interview.
  2. Record evidence.
  3. Score the assigned competencies.
  4. Submit feedback.
  5. Lock or preserve the original feedback.
  6. Hold the debrief.
  7. Resolve meaningful differences.

Debrief against the scorecard

The debrief should not begin with:

“So, did everyone like the candidate?”

A better structure is:

  1. Restate the role and expected level.
  2. Review each required competency.
  3. Present evidence.
  4. Identify contradictions or gaps.
  5. Challenge vague concerns.
  6. Determine whether the candidate meets the role.
  7. Assign the appropriate level.
  8. Make the decision.
  9. Document remaining risks.

The discussion should focus on the role, not on whether the candidate resembles existing team members.

Challenge vague objections

When someone says:

  • not a fit;
  • did not feel senior;
  • lacked confidence;
  • would not work well with the team;
  • did not seem passionate;

ask:

  • Which competency does this affect?
  • What did the candidate say or do?
  • What evidence supports the concern?
  • Was the same standard applied to other candidates?
  • Is this behavior relevant to performance?
  • Could the interview environment explain it?
  • Is additional evidence actually necessary?

A concern should not influence the decision merely because it was expressed confidently.

Avoid consensus as the only decision rule

Requiring every interviewer to agree can cause delay and give disproportionate power to vague objections.

The hiring manager or another designated owner should make the final decision after considering the structured evidence.

This does not mean ignoring interviewers.

It means the process has a clear accountable decision-maker.

Define what constitutes a serious concern

A blocking concern should relate directly to a required competency.

For example:

The role requires independent ownership of production services, but across several examples the candidate could not explain how they diagnosed or resolved production incidents.

That is more useful than:

I did not connect with them.

The hiring manager should distinguish evidence-based risk from personal preference.

Evaluate problem decomposition

Strong engineers can turn unclear problems into workable steps.

Observe whether the candidate:

  • restates the problem;
  • asks useful questions;
  • identifies missing information;
  • distinguishes requirements from assumptions;
  • chooses a reasonable starting point;
  • separates core work from optional improvements;
  • adapts when constraints change.

A candidate who begins coding immediately without understanding the problem may produce a correct-looking solution to the wrong requirement.

Evaluate technical judgment

Technical judgment includes deciding:

  • how much complexity is justified;
  • which trade-offs matter;
  • what should be built now;
  • what can wait;
  • where risk is acceptable;
  • which failure modes require protection;
  • when a managed service is appropriate;
  • when custom architecture is justified.

Ask candidates to explain why they chose an approach.

Do not reward a solution merely because it contains more technologies.

Evaluate testing behavior

Look for whether the candidate considers:

  • happy-path behavior;
  • invalid input;
  • empty input;
  • boundary conditions;
  • duplicate requests;
  • permissions;
  • retries;
  • timeouts;
  • concurrency;
  • partial failure;
  • large data;
  • state transitions.

The candidate may not have time to implement every test.

They should still be able to identify what requires verification.

Evaluate debugging discipline

Strong debugging is evidence-driven.

Observe whether the candidate:

  • reproduces the issue;
  • reads available evidence;
  • narrows the failure;
  • forms a hypothesis;
  • avoids random changes;
  • verifies assumptions;
  • revises the hypothesis;
  • confirms the fix;
  • considers regression prevention.

A candidate who guesses correctly once may be less reliable than one who follows a disciplined investigation.

Evaluate maintainability

Maintainability includes:

  • understandable naming;
  • clear organization;
  • appropriate abstraction;
  • coherent control flow;
  • useful tests;
  • sensible error handling;
  • documentation where necessary;
  • avoidance of premature complexity.

Do not treat personal style preferences as universal rules.

Ask whether another engineer could understand, test, change, and operate the solution.

Evaluate security awareness

Security expectations should match the role, but most developers should recognize common risks.

Depending on the exercise, look for awareness of:

  • authentication;
  • authorization;
  • input validation;
  • injection;
  • secret handling;
  • tenant isolation;
  • secure file handling;
  • audit logging;
  • encryption;
  • privacy;
  • rate limiting;
  • abuse prevention;
  • least privilege.

Do not expect every candidate to perform a full threat model during a short exercise.

Do expect them to notice risks relevant to the problem.

Evaluate production readiness

Professional engineering continues after code is written.

Look for whether the candidate considers:

  • logging;
  • metrics;
  • alerts;
  • tracing;
  • deployments;
  • rollback;
  • migrations;
  • compatibility;
  • capacity;
  • cost;
  • incident response;
  • feature flags;
  • failure recovery.

For production-oriented roles, a solution that works only in a local environment is incomplete.

Evaluate communication

Technical communication includes the ability to:

  • explain assumptions;
  • ask clear questions;
  • structure an answer;
  • communicate uncertainty;
  • describe trade-offs;
  • distinguish facts from guesses;
  • adjust detail for the audience;
  • summarize a decision;
  • listen and respond.

Do not confuse one communication style with competence.

A deliberate candidate who pauses before answering may be as effective as someone who speaks quickly.

Avoid accent and fluency bias

Candidates may communicate effectively while having:

  • an accent;
  • a different dialect;
  • non-native phrasing;
  • pauses;
  • assistive technology;
  • a less familiar conversational style.

Evaluate whether the communication is understandable, relevant, and useful.

Do not reward social familiarity.

Evaluate collaboration

Software development is collaborative.

Look for whether the candidate:

  • receives feedback constructively;
  • asks for clarification;
  • recognizes another person's stronger idea;
  • explains disagreement respectfully;
  • gives credit accurately;
  • identifies when to seek help;
  • supports shared ownership;
  • communicates blockers.

A technically strong developer who cannot collaborate may reduce the effectiveness of the entire team.

Evaluate learning ability

Exact tools change.

Evaluate whether the candidate can learn.

Ask about a time they:

  • entered an unfamiliar codebase;
  • learned a new language;
  • changed technical domains;
  • investigated an unknown system;
  • adopted a new tool;
  • corrected an incorrect assumption.

A strong answer explains:

  • why learning was required;
  • how they approached it;
  • how they validated understanding;
  • how they applied the knowledge;
  • what result followed.

Evaluate ownership

Ownership does not mean working alone.

It means taking responsibility for progress and outcomes.

Look for whether the candidate:

  • identifies risk;
  • communicates blockers;
  • proposes next steps;
  • follows work through deployment;
  • monitors results;
  • responds when problems occur;
  • documents decisions;
  • improves the process afterward.

Ask:

  • What happened after launch?
  • How did you know the change worked?
  • What did you do when it failed?
  • What did you personally own?
  • What would you change now?

Evaluate impact

Ask candidates to connect technical work to outcomes.

Possible evidence includes:

  • lower latency;
  • fewer incidents;
  • reduced cost;
  • improved conversion;
  • faster delivery;
  • better accessibility;
  • fewer support requests;
  • successful migrations;
  • stronger security;
  • improved developer productivity;
  • adoption by multiple teams.

Not every candidate will have access to precise business metrics.

Accept specific qualitative outcomes when appropriate.

For example:

  • adopted by five teams;
  • removed a recurring incident class;
  • enabled a launch;
  • reduced manual intervention;
  • shortened onboarding;
  • improved audit readiness.

Use behavioral interviews for real working evidence

Technical ability alone does not predict whether someone will succeed on a team.

Use structured behavioral questions to evaluate:

  • collaboration;
  • conflict;
  • ownership;
  • failure;
  • learning;
  • leadership;
  • ambiguity;
  • feedback;
  • prioritization.

Useful questions include:

  • Tell me about a technical disagreement.
  • Describe a production issue you owned.
  • Tell me about a decision that did not work.
  • Describe a project with unclear requirements.
  • Tell me about feedback that changed your behavior.
  • Describe a time you influenced without authority.
  • Tell me about a time you had to learn something quickly.
  • Describe a trade-off you made under time pressure.

Use follow-up questions to clarify:

  • the situation;
  • the candidate's responsibility;
  • their actions;
  • the result;
  • what they learned.

Avoid “culture fit” as a vague category

“Culture fit” often becomes a substitute for personal comfort.

Interviewers may unconsciously favor candidates who:

  • share their background;
  • speak similarly;
  • have familiar hobbies;
  • attended similar schools;
  • approach social interaction in the same way;
  • seem like someone they would befriend.

Replace vague fit with defined working behaviors.

Evaluate whether the candidate can:

  • communicate respectfully;
  • collaborate;
  • receive feedback;
  • act with integrity;
  • take responsibility;
  • support team standards;
  • contribute a useful perspective.

The goal is not to hire copies of the existing team.

Recognize common interview biases

Pedigree bias

Overvaluing famous employers or schools.

Similarity bias

Preferring candidates who resemble the interviewer.

Confidence bias

Mistaking fast, polished answers for deeper ability.

Halo effect

Allowing one strong accomplishment to improve unrelated evaluations.

Horn effect

Allowing one mistake to damage the entire evaluation.

Confirmation bias

Seeking evidence that supports the initial impression.

Recency bias

Overweighting the final answer or interview.

Technology bias

Rejecting a candidate for lacking one exact tool despite strong transferable knowledge.

Interview-performance bias

Confusing ability to interview with ability to perform the work.

Structure does not eliminate bias, but it makes bias easier to detect and challenge.

Do not overvalue speed

Speed may matter in some roles, but most engineering work rewards correctness, maintainability, and judgment.

A candidate who works more slowly may be:

  • validating assumptions;
  • considering edge cases;
  • managing interview anxiety;
  • using an unfamiliar environment;
  • prioritizing correctness.

Evaluate the full process rather than using completion time as the primary signal.

Avoid technical trivia

Questions about obscure syntax or rarely used framework details often measure memorization.

In real work, developers can consult:

  • documentation;
  • source code;
  • tests;
  • teammates;
  • search tools;
  • approved AI tools.

The more important skill is often knowing:

  • what to investigate;
  • which source to trust;
  • how to test the answer;
  • how to apply it safely.

Use memory-based questions only when immediate recall is genuinely necessary for the role.

Use algorithm interviews selectively

Data structures and algorithms are relevant to many engineering roles.

That does not mean every role requires puzzle-heavy evaluation.

Use algorithmic problems when the work genuinely involves:

  • performance-sensitive computation;
  • complex data processing;
  • search;
  • compilers;
  • graphics;
  • optimization;
  • specialized infrastructure.

For typical product-development roles, combine fundamental problem-solving with more representative tasks such as API design, debugging, testing, or code review.

Avoid adversarial interviewing

An interview should not be designed to intimidate the candidate.

Avoid:

  • trick questions;
  • deliberate ambiguity with no clarification;
  • hostile interruption;
  • mocking mistakes;
  • rapid-fire trivia;
  • withholding basic information;
  • changing rules without warning;
  • treating hints as failure.

A stressful environment may reveal who is most comfortable with stress interviews rather than who will perform best in the job.

Explain the process in advance

Candidates should know:

  • the number of stages;
  • the expected timeline;
  • the format;
  • the duration;
  • the technologies involved;
  • whether external resources are allowed;
  • whether AI tools are allowed;
  • whether the session is recorded;
  • how accommodations can be requested;
  • what each stage evaluates.

Transparency helps candidates prepare for the relevant work and reduces unnecessary anxiety.

Establish an AI-tool policy

As AI-assisted development becomes common, companies should decide how tools may be used during assessments.

Possible policies include:

  • no AI assistance;
  • AI permitted for documentation and syntax;
  • AI permitted with disclosure;
  • AI permitted throughout, with evaluation focused on verification and judgment;
  • role-specific rules.

Whatever policy you choose:

  • state it clearly;
  • apply it consistently;
  • explain why;
  • evaluate whether the policy reflects real work;
  • avoid creating hidden traps.

If developers use approved AI tools in the job, an interview may gain useful signal by evaluating whether candidates can:

  • formulate a request;
  • review generated code;
  • identify errors;
  • test the result;
  • protect sensitive information;
  • explain the final decision.

The candidate must still understand and defend the work.

Protect candidate privacy

Explain how assessment data is handled.

Candidates should know:

  • whether sessions are recorded;
  • who can view recordings;
  • where code is stored;
  • how long submissions are retained;
  • whether submissions are reused;
  • whether automated analysis is involved;
  • how deletion requests are handled.

Collect only the information necessary for hiring.

Avoid invasive monitoring that does not meaningfully improve evaluation.

Avoid surveillance-heavy assessments

Be cautious with tools requiring:

  • continuous webcam monitoring;
  • facial analysis;
  • emotion recognition;
  • biometric collection;
  • intrusive browser tracking;
  • unnecessary screen recording.

These tools may create privacy, accessibility, legal, and fairness concerns.

Measure the work using the least intrusive process that provides sufficient evidence.

Provide accommodations

Candidates may need accommodations related to:

  • disability;
  • neurodivergence;
  • hearing;
  • vision;
  • mobility;
  • anxiety;
  • chronic illness;
  • caregiving;
  • religious observance;
  • time zone;
  • assistive technology.

Possible accommodations include:

  • additional time;
  • breaks;
  • written instructions;
  • captioning;
  • accessible documents;
  • alternative coding environments;
  • different assessment formats;
  • flexible scheduling.

Make requests easy, visible, and private.

Do not require candidates to explain personal medical details to each interviewer.

Respect candidate time

Candidate time is part of the hiring cost.

A process requiring:

  • several screens;
  • a weekend take-home;
  • six interviews;
  • repeated background conversations;
  • long gaps;
  • an executive approval round;

may drive away the strongest candidates.

For every stage, ask:

  • What evidence does this add?
  • Is that evidence necessary?
  • Is another stage already collecting it?
  • Could it be collected more efficiently?
  • Is the burden proportional to the role?

A rigorous process can still be concise.

Reduce repeated questions

Candidates often answer “Tell me about yourself” and describe the same project several times.

This wastes time and signals poor coordination.

Share interview responsibilities through an interview packet.

Each interviewer should know:

  • what earlier stages covered;
  • which competency they own;
  • which questions to ask;
  • which evidence remains missing.

Reserve interview capacity

Hiring becomes slow when interviews must be fitted around ordinary meetings one stage at a time.

Reserve recurring calendar blocks for:

  • technical assessments;
  • manager conversations;
  • interview panels;
  • debriefs.

This improves scheduling and reduces the vacancy period.

Group interviews thoughtfully

Several focused interviews can be completed in one coordinated session with breaks.

For example:

  • technical exercise;
  • break;
  • project walkthrough;
  • lunch or longer break;
  • system design;
  • collaboration conversation;
  • candidate questions.

Avoid exhausting all-day marathons.

Compression should reduce scheduling delay, not increase candidate fatigue.

Require prompt feedback

Interview feedback should be submitted while details are fresh.

A reasonable standard may be:

  • notes completed within two hours;
  • score submitted before viewing others' feedback;
  • all feedback submitted by the end of the day;
  • debrief held within one business day.

Delayed feedback reduces accuracy and increases the chance of losing the candidate.

Make decisions quickly

A candidate should not wait a week while interviewers debate vague concerns.

After the final interview:

  1. Gather independent feedback.
  2. Hold the structured debrief.
  3. Identify whether evidence supports the role.
  4. Resolve level.
  5. Make the decision.
  6. Communicate the next step.

Speed should come from preparation, not pressure.

Do not use exploding offers

A fast process should not become a coercive process.

Avoid requiring candidates to accept immediately.

Provide:

  • written details;
  • time to review;
  • access to benefits information;
  • opportunities for questions;
  • a clear decision deadline;
  • reasonable flexibility.

Candidates should be able to make an informed decision.

Communicate throughout the process

Tell candidates:

  • what happens next;
  • when they should expect an update;
  • who to contact;
  • whether a delay has occurred;
  • whether additional evidence is needed.

Do not leave candidates guessing.

Even a brief update protects trust.

Close the loop respectfully

Rejected candidates should receive a clear response rather than silence.

Where appropriate and allowed, useful feedback may include:

  • the level at which evidence was insufficient;
  • the specific competency needing more support;
  • whether the role required different experience;
  • encouragement to apply for a more suitable role.

Avoid feedback that creates unnecessary legal or privacy risks, but do not use that concern as an excuse for ghosting.

A respectful rejection can preserve the relationship.

Measure the interview process

Track whether the process is working.

Useful metrics include:

  • time-to-hire;
  • time in each stage;
  • candidate withdrawal;
  • offer acceptance;
  • interview-to-offer ratio;
  • interviewer feedback delay;
  • candidate satisfaction;
  • assessment completion;
  • pass-through rate;
  • first-year retention;
  • manager satisfaction;
  • performance against role outcomes.

Do not optimize one metric in isolation.

A very short process is not successful if it produces poor hires.

A highly selective process is not successful if it rejects most qualified candidates for irrelevant reasons.

Track candidate withdrawals

Record known reasons for withdrawal, such as:

  • process too slow;
  • assignment too large;
  • accepted another offer;
  • compensation mismatch;
  • scheduling difficulty;
  • poor interview experience;
  • unclear role;
  • location requirements;
  • personal reasons.

Recurring patterns reveal where the process is losing candidates.

Review stage conversion

For each stage, examine:

  • number entering;
  • number advancing;
  • average score;
  • candidate withdrawals;
  • time spent;
  • interviewer variance.

A stage rejecting nearly everyone may indicate:

  • weak sourcing;
  • poor screening;
  • unclear instructions;
  • an unrealistic assessment;
  • a mismatch between the role and evaluation;
  • inconsistent scoring.

A stage passing nearly everyone may add little value.

Compare interview signals with job performance

Over time, review whether the interview predicted successful work.

Ask:

  • Which stages produced useful signals?
  • Which scores correlated with later performance?
  • Which concerns proved accurate?
  • Which concerns were irrelevant?
  • Which strong employees nearly failed the process?
  • Which questions produced little useful information?
  • Did the role match what was assessed?
  • Did onboarding affect the outcome?

Use this review carefully.

Post-hire performance is influenced by:

  • management;
  • role clarity;
  • onboarding;
  • team support;
  • organizational change;
  • project assignment;
  • compensation;
  • workplace conditions.

Do not assume every unsuccessful outcome proves a hiring mistake.

Conduct hiring retrospectives

After filling a role, review:

  • where candidates waited;
  • which stage provided the strongest evidence;
  • which stages repeated work;
  • whether the scorecard matched the actual role;
  • why candidates withdrew;
  • whether interviewers were calibrated;
  • whether compensation was competitive;
  • whether the offer moved quickly;
  • what should change for the next role.

Assign owners to improvements.

A process should evolve rather than becoming permanent by habit.

Validate assessments before trusting them

An interview exercise should not be considered predictive merely because it feels technical.

Review:

  • whether high performers on the job would perform well;
  • whether the task resembles real work;
  • whether irrelevant factors influence scores;
  • whether candidates understand the instructions;
  • whether interviewers score consistently;
  • whether results differ by interview format;
  • whether the exercise creates accessibility barriers.

The company should be willing to revise or remove an assessment that does not produce useful evidence.

Match the interview to seniority

Early-career engineers

Evaluate:

  • fundamentals;
  • learning;
  • debugging;
  • scoped implementation;
  • testing;
  • communication;
  • response to feedback.

Do not require broad architecture ownership.

Mid-level engineers

Evaluate:

  • independent delivery;
  • maintainability;
  • production awareness;
  • testing;
  • collaboration;
  • reliable ownership.

Senior engineers

Evaluate:

  • architecture;
  • ambiguity;
  • trade-offs;
  • reliability;
  • mentoring;
  • cross-team collaboration;
  • risk management;
  • business impact.

Staff and principal engineers

Evaluate:

  • organizational influence;
  • long-term technical strategy;
  • systems across teams;
  • decision frameworks;
  • risk reduction;
  • leadership without authority;
  • enabling others.

Engineering managers

Evaluate:

  • people leadership;
  • hiring;
  • coaching;
  • delivery;
  • conflict;
  • technical judgment;
  • team health;
  • stakeholder alignment.

Do not use one generic technical loop for every level.

A technical-interview rubric template

Problem clarification

  • Does not clarify essential requirements.
  • Clarifies the primary requirements.
  • Identifies hidden constraints and prioritizes them.

Problem decomposition

  • Approaches the work without structure.
  • Breaks the problem into workable parts.
  • Creates a clear plan and adapts it as evidence changes.

Technical correctness

  • Solution contains major unresolved errors.
  • Solution addresses the central requirement.
  • Solution is correct, robust, and appropriately scoped.

Testing

  • Does not verify the result.
  • Tests important behavior and common edges.
  • Identifies meaningful boundaries, failure modes, and regression protection.

Maintainability

  • Code or design is difficult to understand.
  • Structure and naming are generally clear.
  • Solution is simple, coherent, and easy to evolve.

Communication

  • Reasoning remains unclear.
  • Explains assumptions and decisions.
  • Communicates trade-offs, uncertainty, and alternatives precisely.

Collaboration

  • Resists feedback or ignores input.
  • Responds constructively.
  • Integrates feedback while preserving independent judgment.

Production awareness

  • Does not consider operation after implementation.
  • Addresses common operational concerns.
  • Discusses monitoring, rollout, failure recovery, security, and cost.

Questions for evaluating a project

  • What problem were you solving?
  • Who used the system?
  • What did you personally own?
  • What constraints shaped the work?
  • Which alternatives did you consider?
  • Why did you choose the final approach?
  • How did you test it?
  • How did you deploy it?
  • What happened when it failed?
  • How was it monitored?
  • What result did it create?
  • What would you change today?

Questions for evaluating technical judgment

  • Which assumptions are you making?
  • What is the simplest workable design?
  • Which requirement matters most?
  • Which trade-off is most significant?
  • What happens if a dependency fails?
  • Where could data become inconsistent?
  • How would retries affect the system?
  • What should be monitored?
  • How would you release this safely?
  • When would you redesign it?

Questions for evaluating collaboration

  • Tell me about a technical disagreement.
  • Describe a time you changed your mind.
  • How do you handle critical code-review feedback?
  • Tell me about a time another engineer improved your solution.
  • Describe a decision involving several teams.
  • Tell me about a communication failure.
  • How have you helped a teammate become more effective?

Questions for evaluating growth

  • Tell me about a technology you had to learn quickly.
  • Describe a mistake that changed how you work.
  • Tell me about an unfamiliar codebase you entered.
  • Which skill are you currently developing?
  • Describe a time your first approach failed.

Common technical-interview mistakes

Testing the interview rather than the job

Candidates become better at puzzles without becoming better at the role.

Undefined success criteria

Interviewers apply different personal standards.

Too many rounds

The process adds time without adding new evidence.

Excessive take-home work

Candidates with less free time are disadvantaged.

Inconsistent hints

Some candidates receive support while others do not.

Vague feedback

Terms such as “smart” or “not a fit” cannot support a fair decision.

Group discussion before scoring

Interviewers influence one another before recording independent judgment.

Overvaluing confidence

Fast speaking and polished presentation are not technical depth.

Requiring exact stack experience

Transferable engineering ability is ignored.

Ignoring accessibility

The process measures an unnecessary barrier instead of job performance.

Poor candidate communication

Strong candidates withdraw or reject the company.

Failing to validate the process

Interview traditions continue even when they do not predict successful work.

A practical 30-day redesign plan

Week 1: define the role

  • Create the role scorecard.
  • Identify first-year outcomes.
  • Separate essential and learnable skills.
  • Define the expected level.
  • Remove vague competencies.

Week 2: redesign the evidence

  • Map each competency to an interview stage.
  • Replace irrelevant puzzles with job-like exercises.
  • Create a structured project walkthrough.
  • Reduce repeated questions.
  • Set an appropriate total candidate burden.

Week 3: build the system

  • Write rubrics.
  • Create interviewer guides.
  • Pilot exercises internally.
  • Define accommodations.
  • Establish AI-tool rules.
  • Set feedback deadlines.
  • Assign the decision owner.

Week 4: train and pilot

  • Run mock interviews.
  • Calibrate scoring.
  • Test scheduling.
  • Use the process with a small number of candidates.
  • Collect candidate and interviewer feedback.
  • Revise unclear stages.

A final technical-interview checklist

Before opening the role, confirm that:

  • the role mission is clear;
  • expected outcomes are documented;
  • required skills are separated from learnable tools;
  • the interview matches the discipline and level;
  • each stage evaluates defined competencies;
  • the technical exercise resembles real work;
  • the assignment is proportionate;
  • rubrics are complete;
  • interviewers are trained;
  • accommodations are available;
  • the AI policy is clear;
  • privacy and recording practices are documented;
  • compensation is approved;
  • the decision owner is identified.

During the process, confirm that:

  • candidates know what to expect;
  • interviewers ask comparable questions;
  • hints are handled consistently;
  • evidence is recorded;
  • scores are submitted independently;
  • vague concerns are challenged;
  • feedback is submitted promptly;
  • candidates receive updates;
  • the candidate has time to ask meaningful questions;
  • unnecessary stages are not added.

After hiring, confirm that:

  • stage timing is reviewed;
  • candidate withdrawals are analyzed;
  • interviewer variance is checked;
  • candidate feedback is collected;
  • the process is compared with later outcomes;
  • weak stages are redesigned or removed;
  • improvements are documented.

The takeaway

A technical interview should not be a ritual inherited from another company.

It should be a deliberately designed evidence system.

The strongest interview processes:

  1. Define the role before interviewing.
  2. Identify the outcomes and competencies that matter.
  3. Test work resembling the actual job.
  4. Use the smallest assessment that produces sufficient evidence.
  5. Match questions to the role's discipline and level.
  6. Evaluate problem-solving, testing, debugging, and judgment.
  7. Assess communication, collaboration, learning, and ownership.
  8. Use structured rubrics.
  9. Collect independent, evidence-based feedback.
  10. Challenge vague impressions and hidden criteria.
  11. Respect candidate time, privacy, and accessibility.
  12. Communicate clearly and decide promptly.
  13. Measure whether the process predicts successful performance.
  14. Improve the interview loop after every hiring cycle.

The goal is not to make technical interviews easier.

The goal is to make them more relevant.

A difficult interview that measures the wrong ability is not rigorous. It is merely difficult.

A strong interview gives candidates a fair opportunity to demonstrate how they think, build, test, communicate, and improve. It gives interviewers comparable evidence. It gives the hiring manager a defensible decision. And it gives the company a better chance of hiring engineers who will perform well after the interview is over.
