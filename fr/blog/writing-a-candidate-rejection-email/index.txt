3:T128d,<p>Every hire creates dozens of rejections. How you handle those rejections is one of the most visible, most under-managed parts of your hiring brand — because rejected candidates talk, review, and sometimes re-apply or refer others. A thoughtful rejection costs a few minutes and buys goodwill; a ghosting costs you a public one-star review and a candidate who warns their network away. Here&#39;s how to do it well and at scale.</p>
<h2>The cardinal rule: don&#39;t ghost</h2>
<p>The single worst thing you can do is go silent after a candidate has invested time — especially after an interview. Ghosting is the number-one complaint candidates have about hiring, and it&#39;s entirely self-inflicted. Every applicant who reached a human stage deserves a clear &quot;no.&quot; Automated pipelines make this trivial; there is no excuse.</p>
<h2>Match the effort to the stage</h2>
<p>Calibrate the message to how far they got:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Résumé screen (no interview):</strong> a prompt, warm templated email is completely fine.</li>
<li><strong>Phone screen / one round:</strong> a short personal note referencing that you spoke.</li>
<li><strong>Final rounds:</strong> a genuinely personal email, ideally with a sentence of real feedback — and for finalists you liked, a call is a classy touch.</li>
</ul>
<p>The further someone advanced, the more human the &quot;no&quot; should feel.</p>
<h2>Send it promptly</h2>
<p>Once you&#39;ve decided, don&#39;t sit on it for two weeks &quot;in case.&quot; Candidates are managing other processes and living with uncertainty. A fast, kind no is more respectful than a slow one — and it frees them to pursue other options while you free yourself from an awkward backlog.</p>
<h2>What a good rejection contains</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>A clear decision.</strong> No false hope, no &quot;we&#39;ll keep you in mind&quot; if you won&#39;t.</li>
<li><strong>Thanks for their time</strong>, specifically.</li>
<li><strong>Warmth and respect</strong> — they&#39;re a person, not a rejected row.</li>
<li><strong>Optional: a door left open</strong> for future roles, if genuine.</li>
<li><strong>Optional: brief, kind feedback</strong> at later stages (more below).</li>
</ul>
<p>Keep it concise. Padding a no with paragraphs of praise reads as insincere.</p>
<h3>Template — early stage</h3>
<blockquote>
<p>Hi [Name], thank you for applying to the [role] at [Company]. After reviewing your background, we&#39;ve decided to move forward with other candidates whose experience more closely matches what we need right now. We really appreciate your interest and the time you took to apply, and we wish you the best in your search.</p>
</blockquote>
<h3>Template — after interviews</h3>
<blockquote>
<p>Hi [Name], thank you for taking the time to interview for the [role] — it was a genuine pleasure to meet you. This was a difficult decision, and after a lot of consideration we&#39;ve chosen to move forward with another candidate for this position. [Optional: one specific, kind note.] I&#39;d sincerely welcome you to apply again for future roles, and I hope our paths cross. Thank you again for your time and interest.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2>On giving feedback</h2>
<p>Feedback is a gift, but a legally and emotionally fraught one. Guidelines:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Only give it if asked, or at final stages</strong> where the person clearly earned it.</li>
<li>Keep it <strong>specific, factual, and about the role&#39;s needs</strong> — &quot;we were looking for deeper experience with X&quot; — not about them as a person.</li>
<li><strong>Avoid anything that sounds like a protected-class comment</strong>, ever.</li>
<li>If your company policy is no-feedback, say so kindly rather than dodging.</li>
</ul>
<p>A little honest feedback to a strong runner-up often turns a rejection into a future re-application.</p>
<h2>Reject at scale without going cold</h2>
<p>You can be humane and efficient:</p>
<ul>
<li>Use templates for early stages, but <strong>personalize the later ones</strong>.</li>
<li>Make sure <em>someone owns</em> sending rejections so nobody falls through the cracks.</li>
<li>Close the loop the moment a role is filled — don&#39;t leave applicants hanging indefinitely.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Why this is a hiring advantage</h2>
<p>Candidates remember how you made them feel more than whether they got the job. A respectful rejection produces referrals, re-applications when they&#39;ve grown, positive reviews on hiring sites, and a reputation that makes your <em>next</em> outreach land. Rejections aren&#39;t the end of the relationship — they&#39;re a huge, cheap opportunity to strengthen your employer brand with the majority of people you&#39;ll ever hire.</p>
