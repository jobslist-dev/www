Building a Diverse Engineering Team: Practical Steps That Work

A practical guide for companies that want to build stronger engineering teams by broadening their talent pipeline, reducing bias, structuring interviews, improving accessibility, and creating a workplace where people can succeed.

The JobsList.dev Team·2 avril 2026·30 blog.article.minuteRead
diversityhiringcultureengineering-teamsinclusive-hiring

Building a diverse engineering team is not a branding exercise, a one-time recruiting campaign, or a target that can be achieved by changing a few photographs on a careers page.

It is the result of building a hiring system that gives qualified people from different backgrounds a fair opportunity to discover the role, demonstrate their ability, receive a consistent evaluation, and succeed after joining the company.

Diversity can include differences in:

  • race and ethnicity;
  • gender and gender identity;
  • age;
  • disability;
  • socioeconomic background;
  • education;
  • geography;
  • nationality;
  • language;
  • career path;
  • family status;
  • military experience;
  • neurodivergence;
  • professional discipline;
  • industry experience;
  • problem-solving style.

A team can also appear diverse in one visible dimension while remaining highly uniform in education, economic background, working style, or professional experience.

The goal is not to hire people because they are different. The goal is to remove unnecessary barriers and evaluate candidates against the real requirements of the work.

When hiring systems rely heavily on personal networks, prestige signals, unstructured conversations, and subjective impressions, they may repeatedly favor people who resemble the existing team.

A more deliberate process improves fairness and often improves hiring quality at the same time.

Start with the business and product context

Before changing the hiring process, clarify why broader representation matters to the team.

Useful reasons may include:

  • serving a wide range of customers;
  • understanding different accessibility needs;
  • operating across multiple regions;
  • challenging assumptions;
  • reducing blind spots;
  • improving decision quality;
  • expanding the available talent pool;
  • building products for unfamiliar markets;
  • strengthening communication across different audiences;
  • creating a more resilient engineering organization.

Avoid reducing the purpose to a slogan.

A stronger internal statement might be:

We are building products used by people with different abilities, devices, languages, financial circumstances, and technical experience. Our engineering team should be capable of recognizing and discussing those differences throughout product development.

This connects hiring to the work rather than presenting diversity as a separate initiative.

Define what the role actually requires

Many hiring problems begin before the job description is written.

Teams often describe an idealized person rather than the work that must be performed.

Before opening the role, ask:

  • Which outcomes must this person deliver?
  • Which skills are essential on the first day?
  • Which skills can be learned after joining?
  • Which responsibilities belong to the role?
  • What level of autonomy is expected?
  • What support will the team provide?
  • Which requirements exist because of genuine constraints?
  • Which requirements were copied from an older description?
  • Is a degree actually necessary?
  • Is a specific number of years meaningful?
  • Is office attendance necessary for the work?
  • Are travel or on-call expectations accurately stated?

A role may require strong database fundamentals without requiring experience with the exact database your company uses.

A role may require production ownership without requiring ten years of experience.

A role may require written communication without requiring a native English speaker.

Distinguishing essential requirements from preferences makes the role more accurate and expands the number of qualified people who can reasonably apply.

Separate must-have skills from learnable skills

A useful role description distinguishes between:

Required now

Skills the person genuinely needs to perform the role safely and effectively.

Examples:

  • production experience with a relevant programming paradigm;
  • the ability to design and review APIs;
  • knowledge of secure authentication practices;
  • experience operating systems with an on-call responsibility;
  • the legal ability to work in a required location.

Learnable after joining

Skills a capable person could reasonably develop with support.

Examples:

  • your internal deployment platform;
  • a specific observability vendor;
  • your design-system conventions;
  • an unfamiliar cloud service;
  • your planning methodology;
  • your internal domain terminology.

Helpful but optional

Experience that may help but should not eliminate otherwise qualified applicants.

Examples:

  • experience in the same industry;
  • familiarity with a secondary language;
  • prior startup experience;
  • public speaking;
  • open-source contributions.

This structure communicates priorities more honestly than one long list of requirements.

Rewrite inflated job descriptions

Long requirement lists can discourage qualified candidates who do not see themselves reflected in every line.

A weak description may include:

  • ten years of experience for work that does not require it;
  • a degree unrelated to the actual responsibilities;
  • advanced knowledge of several technologies used only occasionally;
  • ownership expectations that belong to multiple roles;
  • contradictory requirements such as “entry level” and “five years required”;
  • personality traits presented as qualifications;
  • vague claims about working under pressure.

A stronger description focuses on outcomes.

Instead of:

Must have seven years of React experience, deep expertise in every modern frontend framework, exceptional communication skills, and the ability to thrive in a fast-paced environment.

write:

You will build and maintain accessible product interfaces, collaborate with design and backend engineering, review frontend architecture, and help improve the reliability of our shared component system. Strong JavaScript or TypeScript experience is required; familiarity with React is helpful, but experience with a comparable component framework can also be relevant.

The second version explains the work and leaves room for transferable experience.

Use clear, welcoming language

Avoid language that implies only one personality type will succeed.

Potentially exclusionary phrases include:

  • rock star;
  • ninja;
  • work hard, play hard;
  • thick skin required;
  • aggressive;
  • dominant;
  • culture fit;
  • native English speaker;
  • young and energetic;
  • digital native;
  • we are a family;
  • must live and breathe code;
  • no 9-to-5 mentality.

These phrases may be vague, culturally narrow, or unrelated to performance.

Use direct language instead:

  • communicates technical decisions clearly;
  • works effectively across disciplines;
  • responds constructively to feedback;
  • can prioritize under changing requirements;
  • supports production systems responsibly;
  • collaborates respectfully during disagreement.

Describe observable behavior rather than personality stereotypes.

State the salary range

Compensation transparency helps candidates decide whether the opportunity is viable before investing time in the process.

A useful job listing should explain:

  • base salary range;
  • currency;
  • location assumptions;
  • whether pay varies by geography;
  • bonus eligibility;
  • commission where applicable;
  • equity;
  • major benefits;
  • employment type;
  • expected working hours;
  • on-call compensation;
  • travel expectations.

Avoid ranges so broad that they provide little practical information.

If the range covers multiple levels, explain how level is determined.

For example:

The base salary range is $145,000–$180,000. Placement depends on the final level, relevant experience, scope of ownership, and location. The role also includes an annual bonus target and equity grant.

Transparent compensation reduces uncertainty and makes negotiation less dependent on access to private salary information.

Review the full candidate experience

The job description is only one part of the process.

Audit every stage:

  1. Job discovery
  2. Application
  3. Initial screening
  4. Technical evaluation
  5. Interviews
  6. Debrief
  7. Offer
  8. Negotiation
  9. Onboarding
  10. Promotion and retention

At each stage, ask:

  • Who may be unintentionally excluded?
  • Which information is missing?
  • Which requirements are unnecessary?
  • Where does subjectivity enter?
  • Where do candidates wait too long?
  • Which stages create financial or scheduling burdens?
  • Are accommodations easy to request?
  • Are candidates evaluated consistently?
  • Does the process reflect the actual job?

Improvement usually comes from correcting several small barriers rather than finding one dramatic solution.

Broaden how you source candidates

If most candidates come from employee referrals, the same schools, a small number of companies, or one professional network, the pipeline may repeatedly reproduce the existing team.

Broaden sourcing through:

  • public developer job marketplaces;
  • professional associations;
  • technical communities;
  • return-to-work programs;
  • apprenticeship programs;
  • universities beyond a small prestige group;
  • community colleges;
  • bootcamps;
  • open-source communities;
  • local developer groups;
  • accessibility-focused communities;
  • veteran transition programs;
  • professional groups serving underrepresented technologists;
  • remote and regional talent networks.

Do not treat specialized communities as places to post one job and disappear.

Long-term participation matters.

That may involve:

  • sponsoring events;
  • offering mentorship;
  • supporting educational programs;
  • sharing technical knowledge;
  • building relationships with community leaders;
  • returning candidate feedback where appropriate;
  • providing speakers or workshop facilitators.

Communities can usually distinguish between genuine participation and last-minute candidate extraction.

Use referrals without making them the whole pipeline

Referrals can produce strong candidates, but they should not become the only trusted source.

A balanced referral program can include:

  • clear job requirements;
  • structured screening;
  • the same evaluation process for referred and non-referred candidates;
  • reminders to consider people outside immediate social circles;
  • review of referral-source concentration;
  • incentives tied to successful hiring rather than informal favoritism.

A referral should create awareness of the role, not a lower evaluation standard.

Value nontraditional backgrounds

Strong engineers may come from:

  • self-directed learning;
  • bootcamps;
  • community colleges;
  • military service;
  • technical support;
  • quality assurance;
  • data analysis;
  • operations;
  • design;
  • teaching;
  • scientific research;
  • skilled trades;
  • career changes;
  • entrepreneurship.

Evaluate whether the candidate can perform the work.

Avoid assuming that one educational route is the only reliable signal.

A candidate who moved from technical support into engineering may bring exceptional debugging and customer empathy.

A former designer may understand frontend interaction and accessibility deeply.

A self-taught engineer may have substantial production experience despite lacking a conventional degree.

Different backgrounds should not lower the standard. They should broaden the forms of evidence accepted by the process.

Reduce dependence on prestige signals

School names, previous employers, and recognizable brands can influence reviewers before the candidate's work is examined.

Ask whether those signals predict success in your role.

A candidate from a famous company may have worked on a narrow component with extensive support.

A candidate from a small organization may have owned architecture, deployment, support, and incident response.

Focus on:

  • scope;
  • responsibility;
  • decisions;
  • results;
  • technical depth;
  • growth;
  • collaboration;
  • evidence of learning.

Prestige may provide context, but it should not replace evaluation.

Make applications accessible

The application itself can exclude candidates.

Review:

  • keyboard navigation;
  • screen-reader support;
  • color contrast;
  • form labels;
  • error messages;
  • mobile usability;
  • file-upload compatibility;
  • session timeout behavior;
  • document-format requirements;
  • unnecessary required fields.

Avoid requiring candidates to retype an entire résumé into a poorly designed form.

Only collect information necessary for the hiring process.

Do not request:

  • photographs unless genuinely required and lawful;
  • age or birth date;
  • unrelated personal information;
  • salary history where prohibited or unnecessary;
  • extensive demographic information without explaining its purpose and handling.

Voluntary demographic data should be separated from hiring decisions and handled carefully.

Explain the interview process in advance

Candidates perform better when they understand the format.

Provide:

  • the number of stages;
  • estimated duration;
  • names or roles of interviewers;
  • technical topics;
  • whether coding is live;
  • whether cameras are required;
  • tools used;
  • take-home expectations;
  • accommodation options;
  • decision timeline.

This does not give candidates an unfair advantage.

It reduces surprise and allows the interview to measure relevant ability rather than familiarity with hidden rituals.

Make accommodations easy to request

Candidates may need accommodations related to:

  • disability;
  • hearing;
  • vision;
  • mobility;
  • neurodivergence;
  • anxiety;
  • chronic illness;
  • caregiving;
  • religious observance;
  • time zone;
  • language processing;
  • assistive technology.

Possible accommodations include:

  • additional time;
  • breaks;
  • written instructions;
  • captioning;
  • accessible documents;
  • alternative coding tools;
  • flexible scheduling;
  • reduced-motion interfaces;
  • permission to use assistive software;
  • an alternative to a live whiteboard exercise.

Make the request process visible and private.

Do not require candidates to repeatedly explain their medical history to interviewers.

Use structured interviews

Unstructured interviews often create inconsistency.

One candidate may receive a collaborative conversation about relevant work. Another may be judged through trivia or an unrelated puzzle.

A structured interview uses:

  • defined competencies;
  • consistent questions;
  • documented evaluation criteria;
  • interviewer training;
  • independent scoring;
  • evidence-based debriefs.

Structure does not require a robotic conversation.

Interviewers can ask follow-up questions while still evaluating the same competencies.

Define competencies before interviewing

For each role, identify what the process needs to measure.

An engineering role may include:

  • problem decomposition;
  • coding;
  • testing;
  • system design;
  • debugging;
  • reliability;
  • security;
  • communication;
  • collaboration;
  • ownership.

A senior role may also include:

  • trade-off analysis;
  • mentorship;
  • cross-team influence;
  • risk management;
  • decision-making under ambiguity.

Each competency should have observable evidence.

Weak competency:

Strong culture fit.

Stronger competency:

Communicates disagreement respectfully, uses evidence, and can change direction when new information emerges.

Ask candidates comparable questions

Candidates do not need to receive identical follow-up conversations, but they should have a comparable opportunity to demonstrate the required skills.

For example, every senior backend candidate may be asked to:

  • explain a production incident;
  • design a service under defined constraints;
  • review an API decision;
  • discuss a disagreement;
  • describe a system they owned.

This creates a more reliable comparison than allowing every interviewer to improvise.

Use scoring rubrics

A rubric should explain what evidence corresponds to each rating.

Example for system-design judgment:

Below expectations

  • begins designing without clarifying requirements;
  • selects technologies without justification;
  • ignores reliability or security;
  • cannot discuss trade-offs.

Meets expectations

  • clarifies the main requirements;
  • proposes a workable design;
  • explains major trade-offs;
  • addresses basic reliability, data, and security concerns.

Exceeds expectations

  • identifies hidden constraints;
  • prioritizes based on scale and risk;
  • explains operational consequences;
  • proposes staged evolution rather than unnecessary complexity;
  • connects architecture decisions to product requirements.

Rubrics help interviewers distinguish evidence from personal preference.

Train interviewers to use the rubric

A rubric is ineffective when interviewers ignore it.

Training should cover:

  • how to ask consistent questions;
  • how to take evidence-based notes;
  • how to distinguish communication style from competence;
  • how to avoid evaluating personality similarity;
  • how to provide hints consistently;
  • how to handle accommodations;
  • how to avoid inappropriate questions;
  • how to score before the debrief;
  • how to recognize uncertainty.

Interviewers should understand that confidence, speed, accent, and familiarity with interview rituals are not the same as job performance.

Record evidence, not impressions

Weak notes include:

  • not senior enough;
  • did not feel confident;
  • not a culture fit;
  • seemed difficult;
  • great energy;
  • very polished.

These statements are vague and susceptible to bias.

Stronger notes include:

  • did not clarify data consistency requirements before selecting storage;
  • identified two failure modes and proposed retry handling;
  • explained personal ownership of the migration clearly;
  • changed approach after receiving new constraints;
  • did not test the implementation against empty input;
  • connected user research findings to the final design decision.

Evidence makes debriefs more useful and defensible.

Require independent feedback before discussion

Interviewers should submit their evaluations before the group debrief.

Otherwise, the first confident opinion may influence everyone else.

A better sequence is:

  1. Interviewer completes notes.
  2. Interviewer submits a score against the rubric.
  3. Scores are locked.
  4. The group reviews evidence.
  5. Disagreements are discussed.
  6. The hiring manager makes the decision using the defined criteria.

This protects independent judgment and reduces groupthink.

Prevent interviewer shadow criteria

Sometimes interviewers evaluate standards that were never defined.

Examples include:

  • whether the candidate attended a prestigious school;
  • whether the candidate uses the interviewer's preferred framework;
  • whether the candidate is socially similar to the team;
  • whether the candidate solved a problem in the interviewer's preferred way;
  • whether the candidate appeared enthusiastic enough;
  • whether the candidate shares personal hobbies with the team.

Debriefs should return to the agreed competencies.

Ask:

Which requirement does this concern relate to, and what evidence supports it?

If no one can answer, the concern may not belong in the decision.

Use work samples carefully

Work samples can evaluate relevant skill more directly than abstract credentials.

Useful exercises might include:

  • debugging a small service;
  • reviewing a pull request;
  • designing an API;
  • improving an interface;
  • analyzing a data model;
  • writing tests;
  • reviewing an architecture proposal;
  • explaining a trade-off.

The exercise should resemble the role without becoming unpaid production work.

It should be:

  • time-bounded;
  • clearly explained;
  • accessible;
  • evaluated with a rubric;
  • appropriate to the level;
  • safe from confidentiality concerns;
  • consistent across candidates.

Avoid excessive unpaid take-home work

Large take-home assignments can disadvantage candidates with:

  • caregiving responsibilities;
  • multiple jobs;
  • disabilities;
  • limited equipment;
  • less flexible schedules;
  • urgent financial needs.

Keep assignments small, or compensate candidates for substantial work.

Offer alternatives when possible:

  • a live collaborative exercise;
  • review of previous work;
  • a shortened take-home;
  • a discussion of an existing project.

Do not assume that the candidate with the most free time is the strongest employee.

Evaluate work before identity signals where practical

Blind evaluation is not possible at every stage, but some identity signals can be reduced.

For example:

  • remove names from an initial code review;
  • score take-home work before reviewing the résumé again;
  • use consistent project identifiers;
  • evaluate writing samples against a rubric;
  • separate voluntary demographic information from reviewers.

This will not eliminate bias, but it can reduce the influence of unrelated signals.

Do not overstate the value of anonymization

Anonymization has limits.

Résumés, writing style, project context, and interviews can still reveal background information.

Bias should also be addressed through:

  • structure;
  • training;
  • rubrics;
  • accountability;
  • measurement;
  • inclusive culture.

Blind review is one tool, not a complete strategy.

Review algorithmic screening carefully

Automated screening tools may appear objective while reproducing patterns from historical data or relying on weak proxies.

Before using a tool, ask:

  • What data was it trained on?
  • Which signals does it use?
  • Can candidates understand the evaluation?
  • Can decisions be reviewed?
  • Does it create accessibility barriers?
  • Does it analyze voice, face, or emotion?
  • Has it been independently assessed?
  • How are false negatives handled?
  • Can candidates request human review?
  • Does the tool comply with applicable law?

Avoid using opaque personality, facial-analysis, emotion-recognition, or surveillance-heavy tools as substitutes for job-relevant evaluation.

Do not use assessments as surveillance

A respectful technical assessment should measure ability without creating unnecessary intrusion.

Consider:

  • allowing reasonable tools;
  • explaining whether AI assistance is permitted;
  • avoiding always-on camera requirements unless genuinely necessary;
  • not collecting unrelated biometric data;
  • limiting browser monitoring;
  • protecting submitted code;
  • deleting assessment data according to a documented policy;
  • allowing accessibility accommodations.

The process should evaluate the work, not how comfortable the candidate is under surveillance.

Build balanced interview panels

A varied interview panel may help candidates encounter different perspectives and reduce dependence on one person's impression.

However, do not place all inclusion labor on underrepresented employees.

Repeatedly assigning the same employees to every panel can create:

  • additional workload;
  • emotional burden;
  • reduced focus time;
  • tokenization.

Distribute interviewing responsibility fairly and recognize it as real work.

Avoid tokenizing interviewers or candidates

Do not imply that one person represents an entire group.

Avoid comments such as:

  • You would bring a different perspective because of your background.
  • You would help us look more diverse.
  • You should speak with our only woman engineer.
  • We need someone like you on the team.

Focus on the candidate's professional experience and the role.

Representation matters, but candidates should not be treated as symbols.

Improve scheduling fairness

Interview scheduling can exclude candidates who cannot easily attend during narrow working hours.

Where possible:

  • offer several time windows;
  • account for time zones;
  • avoid demanding same-day availability;
  • permit reasonable breaks;
  • avoid unnecessary multi-hour blocks;
  • provide the complete schedule in advance;
  • allow rescheduling without penalty.

Candidates may be employed, caregiving, managing health needs, or interviewing across regions.

Flexibility can widen participation without lowering standards.

Communicate consistently

Silence and uncertainty damage the candidate experience.

Set expectations for:

  • response times;
  • scheduling;
  • next steps;
  • delays;
  • offer timelines;
  • rejection communication.

A candidate should not need a private connection to receive timely information.

Use templates where useful, but make important communication human and specific.

Review rejection patterns

Rejections can reveal process problems.

Track:

  • stage reached;
  • evaluation scores;
  • rejection reason;
  • interviewer;
  • role;
  • source;
  • time in process;
  • whether accommodations were requested;
  • whether the candidate withdrew.

Look for patterns such as:

  • one interviewer rejecting candidates at a much higher rate;
  • one stage causing disproportionate exits;
  • candidates from certain sources consistently failing a criterion unrelated to later performance;
  • repeated use of vague rejection reasons;
  • high withdrawal after take-home assignments;
  • low offer acceptance from particular groups.

Patterns require investigation, not automatic conclusions.

Measure the funnel responsibly

Useful funnel metrics include:

  • applicants by source;
  • qualified applicants;
  • screening pass rate;
  • assessment completion;
  • interview pass rate;
  • offer rate;
  • acceptance rate;
  • time to decision;
  • candidate withdrawals;
  • reasons for withdrawal;
  • new-hire retention.

Where lawful and appropriate, aggregate demographic data may help identify disparities.

Protect privacy:

  • collect data voluntarily;
  • explain why it is collected;
  • restrict access;
  • report only meaningful aggregates;
  • avoid exposing individuals;
  • establish retention rules;
  • follow applicable law.

Small sample sizes can produce misleading percentages. Use caution before interpreting trends.

Do not chase vanity metrics

A large number of applications does not mean the process is working.

A company may attract a broad applicant pool while repeatedly eliminating candidates through later stages.

Measure:

  • who reaches each stage;
  • where candidates leave;
  • which criteria drive decisions;
  • whether assessments predict performance;
  • whether new hires stay and advance.

The objective is not to create an impressive diversity chart. It is to build a fair, effective system.

Audit compensation decisions

Fair hiring can be undermined by inconsistent offers.

Review:

  • starting salaries;
  • title and level;
  • equity;
  • signing bonuses;
  • negotiation adjustments;
  • geographic pay rules;
  • promotion timing;
  • raise decisions.

Use documented compensation bands.

Avoid basing offers primarily on:

  • prior salary;
  • negotiation aggressiveness;
  • perceived desperation;
  • assumptions about cost of living;
  • personal familiarity.

The same role and level should have a defensible compensation framework.

Standardize leveling

Leveling should be based on the work the candidate is expected to perform.

Define expectations for:

  • scope;
  • autonomy;
  • technical depth;
  • product judgment;
  • collaboration;
  • mentorship;
  • organizational influence;
  • operational responsibility.

Do not use years of experience as the only proxy for level.

Two candidates with the same tenure may have operated at very different scopes.

Review negotiation outcomes

Negotiation can introduce disparities when some candidates have more information, confidence, or alternatives.

Reduce this risk through:

  • published salary ranges;
  • clear compensation bands;
  • consistent initial offers;
  • standardized approval rules;
  • written explanations of package components;
  • proactive correction of internal inequities.

Do not assume that a candidate who negotiates less values themselves less or should be paid less.

Build an inclusive onboarding process

Hiring is only the beginning.

A new employee needs:

  • clear expectations;
  • access to documentation;
  • working equipment;
  • introductions;
  • a defined manager;
  • a first-month plan;
  • context about decisions;
  • support for asking questions;
  • feedback checkpoints;
  • an explanation of team norms.

Informal onboarding favors people who already understand the company's culture or know someone internally.

Documented onboarding distributes information more fairly.

Assign onboarding support thoughtfully

A buddy or mentor can help a new employee learn:

  • team practices;
  • communication norms;
  • technical systems;
  • organizational context;
  • where to ask for help.

Avoid assigning mentors only based on shared identity.

Ask what support the new hire needs and distribute mentorship work fairly.

Make unwritten rules visible

Every workplace has unwritten rules.

Examples include:

  • who is expected to speak in meetings;
  • how decisions are made;
  • when disagreement is acceptable;
  • how promotion works;
  • who receives high-visibility projects;
  • whether after-hours social events affect relationships;
  • how remote employees gain context.

Unwritten rules usually benefit people with existing access.

Document:

  • decision processes;
  • meeting norms;
  • promotion expectations;
  • ownership boundaries;
  • communication channels;
  • escalation paths;
  • feedback practices.

Transparency supports inclusion.

Create psychological safety through behavior

Psychological safety does not mean avoiding disagreement or lowering standards.

It means people can:

  • ask questions;
  • raise risks;
  • admit mistakes;
  • disagree respectfully;
  • request help;
  • report harmful behavior;
  • propose unfamiliar ideas;
  • share incomplete work.

Leaders create safety through their response.

If someone raises a problem and is dismissed, punished, mocked, or ignored, written values will not repair the damage.

Establish respectful meeting practices

Meeting norms can improve participation.

Examples include:

  • sharing agendas in advance;
  • allowing written input;
  • avoiding interruptions;
  • rotating facilitation;
  • documenting decisions;
  • inviting dissent;
  • clarifying owners;
  • not relying exclusively on rapid verbal debate;
  • providing remote participation;
  • avoiding decisions during informal gatherings.

Not everyone contributes best in the same way.

A well-run meeting creates multiple ways to participate.

Review who receives high-impact work

Representation at entry level does not guarantee equitable growth.

Track who receives:

  • greenfield projects;
  • visible launches;
  • architecture ownership;
  • customer exposure;
  • leadership opportunities;
  • conference attendance;
  • mentorship;
  • promotion support;
  • incident leadership;
  • strategic work.

If some employees repeatedly receive maintenance work while others receive career-building assignments, the team may reproduce inequity after hiring.

Make promotion criteria clear

Promotion should not depend on private sponsorship or vague impressions.

Document expectations for:

  • scope;
  • impact;
  • collaboration;
  • technical or design leadership;
  • reliability;
  • mentoring;
  • organizational influence.

Provide examples of evidence.

Managers should discuss growth throughout the year, not introduce hidden standards during promotion review.

Calibrate performance reviews

Performance reviews can contain vague and inconsistent language.

Examples of weak feedback include:

  • lacks executive presence;
  • needs more confidence;
  • not strategic enough;
  • too aggressive;
  • not visible;
  • difficult personality.

Ask reviewers to provide:

  • specific behavior;
  • context;
  • impact;
  • expected alternative;
  • evidence over time.

Calibration should compare employees against defined expectations, not against a stereotyped image of leadership.

Support flexible working arrangements

Flexibility can widen access for:

  • caregivers;
  • disabled employees;
  • people managing health conditions;
  • employees outside major technology hubs;
  • people with transportation constraints;
  • those needing focused working environments.

Clarify:

  • core collaboration hours;
  • remote eligibility;
  • office expectations;
  • travel;
  • equipment support;
  • location restrictions;
  • time-zone requirements.

Do not advertise remote work and later rely on unspoken office attendance for visibility or advancement.

Make accessibility part of engineering quality

Accessibility should not be treated only as a hiring accommodation.

It can be integrated into:

  • product requirements;
  • design reviews;
  • component libraries;
  • testing;
  • documentation;
  • procurement;
  • quality assurance;
  • employee tools.

An engineering culture that values accessibility is more likely to support both disabled customers and disabled employees.

Provide safe reporting mechanisms

Employees need trustworthy ways to raise concerns.

A reporting process should explain:

  • where to report;
  • confidentiality limits;
  • investigation steps;
  • protection against retaliation;
  • expected timeline;
  • escalation options;
  • how outcomes are communicated.

A policy is not enough if employees believe reporting will damage their careers.

Leadership behavior determines whether the system is credible.

Respond to harmful behavior consistently

Inclusion cannot survive when high performers are excused for disrespectful conduct.

Address:

  • harassment;
  • discriminatory comments;
  • repeated interruption;
  • exclusion;
  • retaliation;
  • bullying;
  • public humiliation;
  • hostile communication;
  • inappropriate jokes.

Technical skill does not compensate for behavior that harms the team.

Standards should apply consistently across seniority and performance levels.

Conduct retention interviews

Do not wait for exit interviews.

Ask employees periodically:

  • What helps you do your best work?
  • What makes the job harder?
  • Do you receive useful feedback?
  • Are expectations clear?
  • Do you have opportunities to grow?
  • Do you feel safe raising concerns?
  • Which processes feel unfair?
  • What might cause you to leave?

Look for patterns and act on them.

Collecting feedback without visible change can reduce trust.

Learn from exit data without blaming employees

When employees leave, examine:

  • manager relationship;
  • compensation;
  • growth;
  • workload;
  • flexibility;
  • team climate;
  • promotion;
  • project access;
  • inclusion;
  • organizational change.

Avoid treating departures as individual failures without examining the environment.

Hold leaders accountable

Diversity and inclusion cannot remain the responsibility of recruiting or employee resource groups alone.

Engineering and company leaders should be accountable for:

  • hiring-process quality;
  • compensation consistency;
  • retention;
  • promotion access;
  • team behavior;
  • management quality;
  • response to concerns;
  • measurable improvement.

Accountability should focus on controllable systems and behavior, not pressure to make individual hiring decisions based on identity.

Avoid performative approaches

Warning signs include:

  • publishing goals without changing the process;
  • using diverse imagery while the workforce remains highly uniform;
  • launching a campaign only during a heritage month;
  • asking underrepresented employees to solve every inclusion problem;
  • prioritizing public statements over internal safety;
  • hiring broadly without improving retention;
  • using demographic targets without addressing evaluation quality;
  • treating one hire as proof that the problem is solved.

Employees and candidates notice the difference between communication and practice.

Do not lower standards

Inclusive hiring does not require lowering standards.

It requires defining standards more accurately and evaluating them more consistently.

A weak process may use:

  • prestige;
  • personal similarity;
  • fast puzzle solving;
  • charisma;
  • insider referrals;
  • vague confidence;
  • cultural familiarity.

A stronger process evaluates:

  • relevant skills;
  • evidence;
  • judgment;
  • learning;
  • collaboration;
  • outcomes;
  • ability to perform the role.

Removing irrelevant barriers can raise the quality of the process.

Avoid “culture fit” as a decision criterion

“Culture fit” often means:

  • similar personality;
  • similar hobbies;
  • familiar communication style;
  • shared background;
  • social comfort.

Replace it with explicit values and behaviors.

Instead of:

Would I enjoy having coffee with this person?

ask:

  • Can this person collaborate respectfully?
  • Can they receive feedback?
  • Can they disagree constructively?
  • Do they demonstrate integrity?
  • Can they support the team's operating principles?
  • Do they add a useful perspective or capability?

Some companies use the term “culture contribution” to emphasize what a candidate may add, but it still needs structured criteria.

Respect legal and ethical boundaries

Employment, demographic data, accessibility, compensation, automated decision tools, and reporting obligations may be governed by laws that vary by location.

Companies should obtain appropriate legal and human-resources guidance for:

  • protected characteristics;
  • data collection;
  • applicant tracking;
  • accommodations;
  • salary disclosure;
  • automated screening;
  • record retention;
  • adverse-impact analysis;
  • international hiring.

Legal compliance is a minimum. Ethical hiring often requires additional care.

Use experiments responsibly

When changing the process, define what you expect to improve.

For example:

Change

Remove degree requirements from roles where the degree is not essential.

Expected effect

Increase qualified applications from candidates with alternative education paths.

Measure

  • applicant volume;
  • screening pass rate;
  • interview performance;
  • quality of hire;
  • retention.

Review

Compare results over a meaningful period without exposing individual candidates.

This is more useful than changing several parts of the funnel at once and guessing what worked.

Create a hiring-process scorecard

A practical internal scorecard might track:

Area Question
Role design Are requirements tied to real work?
Job description Is the language clear and inclusive?
Compensation Is the range visible and consistent?
Sourcing Are multiple channels used?
Accessibility Can candidates request accommodations easily?
Interviewing Are questions and rubrics structured?
Assessment Does it resemble the work and respect candidates' time?
Debrief Is independent evidence collected before discussion?
Offer Are level and pay decisions consistent?
Onboarding Are expectations and unwritten rules documented?
Growth Are promotion criteria transparent?
Retention Are employees receiving equitable access to opportunity?

Review the scorecard after each hiring cycle.

A practical 90-day improvement plan

Days 1–30: audit

  • Review job descriptions.
  • Map the full candidate journey.
  • Inspect sourcing concentration.
  • Document interview stages.
  • Review rubrics and interviewer notes.
  • Check accessibility.
  • Analyze compensation and leveling practices.
  • Review candidate feedback.

Days 31–60: redesign

  • Rewrite role templates.
  • Separate required and preferred skills.
  • publish salary ranges;
  • define competencies;
  • create rubrics;
  • standardize interview questions;
  • introduce independent scoring;
  • reduce unnecessary take-home work;
  • document accommodations.

Days 61–90: measure and improve

  • Track stage conversion.
  • Review candidate withdrawals.
  • examine interviewer variance;
  • collect candidate feedback;
  • audit offer consistency;
  • review onboarding;
  • assign owners for unresolved issues;
  • publish an internal improvement report.

Do not wait for a perfect system before making obvious improvements.

A practical job-description checklist

Before publishing a role, confirm that:

  • the title matches the responsibilities;
  • the work is described clearly;
  • required skills are genuinely required;
  • preferred skills are labeled separately;
  • degree requirements are justified;
  • years-of-experience requirements are reasonable;
  • compensation is transparent;
  • location expectations are clear;
  • on-call and travel requirements are stated;
  • accessibility accommodations are offered;
  • the language avoids unnecessary stereotypes;
  • the interview process is summarized;
  • the application works on mobile and with assistive technology.

A practical interview checklist

Before interviewing, confirm that:

  • competencies are defined;
  • interviewers understand their assigned area;
  • candidates receive the format in advance;
  • accommodations are supported;
  • questions are comparable;
  • work samples are job-related;
  • evaluation rubrics exist;
  • interviewers take evidence-based notes;
  • feedback is submitted independently;
  • debriefs focus on requirements;
  • vague “fit” concerns are challenged;
  • decisions are documented.

Questions leaders should ask regularly

  • Which people are not entering our pipeline?
  • Where do candidates withdraw?
  • Which requirements eliminate the most applicants?
  • Are those requirements related to performance?
  • Are interviewers applying the rubric consistently?
  • Are certain communication styles being mistaken for competence?
  • Who receives the highest-visibility work after hiring?
  • Are compensation and promotion outcomes consistent?
  • Do employees feel safe raising concerns?
  • Are we acting on feedback?
  • Are people from different backgrounds staying and advancing?

These questions should become part of operating the engineering organization, not an annual exercise.

The takeaway

Building a diverse engineering team is not mainly about finding a new recruiting slogan.

It is about designing a hiring and employment system that:

  1. Defines roles accurately.
  2. Removes unnecessary requirements.
  3. Communicates compensation clearly.
  4. Reaches candidates through broader channels.
  5. Values different forms of experience.
  6. Provides an accessible candidate experience.
  7. Uses structured, job-relevant evaluations.
  8. Requires evidence instead of intuition.
  9. Makes offers and leveling consistent.
  10. Creates fair access to onboarding, growth, and promotion.
  11. Responds consistently to harmful behavior.
  12. Measures outcomes and improves continuously.

Diversity may begin with recruiting, but it succeeds through inclusion, fairness, trust, and opportunity.

The objective is not to assemble a team that looks different while operating exactly as before.

The objective is to build an engineering organization where different people can contribute fully, challenge assumptions, grow professionally, and create stronger products together.
