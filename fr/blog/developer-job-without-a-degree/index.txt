3:T1d25,<p>Software is one of the few well-paid fields where the credential is genuinely optional. Plenty of working engineers — including senior ones at companies you&#39;ve heard of — never finished a computer science degree, or never started one.</p>
<p>That doesn&#39;t mean it makes no difference. It means the difference is concentrated in specific places, and once you know where those are, you can route around them instead of worrying in general.</p>
<h2>Where the degree actually matters</h2>
<p>Being precise about this is more useful than reassurance.</p>
<p><strong>It matters most for:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Visa sponsorship.</strong> Immigration rules in many countries lean heavily on formal qualifications. This is the hardest barrier to work around and the one worth planning for early.</li>
<li><strong>Large graduate schemes</strong>, which are frequently structured around universities and cohorts.</li>
<li><strong>Some specialised fields</strong> — research roles, machine learning, compilers, cryptography — where the theory is the job rather than background knowledge.</li>
<li><strong>A minority of large, traditional employers</strong> with rigid HR filters.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>It matters much less for:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Startups and small companies, where almost nobody asks.</li>
<li>Most product engineering, web, mobile, and infrastructure work.</li>
<li>Any company that assesses through practical work.</li>
<li>Every role you reach through a referral, which bypasses the filter entirely.</li>
</ul>
<p>The second list is far larger than the first. After your first job, the question mostly disappears — your work history becomes the credential.</p>
<h2>Build the thing that replaces it</h2>
<p>A degree is a proxy for &quot;this person can probably build software.&quot; Any better evidence beats a proxy. The strongest substitute, in order:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Something real, shipped, and maintained.</strong> A deployed application with users — even a handful — outperforms every alternative. Users mean you dealt with reality: bugs, feedback, edge cases, someone depending on it.</li>
<li><strong>Paid work of any kind.</strong> Freelance, contract, a small business&#39;s website, a tool for a local organisation. Paid means someone judged it worth money.</li>
<li><strong>Meaningful open-source contributions.</strong> Not typo fixes — a real fix or feature in a project you didn&#39;t start, which proves you can work in someone else&#39;s codebase to their standards.</li>
<li><strong>Depth in one area.</strong> Being genuinely strong in one stack beats shallow familiarity with six.</li>
</ol>
<p>One substantial project you can discuss for thirty minutes is worth more than eight tutorial repositories.</p>
<h2>Fill the actual gaps, not the imagined ones</h2>
<p>Self-taught developers often have sharper practical skills than graduates and specific theoretical holes. Interviews probe those holes, so close the ones that come up:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Data structures and algorithms</strong>, at a working level. You don&#39;t need competition fluency, but you should know what a hash map costs, when a tree is appropriate, and how to reason about complexity.</li>
<li><strong>How computers work underneath</strong> — memory, processes, threads, what happens when a program runs.</li>
<li><strong>Networking basics</strong> — HTTP, DNS, TCP, what actually happens when a request is made.</li>
<li><strong>Databases beyond queries</strong> — indexes, transactions, why something is slow.</li>
<li><strong>Concurrency</strong>, at least conceptually.</li>
</ul>
<p>These are learnable from books and practice in a few months of steady effort. They&#39;re also the areas where interviewers unconsciously test whether the missing degree left a hole, so closing them removes the doubt entirely.</p>
<h2>Target employers who assess rather than filter</h2>
<p>This is the highest-leverage decision in the whole search.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Read the requirements.</strong> &quot;Degree or equivalent experience&quot; means it&#39;s genuinely negotiable. &quot;BSc in Computer Science required&quot; from a large company with an automated system may be a hard filter — spend your effort elsewhere.</li>
<li><strong>Favour companies with practical assessment.</strong> A take-home or pairing round is good news: it evaluates what you can do rather than where you studied.</li>
<li><strong>Favour smaller companies for the first role.</strong> They care about output and they can make exceptions without a committee.</li>
<li><strong>Apply anyway when a degree is &quot;preferred.&quot;</strong> Preferred is a wish, and self-rejecting costs you far more interviews than it saves.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Referrals are the cleanest route around the filter</h2>
<p>A referral converts your application from a stranger&#39;s CV into a colleague&#39;s recommendation, and it skips whatever automated screen might have removed you.</p>
<p>Building this doesn&#39;t require networking events. It requires being visible somewhere technical over time: contributing to a project, being helpful in a community around your stack, writing about what you&#39;re learning, or attending a local meetup. People hire people they&#39;ve seen do things.</p>
<h2>Handling the question in interviews</h2>
<p>It won&#39;t come up as often as you fear. When it does, answer briefly and without defensiveness, then redirect to evidence.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>&quot;I don&#39;t have a CS degree — I came in through [route]. I&#39;ve spent the time since building [thing], and I filled in the fundamentals deliberately: I worked through [what you studied] because I kept hitting the gaps. Happy to go as deep as you&#39;d like on any of it.&quot;</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Two things make this work: it&#39;s matter-of-fact, and it demonstrates you identified and closed your own gaps, which is exactly the self-direction the job requires.</p>
<p>What not to do: apologise, over-explain, pre-emptively raise it in every conversation, or disparage people who did study. And never imply the theory doesn&#39;t matter — you&#39;ll sound like someone who hasn&#39;t yet discovered why it does.</p>
<h2>On your CV and profile</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Lead with skills and projects</strong>, not education. Put the education section at the bottom, or omit it if it&#39;s empty.</li>
<li><strong>List relevant courses or certifications</strong> if you have them, without inflating them into something they aren&#39;t.</li>
<li><strong>Link to working things</strong> — live sites, repositories, published work. This is your credential.</li>
<li><strong>Never fabricate a qualification.</strong> It&#39;s routinely checked, it&#39;s a firing offence at most companies, and it converts a non-issue into a career problem.</li>
</ul>
<h2>The takeaway</h2>
<p>The degree matters for visas, graduate schemes, and a few theory-heavy specialisms — and much less everywhere else. Build something real and shipped, close the fundamentals gaps deliberately, target employers who assess practically, and work toward referrals. Answer the question in two calm sentences when it comes up. The first job is the hard one; after that, what you&#39;ve built is what you&#39;re judged on.</p>
